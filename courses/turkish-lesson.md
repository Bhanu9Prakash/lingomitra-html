# Turkish: A pattern-first foundation

This course teaches contemporary standard Turkish as used in Turkey, in its modern Latin alphabet. It preserves ç, ğ, ı, İ, ö, ş and ü. Dotted i and dotless ı are different vowels: their capital forms are İ and I respectively. Turkish is not written accurately by removing these distinctions. The examples use neutral everyday language, with respectful or plural siz when addressing unfamiliar adults.

Suffixes express much of the information that English places in separate words. We write A for a two-way suffix vowel, a or e, and I for a four-way suffix vowel, ı, i, u or ü, only when explaining a pattern. These capital symbols are grammar notation, not literal letters to type into sentences. Actual examples always spell the selected suffix in full.

### References and review notes

[Turkish Textbook's vowel harmony guide](https://www.turkishtextbook.com/vowel-harmony/) was consulted to check the front/back and rounding distinction used in the suffix explanations. Examples and teaching prose are original. This introduction covers common productive patterns and explicitly marks selected irregular forms. It does not claim that every loanword, stress pattern or conversational register follows a single rule.

## Lesson 1: Introducing yourself with noun endings

**Outcome:** introduce yourself and address another person respectfully using a noun sentence.

### Think first

Öğretmen means “teacher,” while öğretmenim means “I am a teacher.” Where has Turkish put the information expressed by English “am”?

### A person ending on a noun

Turkish can attach a person ending directly to a noun or description. Ben öğretmenim means “I am a teacher.” Ben is the pronoun “I,” but the ending already identifies the speaker, so öğretmenim can stand alone in a clear context. We retain pronouns in early examples to make the pattern visible.

After a vowel, a connecting y appears in the first-person singular ending: öğrenci, student, becomes öğrenciyim. This avoids placing two vowels directly together. Third-person singular identity often has no overt ending in neutral conversation: O öğretmen means “he or she is a teacher.” The pronoun o does not distinguish gender.

Sen is informal singular you. Siz addresses several people or one person respectfully. Öğretmensiniz means “you are a teacher” with siz. A stranger, teacher or older adult is usually a sensible context for beginning with siz until the relationship supports another choice.

Turkish has no grammatical masculine/feminine noun agreement. The same öğrenci describes a student of any gender. Nor is there a compulsory indefinite article equivalent to English “a” in these simple identity statements. Bir can mean one or an indefinite “a” in other contexts, but you do not need to insert it into every introduction.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| ben | I |
| sen | you, informal singular |
| siz | you, respectful or plural |
| o | he, she or it |
| öğretmen | teacher |
| öğrenci | student |

### Worked examples

- `Ben öğretmenim.`: I am a teacher; the noun carries the speaker ending.
- `Ben öğrenciyim.`: I am a student; y connects a vowel-final noun to the ending.
- `O öğretmen.`: He or she is a teacher; no gender distinction is made.

### Common trap

Do not look for a separate present word for “am” in every noun sentence. Also, o does not specify whether the person is a man or a woman; context supplies that information when relevant.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: I am a student.
2. Say respectfully: You are a teacher.
3. Say: She is a student.
4. Say: I am a teacher.

***Answers:***

1. `Ben öğrenciyim.`
2. `Siz öğretmensiniz.`
3. `O öğrenci.`
4. `Ben öğretmenim.`

### Why these answers work

Öğrenciyim uses a connecting y after the final vowel. Öğretmensiniz carries the respectful or plural person ending. O remains the same regardless of the person's gender. Öğretmenim needs no independent linking verb.

### Retrieve and recombine

Introduce two imaginary people with different occupations from the table. Then speak as one of them and explain which suffix turns the noun into a statement about yourself.


## Lesson 2: Letters, vowels and readable words

**Outcome:** preserve the Turkish alphabet while reading useful words aloud and identifying objects.

### Think first

Compare the letters i and ı. If a keyboard removes the dot, has it merely changed typography, or has it selected another vowel?

### Read the actual alphabet

Turkish i is a front vowel roughly like the vowel in English “machine,” usually shorter. Dotless ı is a high unrounded back vowel with no exact English equivalent. Try forming u while relaxing the lip rounding as an initial approximation, then refine it with audio. Ö and ü are front rounded vowels; they are not decorative variants of o and u.

Ç sounds roughly like ch in “chair,” ş like sh in “ship,” and c like j in “jam.” J occurs mainly in loanwords and has a sound like the middle consonant of “measure.” The letter ğ usually affects neighbouring vowels rather than behaving like a strong English g. Its realisation depends on the sound environment, so do not assign one hard consonant sound to every occurrence.

Bu means “this.” Bu kitap means “this book” as a phrase, or “this is a book” as a short identification in context. Şu points out something to direct attention to it, while o can mean that as well as he, she or it. A question Bu ne? means “What is this?”

The examples use the same simple third-person identity pattern as the first lesson. That lets you practise accurate letters inside meaningful sentences instead of treating the alphabet as an isolated list.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| bu | this |
| şu | that, drawing attention |
| o | that; he, she or it |
| ne | what |
| kitap | book |
| çay | tea |
| su | water |
| kapı | door |

### Worked examples

- `Bu ne?`: What is this? The question keeps the identifying frame.
- `Bu çay.`: This is tea; ç is the ch-like consonant.
- `Şu kapı.`: That is a door; the last vowel is dotless ı.

### Common trap

Do not replace ş with s or ı with i when writing answers. Those are different Turkish letters. A simplified keyboard spelling may be understood in context, but it is not the standard spelling being taught.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Identify a book: This is a book.
2. Ask: What is this?
3. Identify water: This is water.
4. Point out a door: That is a door. Use şu.

***Answers:***

1. `Bu kitap.`
2. `Bu ne?`
3. `Bu su.`
4. `Şu kapı.`

### Why these answers work

Bu and şu introduce objects without an English-style “is.” Kitap, su and kapı are ordinary noun forms. Ne replaces the unknown identity. The final ı in kapı is part of the noun and must be retained.

### Retrieve and recombine

Read the six core letters ç, ş, c, ı, ö and ü in words you know. Then point to a real object and identify it with bu, keeping the alphabet distinction visible in your written version.


## Lesson 3: Vowel harmony as a suffix choice

**Outcome:** choose the common plural suffix and understand the two main vowel-harmony patterns.

### Think first

Why is the plural of kitap kitaplar, but the plural of ev evler? Which vowel in each noun gives the most useful clue?

### Follow the last vowel

For a two-way suffix vowel A, choose a after a back vowel a, ı, o or u, and e after a front vowel e, i, ö or ü. The plural suffix is therefore lar or ler: kitaplar, evler, okullar, öğrenciler. The last vowel of the base normally determines the choice. A long word does not require you to restart from its first letter every time you add a suffix.

Four-way harmony also considers rounding. For the notation I, choose ı after a or ı, i after e or i, u after o or u, and ü after ö or ü. This pattern appears in many person and case endings later. It is a separate choice from the two-way plural suffix: a noun with ü still takes ler in the plural, not a made-up lür.

Some borrowed words have exceptional suffix behaviour, so vowel harmony is a productive default rather than proof that every written vowel sequence must be native and regular. The common nouns in today's table follow the regular pattern.

Plural marking is not automatically required whenever English uses a plural noun. In particular, nouns after numbers are normally singular in Turkish. Today we practise uncounted plural groups; the number construction will be introduced separately.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| ev | house |
| şehir | city |
| okul | school |
| gün | day |
| kitaplar | books |
| evler | houses |
| okullar | schools |
| günler | days |

### Worked examples

- `Bunlar kitap.`: These are books; bunlar means these, and the predicate can remain singular.
- `Kitaplar burada.`: The books are here; the noun has back-vowel plural lar.
- `Evler burada.`: The houses are here; the noun has front-vowel plural ler.

### Common trap

Do not let the English pronunciation of a loanword choose the suffix. Use its Turkish vowels. Also, lar/ler has two choices, while the I notation has four; those patterns must not be collapsed.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Write the plural of okul.
2. Write the plural of öğrenci.
3. Write the plural of şehir.
4. Write the plural of kitap.

***Answers:***

1. `Okullar.`
2. `Öğrenciler.`
3. `Şehirler.`
4. `Kitaplar.`

### Why these answers work

Okul ends in the back vowel u, so it takes lar. Öğrenci and şehir end in front i, so they take ler. Kitap ends in the back vowel a and takes lar. Capitalisation simply reflects the answer's sentence position.

### Retrieve and recombine

Sort ev, okul, şehir and gün into front-vowel and back-vowel groups. Then form their plurals and explain why ü produces ler here even though it can produce ü in a four-way suffix.


## Lesson 4: Identity questions and negative descriptions

**Outcome:** check an identity and deny a description using the right question and negative forms.

### Think first

If öğretmensiniz means “you are a teacher,” where might a question particle fit? Does denying the identity require the same suffix as denying an action?

### Separate words with attached endings

The yes-or-no question particle has the forms mı, mi, mu and mü, selected by four-way harmony. It is written separately from the preceding word. In a noun sentence, the person ending follows the question particle: Siz öğretmen misiniz? means “Are you a teacher?” Ben öğrenci miyim? means “Am I a student?” A connecting y appears before the vowel-initial person ending in miyim.

Değil negates noun and adjective predicates. Ben öğretmen değilim means “I am not a teacher.” The person ending attaches to değil, giving değilim, değilsin, değiliz and değilsiniz. Third-person O öğrenci değil means “he or she is not a student.” This negative word is not the general negator for every action verb.

Evet and hayır are independent yes and no responses. Hayır, öğrenci değilim is a complete correction. The short response hayır can also stand alone when the context is clear.

The question particle follows the element being questioned in more advanced patterns, but our examples focus on whole identity statements. Learn the spacing now: separate particle, attached person ending. This is one of the most useful spelling habits to establish before longer verb forms arrive.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| evet | yes |
| hayır | no |
| değil | not, with noun or adjective predicates |
| öğretmen misiniz | are you a teacher, respectful |
| öğrenci miyim | am I a student |
| değilim | I am not |
| değilsiniz | you are not, respectful or plural |

### Worked examples

- `Siz öğrenci misiniz?`: Are you a student? The question particle is separate.
- `Ben öğretmen değilim.`: I am not a teacher; the person ending is on değil.
- `O öğrenci değil.`: He or she is not a student; there is no gender agreement.

### Common trap

Do not write the question particle as part of öğretmen. Conversely, the person ending belongs to the particle in misiniz. Değil is the negative pattern for these noun sentences, not a replacement for every verb's negative suffix.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Ask respectfully: Are you a teacher?
2. Say: I am not a student.
3. Say: She is not a teacher.
4. Ask: Am I a teacher?

***Answers:***

1. `Siz öğretmen misiniz?`
2. `Ben öğrenci değilim.`
3. `O öğretmen değil.`
4. `Ben öğretmen miyim?`

### Why these answers work

Misiniz and miyim combine the harmonised question particle with person endings. Değilim carries first-person information, while değil needs no third-person ending in the neutral examples. The occupation nouns themselves remain unchanged.

### Retrieve and recombine

Make an incorrect guess about a person's occupation, then correct it in that person's voice. Keep the noun stable while moving person information between the affirmative, question and negative patterns.


## Lesson 5: Describing objects without gender agreement

**Outcome:** describe a familiar noun and distinguish an adjective phrase from a complete description.

### Think first

Yeni means “new.” Does it need a different form before a house, a book or a door, as adjectives do in some other languages?

### An adjective stays unchanged

Turkish adjectives normally precede the noun they modify: yeni kitap, “new book,” and büyük ev, “big house.” They do not agree for grammatical gender because Turkish nouns have no masculine/feminine gender system. They also do not become plural just because the noun does: yeni kitaplar, “new books.”

A predicate description can place the noun first: Kitap yeni, “the book is new.” The difference between yeni kitap and kitap yeni is therefore useful. The first is a noun phrase; the second can be a complete sentence in context. Bu yeni kitap identifies or presents “this new book,” while Bu kitap yeni clearly says “this book is new.”

Çok means “very” before an adjective. Çok büyük means very big, and çok küçük means very small. The adjective still does not change its ending for the object described. Güzel can mean beautiful, nice or good in an appropriate context; do not assume it translates every English use of “good.”

To describe yourself, add the noun-sentence person ending to an adjective: Ben mutluyum, “I am happy.” The final u in mutlu selects u in the four-way ending, with y linking the vowels. This recycles both personal predication and harmony in a new grammatical setting.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| yeni | new |
| eski | old, formerly existing |
| büyük | big |
| küçük | small |
| güzel | beautiful; nice |
| çok | very; much or many |
| mutlu | happy |

### Worked examples

- `Bu kitap yeni.`: This book is new; the adjective is the predicate.
- `Yeni kitaplar burada.`: The new books are here; yeni does not become plural.
- `Ben mutluyum.`: I am happy; the adjective carries the person ending.

### Common trap

Do not add plural lar/ler to the adjective in yeni kitaplar. Also, eski often means old as opposed to new or former; a person's age is normally described with yaşlı, not by transferring every English use of old.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: This house is big.
2. Say: This book is very small.
3. Say: I am happy.
4. Say: The old books are here. Use burada for here.

***Answers:***

1. `Bu ev büyük.`
2. `Bu kitap çok küçük.`
3. `Ben mutluyum.`
4. `Eski kitaplar burada.`

### Why these answers work

Büyük and küçük remain unchanged predicates. Mutluyum uses the speaker ending after a vowel-final adjective. Eski stays singular-looking before plural kitaplar, and burada gives the location without changing adjective agreement.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe a new object and an old object, then turn one description into a noun phrase. Explain how moving yeni or eski changes the structure without requiring a gender ending.


## Lesson 6: Actions happening now

**Outcome:** say what you are doing with the common present progressive suffix.

### Think first

Okumak means “to read,” while okuyorum means “I am reading.” Which part names the action, which marks the time view, and which identifies the speaker?

### Stem plus progressive plus person

Remove the dictionary infinitive ending mak or mek to find a useful stem: okumak gives oku-, gelmek gives gel-. The progressive suffix is conventionally written -(I)yor. After a consonant, the vowel follows four-way harmony: gel becomes geliyor. After some vowel-final stems, the stem vowel changes or merges with the suffix pattern: oku becomes okuyor, and ye, eat, becomes yiyor. We teach the full common forms rather than pretending all stems can be handled by blind concatenation.

First-person singular adds um to the progressive form: okuyorum, geliyorum. The vowel in the person ending follows the fixed o in yor, so it is um in these forms. Ben kitap okuyorum means “I am reading a book.” Neutral order is often subject, object, verb.

Şimdi means now and makes the immediate setting explicit. The progressive can also describe current ongoing situations and some planned near-future actions; it is not limited to the exact second of speaking. This lesson uses unmistakably current activities.

Turkish frequently omits the subject pronoun when the ending and context identify it. Both Ben okuyorum and Okuyorum can be complete. Keep ben when it clarifies a contrast, rather than treating it as either always required or always forbidden.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| okumak | to read |
| gelmek | to come |
| yemek | to eat |
| içmek | to drink |
| okuyorum | I am reading |
| geliyorum | I am coming |
| şimdi | now |
| çalışmak | to work; to study |

### Worked examples

- `Ben kitap okuyorum.`: I am reading a book; the object precedes the verb.
- `Şimdi çalışıyorum.`: I am working now; the ending identifies the speaker.
- `Su içiyorum.`: I am drinking water; an overt I is unnecessary here.

### Common trap

Do not keep mak/mek before the progressive suffix. Also, -yor contains a fixed o; it does not change into yer to match a front-vowel stem. Only the variable vowel position follows the stated harmony pattern.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: I am coming.
2. Say: I am reading a book.
3. Say: I am drinking tea.
4. Say: I am working now. Put now first.

***Answers:***

1. `Geliyorum.`
2. `Kitap okuyorum.`
3. `Çay içiyorum.`
4. `Şimdi çalışıyorum.`

### Why these answers work

Geliyorum inserts i after a front-vowel stem. Okuyorum preserves the rounded high vowel of oku. İçiyorum places the drinking action last. Çalışıyorum uses ı from the stem's last vowel before the fixed yor sequence.

### Retrieve and recombine

Replace the object in your drinking sentence, then replace the action with reading. Separate the stable person ending from the vocabulary choices so you can recombine them without memorising whole sentences.


## Lesson 7: Present questions, negatives and other people

**Outcome:** ask about an ongoing activity and say that it is not happening.

### Think first

If okuyorum is affirmative, would değil from an identity sentence normally be the basic way to negate this action? Where should the action's negative marker belong?

### Negation inside the verb

The ordinary negative progressive uses a negative element before yor, producing forms such as okumuyorum, “I am not reading,” and gelmiyorum, “I am not coming.” The negative ma/me vowel is narrowed in this environment. Learn these common complete forms first; the more general negative suffix will reappear with other tenses.

The progressive person forms include okuyorsun for informal singular you, okuyor for he or she, okuyoruz for we, okuyorsunuz for respectful or plural you, and okuyorlar for they. Third-person plural marking can vary with context and subject type, but these explicit human-subject forms provide a clear initial pattern.

A yes-or-no question places the separate question particle after the progressive base and before the person ending: Okuyor musunuz? means “Are you reading?” The particle is mu because yor ends in the back rounded vowel o. Geliyor musunuz? therefore also uses mu even though gel itself has a front vowel.

Negation and questioning can combine: Gelmiyor musunuz? means “Aren't you coming?” Such negative questions can carry expectations, so begin with neutral affirmative questions when you simply need information. The grammatical pieces tell you how to build the form; context determines what assumption the question may suggest.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| okuyorsun | you are reading, informal singular |
| okuyor | he or she is reading |
| okuyoruz | we are reading |
| okuyorsunuz | you are reading, respectful or plural |
| okumuyorum | I am not reading |
| gelmiyorum | I am not coming |
| musunuz | question particle with respectful or plural ending |

### Worked examples

- `O kitap okuyor.`: He or she is reading a book; the verb has third-person form.
- `Çay içmiyorum.`: I am not drinking tea; negation belongs inside the verb.
- `Kitap okuyor musunuz?`: Are you reading a book? The question particle remains separate.

### Common trap

Do not choose mi from the original stem when asking a progressive question. The immediately preceding yor controls mu. Also, avoid using değil as the default replacement for the verb's ordinary negative morphology.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: We are reading.
2. Ask respectfully: Are you coming?
3. Say: I am not working.
4. Say: She is drinking water.

***Answers:***

1. `Okuyoruz.`
2. `Geliyor musunuz?`
3. `Çalışmıyorum.`
4. `O su içiyor.`

### Why these answers work

Okuyoruz places the we ending after yor. Geliyor musunuz uses the separately written particle plus person ending. Çalışmıyorum contains the negative sequence before yor. O su içiyor keeps the object before the third-person verb.

### Retrieve and recombine

Ask an unfamiliar colleague about a current activity, then answer negatively in your own voice. Identify the changes in person, question spacing and the location of the negative marker.


## Lesson 8: Specific objects and the accusative

**Outcome:** distinguish an unspecified object from a particular object already identified in context.

### Think first

Compare Kitap okuyorum with Kitabı okuyorum. Both concern reading, so what difference might the extra ending on the second book express?

### Specificity changes the object form

A direct object that is definite or specific commonly takes accusative -(y)I. Kitap okuyorum means “I am reading a book” or “I am doing book-reading,” without identifying a particular book. Kitabı okuyorum means “I am reading the book,” with a particular object understood. The suffix is not a universal translation of English “the,” but this contrast is a useful starting point.

Kitap changes p to b before the vowel-initial suffix: kitabı. Many multisyllabic nouns ending in p, ç, t or k show a consonant alternation before vowel-initial suffixes, but not every word does. Learn the suffixed form alongside the noun. Kapı ends in a vowel, so the accusative uses a connecting y: kapıyı. Ev becomes evi, and su becomes suyu.

The suffix vowel follows four-way harmony. Kitap has final a and therefore selects ı. Ev has e and selects i. A demonstrative makes specificity easy to see: bu kitabı, this book as a direct object. Bu kitabı okuyorum means “I am reading this book.”

An unmarked object commonly stays next to the verb in the neutral pattern. Marked objects allow more flexibility, but this foundation keeps ordinary object-before-verb order while you learn what the suffix contributes.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| kitabı | the book, accusative |
| kapıyı | the door, accusative |
| evi | the house, accusative |
| suyu | the water, accusative |
| açmak | to open |
| açıyorum | I am opening |
| bu kitabı | this book, direct object |

### Worked examples

- `Bu kitabı okuyorum.`: I am reading this book; the specific object is marked.
- `Kapıyı açıyorum.`: I am opening the door; y connects the vowel-final noun to the suffix.
- `Suyu içiyorum.`: I am drinking the water; the water is understood as particular.

### Common trap

Do not add accusative to every noun merely because it follows an English verb. The distinction involves specificity and the Turkish construction. Also, kitap becomes kitabı here; the consonant change is part of the standard form.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: I am reading the book.
2. Say: I am drinking water, unspecified.
3. Say: I am opening this door.
4. Ask respectfully: Are you reading this book?

***Answers:***

1. `Kitabı okuyorum.`
2. `Su içiyorum.`
3. `Bu kapıyı açıyorum.`
4. `Bu kitabı okuyor musunuz?`

### Why these answers work

Kitabı marks the particular book. Unspecified su remains unmarked in the second answer. Kapıyı uses the linking y after a vowel, while kitabı shows p becoming b. The question retains the progressive question pattern independently of object marking.

### Retrieve and recombine

Imagine two books on a table. First say you are reading a book without identifying it, then point to one and say you are reading this book. Explain the added information carried by the accusative.


## Lesson 9: Location with de and da

**Outcome:** state where someone or something is using the locative suffix.

### Think first

Ev means house and evde means “at home” or “in the house.” What happens to the suffix after a word ending in a voiceless consonant, such as kitap?

### A place suffix with two adjustments

The locative suffix is written -DA in grammatical shorthand. Its vowel is a or e by two-way harmony. Its initial consonant is d after vowels and voiced consonants, but t after the voiceless consonants f, s, t, k, ç, ş, h and p. Thus evde, okulda, şehirde and kitapta are regular examples.

Ben evdeyim means “I am at home.” The locative phrase can take the same noun-predicate person ending learned earlier, with y connecting vowels. O okulda means “he or she is at school.” To describe an action at a place, put the location before the verb: Evde çalışıyorum, “I am working at home.”

Nerede means “where” for location. Nerede çalışıyorsunuz? asks where someone works or is working. Burada and orada mean here and there. Their spellings contain the same locative idea, but learn them as frequent whole words.

The suffix indicates location, not movement toward a destination. English “at,” “in” and “on” can all correspond to a Turkish locative in suitable contexts. Rather than choosing from English prepositions, ask whether the Turkish phrase tells us where an object or activity is situated.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| evde | at home; in the house |
| okulda | at school |
| şehirde | in the city |
| kitapta | in the book |
| nerede | where, location |
| burada | here |
| orada | there |
| park | park |

### Worked examples

- `Ben evdeyim.`: I am at home; the location phrase carries the person ending.
- `O okulda.`: He or she is at school; third-person predication is unmarked here.
- `Parkta çalışıyorum.`: I am working in the park; k selects the voiceless t form.

### Common trap

Do not write ev de when you mean “in the house.” The locative is attached. A separate de/da meaning “also” exists, but it is a different word and will be taught later.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: I am at school.
2. Say: I am reading at home.
3. Ask respectfully: Where are you working?
4. Say: The book is here.

***Answers:***

1. `Ben okuldayım.`
2. `Evde okuyorum.`
3. `Nerede çalışıyorsunuz?`
4. `Kitap burada.`

### Why these answers work

Okulda takes a back vowel, followed by the speaker ending yım. Evde uses front e after voiced v. Nerede asks for location. Burada can serve as the whole location predicate without an additional verb for is.

### Retrieve and recombine

Locate a person and an action in the same place. Then switch from ev to park and explain why both the vowel and the initial consonant of the locative suffix may need reconsideration.


## Lesson 10: Destinations and sources

**Outcome:** say where you are going and where you are coming from.

### Think first

Evde answers “where are you?” What different ending would be needed when the house is your destination rather than your current location?

### Toward and away from

The dative suffix -(y)A commonly marks a destination: eve, to the house or home; okula, to school; kapıya, to the door. Its vowel follows two-way harmony, and y connects a vowel-final noun. Eve gidiyorum means “I am going home.” Gitmek has the common progressive form gidiyorum, showing a consonant change in this verb.

The ablative suffix -DAn marks a source: evden, from the house; okuldan, from school; parktan, from the park. Its d/t choice follows the same voicing rule as the locative. Evden geliyorum means “I am coming from home.”

Nereye asks “where to?” and nereden asks “where from?” These contrast with nerede, where at. Turkish makes the relationship explicit in the question word, so one English “where” cannot determine the correct form by itself.

These case suffixes have additional uses beyond physical movement. For example, the dative also marks recipients, which we will use later. At this stage, a three-picture sequence is enough: someone is in the house, leaves from the house, and goes to school. The noun remains recognisable while its ending tells the listener how the place relates to the action.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| eve | homeward; to the house |
| okula | to school |
| kapıya | to the door |
| evden | from home |
| okuldan | from school |
| parktan | from the park |
| nereye | where to |
| nereden | where from |

### Worked examples

- `Eve gidiyorum.`: I am going home; the destination uses dative.
- `Okuldan geliyorum.`: I am coming from school; the source uses ablative.
- `Nereye gidiyorsunuz?`: Where are you going? The question asks for a destination.

### Common trap

Do not use nerede for every location-related English question. A destination needs nereye, and a source needs nereden in these patterns. Also, park takes parktan because k selects t.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: I am going to school.
2. Say: I am coming from home.
3. Ask respectfully: Where are you coming from?
4. Say: I am going to the door.

***Answers:***

1. `Okula gidiyorum.`
2. `Evden geliyorum.`
3. `Nereden geliyorsunuz?`
4. `Kapıya gidiyorum.`

### Why these answers work

Okula and kapıya identify destinations; kapıya needs the connecting y. Evden identifies a source. Nereden carries the same source relation inside the question word. The progressive person forms remain unchanged by the noun's case.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe a journey using a source and a destination in one sentence: Evden okula gidiyorum. Then reverse the two places and rebuild both suffixes rather than merely swapping whole English phrases.


## Lesson 11: Existence and having

**Outcome:** say that something is present or absent and express simple possession.

### Think first

Evde kitap var means “there is a book at home.” How could adding “my” to book turn an existence statement into “I have a book”?

### Var and yok

Var asserts existence or availability. Yok asserts absence. Evde su var means “there is water at home,” while evde su yok means “there is no water at home.” Neither word behaves like an ordinary action verb with the progressive suffix in these statements.

Possession commonly uses a possessed noun plus var or yok: Kitabım var, “I have a book,” and Kitabım yok, “I do not have a book.” The suffix -(I)m means my. Kitap becomes kitabım before the vowel-initial suffix, just as its consonant changed in the accusative. Evim is my house, and arabam is my car. After a vowel-final noun, the possessive suffix can be simply m.

Benim is the explicit possessor “my” or “of me.” Benim kitabım var includes both the possessor and the possessed-noun ending. The ending is not dropped just because benim appears. In a clear context, the shorter kitabım var is often enough.

This construction presents the possessed object as available to its owner. It differs structurally from English subject plus have plus object. Therefore the possessed noun is not automatically accusative, and var does not acquire an I ending to match the owner.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| var | there is; available |
| yok | there is not; absent |
| benim | my; of me |
| kitabım | my book |
| evim | my house |
| arabam | my car |
| araba | car |
| suyum | my water |

### Worked examples

- `Evde çay var.`: There is tea at home; var asserts availability.
- `Benim arabam var.`: I have a car; arabam carries the possession ending.
- `Kitabım yok.`: I do not have a book; yok states absence.

### Common trap

Do not use değil as the default negation of var. Yok is the ordinary absence word. Also, benim kitap var omits the possessed-noun ending required by the full possession phrase being taught here.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: There is water here.
2. Say: I have a book.
3. Say: I do not have a car.
4. Say: There is no tea at home.

***Answers:***

1. `Burada su var.`
2. `Kitabım var.`
3. `Arabam yok.`
4. `Evde çay yok.`

### Why these answers work

Var and yok state availability without personal conjugation. Kitabım and arabam identify the owner through their suffixes. The location expressions burada and evde specify where the available or absent item is relevant.

### Retrieve and recombine

Prepare for an imaginary trip by naming one thing you have and one thing you lack. Then describe an item available at home without claiming personal ownership, contrasting possession with simple existence.


## Lesson 12: Your book and the possession chain

**Outcome:** identify an owner and build a simple named possession phrase.

### Think first

Benim kitabım marks possession at both ends. What happens when the owner is another person, such as Ali, rather than the speaker?

### Both nouns participate

The respectful or plural “your book” is sizin kitabınız. Sizin identifies the possessor, and -(I)nIz on the noun identifies the possessed relationship. Your house is eviniz, and your car is arabanız. As before, the explicit possessor can be omitted when the ending and context are sufficient.

For a named owner, the possessor commonly takes genitive -(n)In and the possessed noun takes third-person possessive -(s)I. Ali'nin kitabı means Ali's book. The proper name is separated from its case suffix by an apostrophe in standard spelling. Ayşe'nin arabası means Ayşe's car; the possessed vowel-final araba uses connecting s before ı.

Kimin? means whose? Bu kimin kitabı? asks “Whose book is this?” The book still carries the possessed-noun suffix even though the owner is unknown. This is a relation between two parts, not merely an English apostrophe pasted into a Turkish word.

The third-person possessive form may resemble an accusative form, as kitabı does. Their grammatical functions differ. Context tells whether kitabı means “his/her book” as a possession or “the book” as a direct object. Later combinations of possession and case can make both roles visible in one longer word.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| sizin | your, respectful or plural |
| kitabınız | your book |
| eviniz | your house |
| arabanız | your car |
| kimin | whose |
| Ali'nin | Ali's |
| kitabı | his or her book; also accusative in another role |
| arabası | his or her car |

### Worked examples

- `Bu sizin kitabınız.`: This is your book; both parts can mark the possession relation.
- `Ali'nin kitabı burada.`: Ali's book is here; the owner and possessed noun are both marked.
- `Bu kimin arabası?`: Whose car is this? Arabası retains the possession suffix.

### Common trap

Do not write Ali'nin kitap for the ordinary possession chain. The possessed noun needs its own ending. Also, the apostrophe convention applies to proper names here, not to every common noun plus suffix.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: This is your house. Use respectful your.
2. Say: Ali's car is here.
3. Ask: Whose book is this?
4. Say: Your book is at home. Use kitabınız.

***Answers:***

1. `Bu sizin eviniz.`
2. `Ali'nin arabası burada.`
3. `Bu kimin kitabı?`
4. `Kitabınız evde.`

### Why these answers work

Eviniz and kitabınız mark respectful or plural ownership. Ali'nin supplies the genitive owner and arabası the possessed noun. Kimin asks for the owner while kitabı keeps the relation visible. Evde locates the owned book.

### Retrieve and recombine

Choose two owners and two objects. Build both possession chains, then ask whose one object is. Explain why the noun ending remains necessary even when the owner is named explicitly.


## Lesson 13: Numbers and counting objects

**Outcome:** ask a quantity and count a small number of objects without unnecessary plural marking.

### Think first

If kitaplar means books, would Turkish normally say iki kitaplar for “two books,” or does the number already supply the plural information?

### Number plus singular noun

After a cardinal number, the counted noun normally remains singular: iki kitap, three books üç kitap, five houses beş ev. The number already states the quantity. This is a productive contrast with English, which normally adds plural s after two or more.

Kaç means “how many.” Kaç kitap? asks how many books, again with singular kitap. Bir means one and can also function as an indefinite “a” in suitable contexts. Do not assume every occurrence of bir is a precise numerical contrast; context decides whether the speaker stresses one rather than two or simply introduces an unspecified item.

Adet is a general item counter used in some commercial or formal contexts, but everyday Turkish does not require a classifier between every number and noun. İki çay is a normal café order for two teas. A polite full request can be added later, but the quantity phrase itself is already complete.

Numbers can combine with possession and existence: İki kitabım var means “I have two books.” The noun carries my but not a plural suffix. This is a useful example of different information layers: the number states quantity, the possessive ending states ownership, and var states availability.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| bir | one; an indefinite a in context |
| iki | two |
| üç | three |
| dört | four |
| beş | five |
| altı | six |
| kaç | how many |
| adet | item; general counter in some contexts |

### Worked examples

- `İki kitap burada.`: Two books are here; the counted noun stays singular.
- `Kaç öğrenci var?`: How many students are there? Kaç also takes the unpluralised noun here.
- `Üç kitabım var.`: I have three books; possession and quantity coexist without lar.

### Common trap

Do not add lar/ler automatically after a number. İki kitap is the normal counted phrase. Also, Turkish does not need a special classifier for every category of object in these everyday examples.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: four books.
2. Ask: How many houses?
3. Say: I have two books.
4. Order the quantity: three teas.

***Answers:***

1. `Dört kitap.`
2. `Kaç ev?`
3. `İki kitabım var.`
4. `Üç çay.`

### Why these answers work

Dört and üç directly precede singular-looking nouns. Kaç supplies the quantity question without plural marking. Kitabım keeps the first-person possessive ending, and var asserts that the two books are available to the speaker.

### Retrieve and recombine

Count the same object with one, two and five, keeping the noun stable. Then turn the quantity into a possession statement and identify the new suffix that expresses ownership rather than plurality.


## Lesson 14: A direct past event

**Outcome:** report a straightforward past action using the common -DI past suffix.

### Think first

Geliyorum means “I am coming.” If geldim means “I came,” which part changed to locate the event in the past?

### Past suffix and person

The direct past suffix is -DI: dı, di, du, dü, tı, ti, tu or tü according to vowel harmony and consonant voicing. After the suffix, first-person singular uses m: geldim, I came; okudum, I read; çalıştım, I worked. The t in çalıştım follows voiceless ş, while the vowel ı follows the stem's last vowel.

Third-person singular has no extra person ending: geldi, he or she came. We came is geldik. Respectful or plural you came is geldiniz. These past person endings are not identical to the progressive endings, so do not attach the entire present ending to a past form.

Dün means yesterday. Dün evde çalıştım reports a past work event. The label “direct past” is often contrasted with the reported or inferred -miş form introduced later. It is a useful basic tense for ordinary personal narratives; it should not be treated as a legal claim that the speaker visually witnessed every detail.

Objects and locations keep their own grammar. Dün kitabı okudum has a specific accusative object, while dün kitap okudum describes book-reading without identifying a particular book. Past tense does not erase the specificity distinction.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| dün | yesterday |
| geldim | I came |
| okudum | I read, past |
| çalıştım | I worked |
| geldi | he or she came |
| geldik | we came |
| geldiniz | you came, respectful or plural |
| içtim | I drank |

### Worked examples

- `Dün evde çalıştım.`: I worked at home yesterday; the locative remains unchanged.
- `Kitabı okudum.`: I read the book; the specific object retains accusative.
- `O geldi.`: He or she came; no gender ending is added.

### Common trap

Do not make the past suffix always di. Its vowel harmonises, and d becomes t after the listed voiceless consonants. Also, Turkish past verbs do not change for the speaker's gender.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: I drank tea.
2. Say: We came yesterday. Put yesterday first.
3. Say respectfully: You worked.
4. Say: I read a book yesterday. Put yesterday first.

***Answers:***

1. `Çay içtim.`
2. `Dün geldik.`
3. `Çalıştınız.`
4. `Dün kitap okudum.`

### Why these answers work

İçtim uses t after voiceless ç. Geldik has the past we ending k. Çalıştınız combines the harmonised, devoiced past suffix with respectful or plural person marking. Okudum reports the past action without adding a specific-object ending.

### Retrieve and recombine

Retell one current activity as yesterday's event. Keep its object and location fixed, and explain the changes in both the tense suffix and the person ending.


## Lesson 15: Past negatives, questions and former states

**Outcome:** deny a past action, ask whether it happened and describe where you were.

### Think first

If geldim means “I came,” can the negative ma/me suffix fit before the past marker? Where does the question particle go when the past form already includes a person ending?

### Keep the suffix order clear

Gelmedim means “I did not come”: gel-me-di-m. The negative suffix precedes the past suffix, which precedes the person ending. Okumadım similarly means “I did not read.” Unlike the progressive negative, the negative vowel is not narrowed before yor because yor is absent.

Past yes-or-no questions normally keep the personal past form together and place the question particle after it: Geldiniz mi? means “Did you come?” Okudunuz mu? means “Did you read?” This differs from progressive geliyor musunuz, where the person ending follows the question particle. Learn the contrast as a real structural difference rather than forcing one question template onto every tense.

Past noun or location predicates use forms derived from idi. Evdeydim means “I was at home.” The connecting y follows the vowel-final locative phrase. Öğrenciydim means “I was a student.” Third-person evdeydi means he or she was at home.

Dün evde değildim means “I was not at home yesterday.” The negative noun-predicate word değil carries the past and person information. This contrasts with an action negative such as gelmedim, where the negative suffix belongs inside the verb.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| gelmedim | I did not come |
| okumadım | I did not read |
| geldiniz mi | did you come, respectful |
| okudunuz mu | did you read, respectful |
| evdeydim | I was at home |
| öğrenciydim | I was a student |
| değildim | I was not, noun predicate |

### Worked examples

- `Dün gelmedim.`: I did not come yesterday; negation precedes the past suffix.
- `Kitabı okudunuz mu?`: Did you read the book? The particle follows the complete past person form.
- `Dün okuldaydım.`: I was at school yesterday; the location takes past predication.

### Common trap

Do not copy the progressive question placement into every past question. Geldiniz mi keeps niz on the verb. Also, a past location negative uses değildim, while an action negative uses the verb's ma/me suffix.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: I did not work yesterday. Put yesterday first.
2. Ask respectfully: Did you drink tea?
3. Say: I was at home.
4. Say: I was not a student.

***Answers:***

1. `Dün çalışmadım.`
2. `Çay içtiniz mi?`
3. `Evdeydim.`
4. `Öğrenci değildim.`

### Why these answers work

Çalışmadım follows stem, negative, past and person order. İçtiniz mi keeps the person ending before the separate particle. Evdeydim attaches past predication to a location. Değildim negates the student identity rather than an action verb.

### Retrieve and recombine

Ask whether a person came yesterday, then answer negatively and state where you were instead. Use the contrast to retrieve both an action past and a past location predicate.


## Lesson 16: Future plans with ecek and acak

**Outcome:** state an intended future action and ask about another person's plan.

### Think first

Gelecek means “will come,” while geleceğim means “I will come.” Why does the k change before the first-person ending?

### A future suffix with linking changes

The future suffix is -(y)AcAk, realised as acak or ecek with a connecting y after a vowel-final stem. Gelmek gives gelecek; okumak gives okuyacak. Before a vowel-initial person ending, the final k commonly softens to ğ: geleceğim, I will come; okuyacağım, I will read. The letter ğ affects the neighbouring vowels and is not pronounced as a strong English g.

Respectful or plural you will come is geleceksiniz. The suffix's k stays because the following person ending begins with a consonant. We will come is geleceğiz. Thus the full word shape depends on the boundary between the future suffix and the person ending, not only on the original verb stem.

Yarın means tomorrow. Yarın çalışacağım states a future work plan. A question uses the separate particle after the future base and before the person ending: Gelecek misiniz? The particle is mi because gelecek ends with the front vowel e; Okuyacak mısınız? uses mı after a.

Future forms can express predictions as well as intentions. We practise concrete plans, but do not assume every acak/ecek sentence is a promise. The speaker's certainty and commitment also depend on context, adverbs and the kind of event described.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| yarın | tomorrow |
| geleceğim | I will come |
| geleceksiniz | you will come, respectful or plural |
| geleceğiz | we will come |
| okuyacağım | I will read |
| çalışacağım | I will work |
| gideceğim | I will go |

### Worked examples

- `Yarın geleceğim.`: I will come tomorrow; k becomes ğ before the speaker ending.
- `Evde çalışacağım.`: I will work at home; the location remains locative.
- `Gelecek misiniz?`: Will you come? The question particle carries the person ending.

### Common trap

Do not write gelecekim by mechanically attaching an English-like I ending. The standard form is geleceğim. Also, the future question particle follows harmony from the future base, not from an earlier unrelated word.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: I will read tomorrow. Put tomorrow first.
2. Say: We will come.
3. Ask respectfully: Will you work?
4. Say: I will go home.

***Answers:***

1. `Yarın okuyacağım.`
2. `Geleceğiz.`
3. `Çalışacak mısınız?`
4. `Eve gideceğim.`

### Why these answers work

Okuyacağım uses y after the vowel-final stem and ğ before the person ending. Geleceğiz marks we. Çalışacak selects the back-vowel question mı. Eve remains a destination, while gideceğim supplies the future action.

### Retrieve and recombine

Turn one of yesterday's events into tomorrow's plan. Then ask another person about the same plan and account for the different placement of the person ending in the question.


## Lesson 17: Wanting and necessity

**Outcome:** say what you want to do and what you need to do.

### Think first

In “I want to read,” could the reading action remain an infinitive while the wanting verb identifies the speaker?

### Infinitive plus a finite verb

Okumak istiyorum means “I want to read.” The infinitive keeps mak or mek and comes before istiyorum. The finite wanting verb carries the progressive and person information. Gitmek istiyorum means “I want to go,” and kitap okumak istiyorum means “I want to read a book.” This order puts the desired action before the expression of wanting.

İstiyor musunuz? asks respectfully whether someone wants something or wants to act. The question follows the progressive pattern already learned. İstemiyorum means “I do not want.” For example, çay içmek istemiyorum denies the desire to drink tea without saying that drinking tea is impossible.

Necessity can be expressed with the verbal noun plus a possessive ending and gerekiyor: çalışmam gerekiyor, “I need to work.” Here çalış-ma-m means “my working.” The first ma is a nominalising suffix, not the negative suffix in this particular construction. Context and the full pattern distinguish identical-looking pieces.

For a manageable foundation, learn okumam gerekiyor and gitmem gerekiyor as parallel first-person need constructions. A wider account of verbal nouns and all possessive persons can follow later. Today the important meaning distinction is desire versus necessity, not treating both as interchangeable translations of “want to.”

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| istemek | to want |
| istiyorum | I want |
| istemiyorum | I do not want |
| okumak istiyorum | I want to read |
| çalışmam gerekiyor | I need to work |
| gitmem gerekiyor | I need to go |
| okumam gerekiyor | I need to read |

### Worked examples

- `Eve gitmek istiyorum.`: I want to go home; the destination belongs to gitmek.
- `Çay içmek istemiyorum.`: I do not want to drink tea; the wanting verb is negative.
- `Bugün çalışmam gerekiyor.`: I need to work today; çalışmam is a possessed verbal noun.

### Common trap

Do not interpret every ma/me sequence as negation. In çalışmam gerekiyor, the construction expresses the necessity of my working. Also, retain the infinitive in okumak istiyorum instead of using a finite reading verb.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: I want to read this book.
2. Say: I do not want to go.
3. Ask respectfully: Do you want to drink tea?
4. Say: I need to go.

***Answers:***

1. `Bu kitabı okumak istiyorum.`
2. `Gitmek istemiyorum.`
3. `Çay içmek istiyor musunuz?`
4. `Gitmem gerekiyor.`

### Why these answers work

The specific object bu kitabı belongs to okumak, while istiyorum carries person. İstemiyorum negates desire. The question uses the progressive wanting form. Gitmem gerekiyor follows the learned first-person necessity pattern.

### Retrieve and recombine

Name one action you want to do and another you need to do. Then choose an object or destination for each and place that information with the correct infinitive or verbal-noun phrase.


## Lesson 18: Ability and polite permission

**Outcome:** describe an ability and make a polite request with the potential form.

### Think first

If okuyorum is “I am reading,” what extra meaning appears in okuyabiliyorum? Is the speaker necessarily reading at this moment?

### The ability element

The productive ability element -(y)Abil- attaches to a verb stem, followed by tense and person marking. Okuyabiliyorum means “I can read” or “I am able to read.” Gelebiliyorum means “I can come.” The vowel in a/e follows two-way harmony, and y links the vowel-final oku stem.

A very common request pattern uses the aorist question: Yardım edebilir misiniz? means “Could you help?” Yardım etmek is “to help”; etmek changes to ed- before the vowel-initial ability element. The request is not simply a literal test of physical ability. In context it politely asks the listener to perform the action.

Girebilir miyim? means “May I enter?” and uses the same potential pattern for permission. Girmek means to enter. This is a useful social use of an ability form: the speaker may be physically able to enter but is asking whether entering is acceptable.

The negative potential has its own patterns, such as gelemiyorum, “I cannot come.” It is not always built by inserting a negative suffix after the full bil sequence. We learn that frequent form directly here and postpone a complete potential paradigm. Keep desire, ability and permission distinct when explaining what a sentence means.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| okuyabiliyorum | I can read |
| gelebilmek | to be able to come |
| gelebiliyorum | I can come |
| yardım etmek | to help |
| edebilir misiniz | could you do, respectful request |
| girebilir miyim | may I enter |
| gelemiyorum | I cannot come |

### Worked examples

- `Türkçe okuyabiliyorum.`: I can read Turkish; the sentence states an ability.
- `Yardım edebilir misiniz?`: Could you help? This is a polite request.
- `Girebilir miyim?`: May I enter? The ability form asks permission here.

### Common trap

Do not confuse “I cannot come” with “I do not want to come.” Gelemiyorum describes inability or practical impossibility, while gelmek istemiyorum denies desire. The social meaning of a request also goes beyond literal physical capacity.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: I can read.
2. Ask respectfully: Could you help?
3. Ask: May I enter?
4. Say: I cannot come.

***Answers:***

1. `Okuyabiliyorum.`
2. `Yardım edebilir misiniz?`
3. `Girebilir miyim?`
4. `Gelemiyorum.`

### Why these answers work

Okuyabiliyorum contains ability before progressive and person marking. Edebilir misiniz is the conventional respectful potential question. Girebilir miyim uses first-person questioning for permission. Gelemiyorum is the learned negative ability form.

### Retrieve and recombine

Imagine you cannot attend a meeting but can help with reading. State the limitation and the ability, then ask permission to enter a room using the same potential family in a different social function.


## Lesson 19: Everyday habits with the aorist

**Outcome:** describe a regular habit and recognise the common aorist pattern in polite offers.

### Think first

Does “I read every evening” require the same event view as “I am reading right now”? How can a different form present a general habit?

### A general or habitual view

The aorist often describes habits, general tendencies, willingness and some future-oriented meanings. Okurum means “I read” in a habitual or general context. Her akşam kitap okurum means “I read books every evening.” İçerim means “I drink,” and giderim means “I go.” The aorist suffix has several stem-dependent forms, commonly r, ar/er or ır/ir/ur/ür. Many one-syllable verbs need their pattern learned individually, so this lesson teaches selected frequent forms rather than a false universal rule.

Her gün means every day; her sabah every morning. These phrases make habitual interpretation explicit. The progressive can also describe routines in natural Turkish, so this is a contrast of viewpoint and usage, not a claim that habits forbid the progressive.

The negative first-person singular aorist is special: okumam, “I do not read,” and içmem, “I do not drink.” Other persons commonly show z, as in okumaz, he or she does not read. Do not add z automatically to the first-person form.

Çay içer misiniz? is a polite offer, “Would you like some tea?” Its literal structure is a question about drinking, but the service or hospitality context makes it an offer. This connects grammatical tense-aspect learning with a practical conversational function.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| okurum | I read, habitually |
| içerim | I drink, habitually |
| giderim | I go, habitually |
| okumam | I do not read, habitual |
| içmem | I do not drink, habitual |
| her gün | every day |
| her akşam | every evening |
| her sabah | every morning |

### Worked examples

- `Her akşam kitap okurum.`: I read books every evening; the habit is explicit.
- `Kahve içmem.`: I do not drink coffee; the first-person negative has no z.
- `Çay içer misiniz?`: Would you like tea? In hospitality this functions as an offer.

### Common trap

Do not form every aorist by adding the same vowel to every stem. İçerim and okurum illustrate different patterns. Also, the aorist is not merely a renamed English simple present; it has several context-dependent uses.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: I read every day. Put every day first.
2. Say: I do not drink tea, as a habit.
3. Offer politely: Would you like tea?
4. Say: I go home every evening. Put every evening first.

***Answers:***

1. `Her gün okurum.`
2. `Çay içmem.`
3. `Çay içer misiniz?`
4. `Her akşam eve giderim.`

### Why these answers work

Okurum and giderim are the taught affirmative aorist forms. İçmem is the first-person negative. İçer misiniz uses a separate question particle and respectful ending, with a polite offer interpretation supplied by context.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe one habit you have and one you do not have. Then reuse the drinking verb in an offer, explaining why the same grammatical family can serve a different conversational purpose.


## Lesson 20: Reported and inferred past information

**Outcome:** recognise -miş when reporting information or noticing a result indirectly.

### Think first

If you find that Ali is already home but did not see him arrive, how could Turkish mark your report differently from a straightforward narrative of an arrival you experienced?

### A source-sensitive past form

The suffix -mIş gives forms such as gelmiş, “apparently came” or “has come, as I learn or infer.” Its vowel follows four-way harmony: okumuş, read; çalışmış, worked. It can report information learned from someone else or infer an event from a result. These are common functions, not a complete definition of every use.

Ali gelmiş can be said after hearing that Ali arrived or discovering his presence. Ali geldi is the ordinary direct-past statement. The distinction concerns how the event is presented and how the speaker relates to the information, not simply whether the event is true or false. Reported information can be perfectly reliable.

First-person gelmişim can express discovering something about oneself, sometimes with surprise or a realisation. That is a more specialised context than a basic self-narrative, so the production exercises stay mainly with third-person reports.

The suffix also appears in stories and other discourse patterns. Avoid translating it with “apparently” in every English sentence if that would falsely imply doubt. English sometimes needs a fuller explanation such as “I hear that...” or no explicit source word at all. Our readings use apparently as a compact teaching cue while the prose explains the broader range.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| gelmiş | apparently came; has come, learned or inferred |
| okumuş | apparently read |
| çalışmış | apparently worked |
| gitmiş | apparently went |
| Ali gelmiş | Ali has arrived, as learned or inferred |
| duymak | to hear |
| haber | news; information |

### Worked examples

- `Ali gelmiş.`: Ali has arrived, as I learn or infer; the source stance is indirect.
- `O kitabı okumuş.`: Apparently he or she read the book; the object remains specific.
- `Ayşe evde çalışmış.`: I hear that Ayşe worked at home; the location is unchanged.

### Common trap

Do not label -miş as a marker of lying or uncertainty. It can report reliable information. Also, its vowel harmonises, but the consonant ş stays the same in the ordinary forms taught here.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Report indirectly: Ali went.
2. Report indirectly: She read this book.
3. Report indirectly: Ayşe came.
4. Report indirectly: He worked at school.

***Answers:***

1. `Ali gitmiş.`
2. `O bu kitabı okumuş.`
3. `Ayşe gelmiş.`
4. `O okulda çalışmış.`

### Why these answers work

Gitmiş and gelmiş select i; okumuş selects u; çalışmış selects ı. Each form presents a past event with the reported or inferred stance requested. The accusative object and locative phrase keep their independent grammatical functions.

### Retrieve and recombine

Imagine learning about a friend's day from a message. Report one event with -miş, then retell a separate event from your own ordinary narrative with -DI. Explain the presentation difference without treating either as untrue.


## Lesson 21: Requests, commands and considerate wording

**Outcome:** make a respectful direct request and recognise a basic prohibition.

### Think first

Gel is an informal command “come.” What change makes the command suitable for respectful or plural address, and can lütfen alone replace that grammatical choice?

### Imperative forms

A simple second-person singular imperative often uses the bare stem: gel, come; oku, read. Respectful or plural commands commonly use -(y)In: gelin, okuyun. An expanded form such as geliniz is also possible in more formal contexts. This course uses the common shorter respectful forms.

Lütfen means please. Lütfen yavaş konuşun asks someone to speak slowly. Konuşmak is to speak, and konuşun is the respectful imperative. Yavaş means slow or slowly in this use. Tekrar means again; Lütfen tekrar edin asks someone to repeat. Etmek has the respectful imperative edin, with its familiar consonant alternation.

A negative imperative uses ma/me before the person form: gelmeyin, do not come; açmayın, do not open. Kapıyı açmayın means “do not open the door.” A prohibition can be appropriate for safety or a clear instruction, but a softer question may be preferable in other interactions.

Use lütfen with suitable tone and context rather than assuming it transforms any command into a considerate request. The potential question from Lesson 18 offers an indirect alternative. Knowing both forms lets you make a practical choice based on urgency, relationship and the listener's likely freedom to decline.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| gelin | come, respectful or plural imperative |
| okuyun | read, respectful or plural imperative |
| lütfen | please |
| yavaş | slow; slowly |
| konuşun | speak, respectful imperative |
| tekrar edin | repeat, respectful imperative |
| açmayın | do not open, respectful imperative |
| teşekkürler | thanks |

### Worked examples

- `Lütfen yavaş konuşun.`: Please speak slowly; the verb is a respectful imperative.
- `Lütfen tekrar edin.`: Please repeat; edin is the learned form of etmek.
- `Kapıyı açmayın.`: Do not open the door; negation precedes the imperative ending.

### Common trap

Do not address an unfamiliar adult with a bare stem merely because lütfen is present. Also, negative imperatives place ma/me inside the verb; they are not built with the noun-predicate word değil.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Ask respectfully: Please read.
2. Ask respectfully: Please come.
3. Ask respectfully: Please repeat.
4. Tell someone respectfully: Do not open this door.

***Answers:***

1. `Lütfen okuyun.`
2. `Lütfen gelin.`
3. `Lütfen tekrar edin.`
4. `Bu kapıyı açmayın.`

### Why these answers work

Okuyun needs y after the vowel-final stem, while gelin follows a consonant. Edin is the conventional imperative of etmek. Açmayın combines negative ma, connecting y and the respectful ending; kapıyı remains a specific direct object.

### Retrieve and recombine

Imagine missing a spoken instruction. Ask for slower speech and repetition, then thank the speaker. Finally, rephrase one direct request as a potential question to compare the two levels of directness.


## Lesson 22: Adding, contrasting and giving reasons

**Outcome:** connect short sentences and keep the separate word de/da distinct from the locative suffix.

### Think first

Evde means “at home,” but ben de means “I too.” Why is the space a meaningful grammatical distinction rather than optional typography?

### Connections between ideas

Ve means and, ama means but, and çünkü means because. Ben okuyorum ve o çalışıyor joins two actions. Gelmek istiyorum ama çalışmam gerekiyor contrasts a desire with an obligation. Evdeyim çünkü çalışıyorum supplies a reason for being at home. Each clause retains its own verb and person structure.

The separate word de/da means also or too and follows two-way vowel harmony. Ben de geliyorum means “I am coming too.” It is written separately and does not change d to t after a voiceless consonant: Kitap da burada, “the book is here too.” This contrasts with attached locative kitapta, “in the book.”

İle means with and can be written separately or attached in harmonised forms such as arkadaşla, with a friend, and arabayla, by car. The attached form uses la/le with y after a vowel. It introduces companionship or means in these examples, not possession.

Connecting clauses does not require every pronoun to be repeated. Where the subject remains clear, a later clause can omit it naturally. Keep enough information for the listener to know who acts, rather than following a rule that pronouns must always be present or absent.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| ve | and |
| ama | but |
| çünkü | because |
| de | also, separate front-vowel form |
| da | also, separate back-vowel form |
| ile | with |
| arkadaşla | with a friend |
| arabayla | by car |

### Worked examples

- `Ben de geliyorum.`: I am coming too; de is a separate word.
- `Kitap da burada.`: The book is here too; the particle retains d after p.
- `Arabayla geliyorum.`: I am coming by car; the attached ile form uses y after a vowel.

### Common trap

Do not spell the additive particle as a locative suffix. Kitap da and kitapta mean different things. The additive particle remains de/da, while the locative can have te/ta after voiceless consonants.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: I am reading too.
2. Say: I want to come, but I need to work.
3. Say: I am coming with a friend.
4. Say: The door is here too.

***Answers:***

1. `Ben de okuyorum.`
2. `Gelmek istiyorum ama çalışmam gerekiyor.`
3. `Arkadaşla geliyorum.`
4. `Kapı da burada.`

### Why these answers work

De and da add a participant or item and remain separate. Ama connects two already learned constructions without altering them. Arkadaşla uses the attached form of ile to mark companionship. Kapı selects the back-vowel additive da.

### Retrieve and recombine

Write two sentences using the same noun: one with attached locative and one with separate also. Then explain how the spacing, possible consonant change and meaning differ.


## Lesson 23: Comparing two choices

**Outcome:** compare familiar objects and express a simple preference with a reason.

### Think first

If büyük means big, how could adding daha change the comparison? What role might the ablative suffix play in identifying the thing used as a standard?

### More and most

Daha before an adjective means more in a comparison: daha büyük, bigger; daha küçük, smaller; daha güzel, nicer or more beautiful. The adjective does not acquire an English-style er ending. Bu ev daha büyük means “this house is bigger,” with the comparison standard understood from context.

To name the standard, Turkish commonly uses the ablative: Bu ev o evden daha büyük means “this house is bigger than that house.” The same den suffix previously meant from in a movement sentence. Here it marks the point of comparison. A case suffix can serve several related grammatical functions without making the earlier source meaning incorrect.

En marks a superlative in a suitable set: en büyük ev, the biggest house. Turkish does not add a separate equivalent of English the to every such phrase. The relevant comparison group should be clear from the situation, otherwise “the biggest” leaves the listener wondering which houses are being compared.

Ucuz means cheap or inexpensive, and pahalı means expensive. These are useful for comparing options without asserting any real-world price. Bu kitap daha ucuz is an imagined comparison, not a current shopping recommendation. You can connect a preference to a reason using çünkü and the wanting construction already learned.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| daha | more, comparative marker |
| en | most, superlative marker |
| ucuz | cheap; inexpensive |
| pahalı | expensive |
| daha büyük | bigger |
| daha küçük | smaller |
| o evden | than that house, comparison standard |

### Worked examples

- `Bu kitap daha ucuz.`: This book is cheaper; the other option is understood.
- `Bu ev o evden daha büyük.`: This house is bigger than that house; evden marks the standard.
- `En küçük kitap burada.`: The smallest book is here; the set is supplied by context.

### Common trap

Do not add an English comparative ending to a Turkish adjective. Also, evden does not always describe physical departure: after a comparison it can identify the standard against which something is measured.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: This house is smaller.
2. Say: This book is more expensive.
3. Say: This house is bigger than that house.
4. Say: The cheapest book is here.

***Answers:***

1. `Bu ev daha küçük.`
2. `Bu kitap daha pahalı.`
3. `Bu ev o evden daha büyük.`
4. `En ucuz kitap burada.`

### Why these answers work

Daha forms each comparative without changing the adjective. O evden names the comparison standard with ablative. En forms the superlative phrase in the fourth answer, and burada locates the selected book without adding a present linking verb.

### Retrieve and recombine

Compare two imaginary books by price or size, then identify the most suitable one within a set. Explain why the same ablative ending can mean from in a journey and than in your comparison.


## Lesson 24: A café and shop interaction

**Outcome:** order a quantity, ask a price and request the bill using familiar structures.

### Think first

You can already form iki çay and a polite potential request. What remains to turn the quantity phrase into a useful café order?

### Short service language

İki çay, lütfen is a natural compact request for two teas. The counted noun stays singular. Bir kahve alabilir miyim? means “May I have a coffee?” or literally “May I take a coffee?” Almak means to take or buy; the potential question makes the request courteous in a service setting.

Ne kadar? asks how much. Bu kitap ne kadar? asks the price of this book. Kaç lira? asks how many lira. After a number, lira normally stays singular: beş lira. These phrases ask for a price rather than asserting any current market value; the numbers here are language examples only.

Hesap, lütfen requests the bill. Teşekkür ederim is a fuller “thank you.” Rica ederim can answer thanks as “you're welcome.” These conventional phrases are useful complete turns; they do not need to be expanded into textbook-length sentences every time.

A café exchange can proceed from a greeting to an order, a clarification and payment. If you do not understand the reply, use lütfen tekrar edin or yavaş konuşun. Repair language belongs inside the interaction, not only in a classroom. Reusing it reduces the pressure to understand every unfamiliar word on the first attempt.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| kahve | coffee |
| almak | to take; to buy |
| alabilir miyim | may I have or take |
| ne kadar | how much |
| lira | Turkish currency unit |
| hesap | bill; account |
| teşekkür ederim | thank you |
| rica ederim | you are welcome |

### Worked examples

- `İki çay, lütfen.`: Two teas, please; the noun is not pluralised after the number.
- `Bir kahve alabilir miyim?`: May I have a coffee? The potential form serves a polite request.
- `Bu kitap ne kadar?`: How much is this book? The price question is a noun sentence.

### Common trap

Do not add lar to çay after iki. Also, almak can mean take or buy depending on context; a service request is not a claim that all English uses of “have” translate with almak.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Order: Three teas, please.
2. Ask: May I have water? Use alabilir miyim.
3. Ask: How much is this coffee?
4. Request: The bill, please.

***Answers:***

1. `Üç çay, lütfen.`
2. `Su alabilir miyim?`
3. `Bu kahve ne kadar?`
4. `Hesap, lütfen.`

### Why these answers work

Üç directly precedes singular çay. Alabilir miyim uses first-person potential questioning for a request. Ne kadar asks the price without a separate is. Hesap, lütfen is complete in the café context because the desired action is understood.

### Retrieve and recombine

Run a short café exchange with a quantity, a price question and a bill request. Change the setting to a bookshop and keep the same question structure while replacing only the relevant object.


## Lesson 25: Review: a day of useful choices

**Outcome:** construct a short account of your day and handle a new practical interaction by choosing suffixes deliberately.

### Think first

You are at home, you came from school, and tomorrow you will go to a café. Which endings distinguish location, source and destination even though all three phrases name places?

### Rebuild the message layer by layer

Begin with a noun and decide its role. Evde is location, evden source and eve destination. If the noun is possessed, a possession ending may come before its case ending in a fuller construction. If it is a particular direct object, choose accusative rather than assuming every English noun after a verb requires the same form.

Next choose the event view. Okuyorum describes a current activity; okudum a straightforward past event; okumuş reports or infers a past event; okuyacağım states a future action. Okurum can describe a habit or willingness in an appropriate context. These are not interchangeable decorations on the same message.

Then decide who is involved and how you are addressing them. The person ending often makes an explicit pronoun unnecessary, but a contrast may make the pronoun useful. Respectful siz forms and considerate wording support a suitable interaction with unfamiliar adults.

Finally check the written boundaries. Locative de/da attaches, additive de/da is separate, and the question particle is separate from its preceding word. Dotted and dotless I remain distinct through suffixes and capitalisation. A spelling check should preserve Turkish structure rather than normalising it toward an English keyboard.

The practice below combines earlier choices without introducing a new paradigm. Explain the purpose of each suffix before trying to produce a longer sentence.

### Working vocabulary

| Form | Meaning and use |
|---|---|
| evde | location: at home |
| evden | source: from home |
| eve | destination: homeward |
| kitabı | specific book as object in this context |
| okuyacağım | future reading by the speaker |
| okudum | past reading by the speaker |
| geliyor musunuz | respectful progressive question |
| gelmedim | negative past, I did not come |

### Worked examples

- `Dün evde kitap okudum.`: I read a book at home yesterday; location, object and past remain distinct.
- `Yarın kafeye gideceğim.`: I will go to the café tomorrow; kafe takes destination ye.
- `Bu kitabı okumak istiyorum.`: I want to read this book; the specific object belongs to the infinitive.

### Common trap

Do not treat a long Turkish word as an indivisible mystery. Equally, do not assume every suffix combination can be assembled mechanically without stem alternations or usage limits. The course gives a foundation, not every exception or discourse pattern.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: I did not come yesterday. Put yesterday first.
2. Say: I want to go home.
3. Ask respectfully: Are you reading this book?
4. Say: I will work at home tomorrow. Put tomorrow first.

***Answers:***

1. `Dün gelmedim.`
2. `Eve gitmek istiyorum.`
3. `Bu kitabı okuyor musunuz?`
4. `Yarın evde çalışacağım.`

### Why these answers work

Gelmedim contains negative, past and person information. Eve marks the destination of gitmek. Kitabı marks a specific object, while musunuz carries question and respectful person information. Çalışacağım combines future marking with the speaker ending and k-to-ğ alternation.

### Retrieve and recombine

Create a five-turn encounter at a new place: introduce yourself, ask a location, mention a past event, state a plan and make a request. Explain one harmony choice and one spacing choice from your own sentences.
