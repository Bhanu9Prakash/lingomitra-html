# Maithili: A pattern-first foundation

This course uses standard written Maithili associated with the central Mithila literary tradition, in Devanagari, with a respectful conversational teaching register. Maithili is not Hindi with different vocabulary. Its person and honorific agreement, case forms, and aspect patterns require their own treatment. Regional speech in India and Nepal varies. Tirhuta is another important Maithili script; this course does not teach Tirhuta or claim that romanisation replaces either orthography.

The romanisation is a readable, spelling-oriented aid: ā, ī, and ū represent the corresponding written vowels; ch/chh distinguish च/छ; ṭ, ḍ, and ṛ mark retroflex sounds; h after a stop indicates aspiration. Nasal vowels use a tilde. Thus अहाँ is ahã, and सँ is sã. Spellings such as अछि and छथि are shown as achhi and chhathi; their ordinary spoken realisations may involve vowel movement, often represented as aichh and chhaith in pronunciation-based resources. Do not treat these reading aids as narrow phonetic transcriptions. The distinction between the dental थ in थिक and retroflex ठ in ठीक also matters.

The lessons concentrate on first person, respectful अहाँ, and carefully labelled third-person examples. Maithili can express agreement with more than one participant. It would be misleading to present the selected forms as a complete system for every subject, object, addressee, gender, and dialect combination.

### References and review notes

Selected facts were checked in Ramawatar Yadav's *A Reference Grammar of Maithili* (1996), including its [publicly displayed text](https://www.scribd.com/document/371601618/A-Reference-Grammar-of-Maithili-OCR). OCR is imperfect, so it is not used as an orthographic authority. [Ruoan Wang's SALT paper](https://journals.linguisticsociety.org/proceedings/index.php/SALT/article/download/34.001/5632/12811) confirms the importance of honorific tiers and number-neutral agreement. [Omniglot's Maithili phrase collection](https://www.omniglot.com/language/phrases/maithili.php) was consulted as a limited conversational cross-check, not a comprehensive grammar. The explanations and exercises are original. Fluent-speaker review should prioritise spoken realisations, local politeness, and any extension beyond the deliberately selected agreement frames.

## Lesson 1: Say who you are

### Outcome and first sentence

You will introduce yourself with a complete Maithili sentence. `हम विद्यार्थी छी।` (ham vidyārthī chhī) means “I am a student.” Although Hindi commonly uses `हम` for “we,” Maithili uses `हम` for an individual speaker. This is an important early transfer trap. Do not assume that a familiar spelling retains exactly the same grammatical job across languages.

### Think before reading on

If `हम` identifies the speaker and `छी` supplies the first-person present linking form, where should a new identity go? Try “I am a teacher” using the table. `हम शिक्षक छी।` (ham shikshak chhī) keeps the order speaker, identity, linking verb. The identity changes, but there is no reason to replace the whole grammatical frame.

### Your initial vocabulary

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| हम | ham | I |
| छी | chhī | am; respectful you are |
| विद्यार्थी | vidyārthī | student |
| शिक्षक | shikshak | teacher |
| मैथिल | maithil | a Maithil person |
| नाम | nām | name |
| हमर | hamar | my |
| प्रणाम | praṇām | respectful greeting |

### Work through an introduction

`हम मैथिल छी।` (ham maithil chhī) means “I am Maithil.” Use an identity that is true of you; a course example is not an instruction to adopt an identity. `हम विद्यार्थी छी` is useful if you are introducing your role as a learner. Neither sentence needs an article equivalent to English “a.”

`हमर नाम रीता अछि।` (hamar nām rītā achhi) means “My name is Rita.” Here the subject is “my name,” not the person pronoun `हम`, so the linking form changes. The possessive `हमर` will receive fuller treatment later. For now, notice that English “my” and “I” do not identify the same grammatical subject.

Maithili present forms do not require the Hindi-style masculine `ता` versus feminine `ती` contrast after the identity noun. A woman and a man can both use `हम विद्यार्थी छी`. Some nouns themselves distinguish gender, such as `शिक्षिका` (shikshikā), female teacher. Noun meaning and verbal agreement are different topics.

### Common trap

Do not translate a sentence by replacing the Hindi pronoun and keeping Hindi `हूँ`. The Maithili sentence has its own linking form `छी`. Pronounce its initial consonant with aspiration, and keep the vowel clear rather than reading it as English “she.”

### Practice

Build one introduction for each prompt.

1. I am a student.
2. I am a teacher.
3. My name is Rita.
4. Give the respectful greeting taught here.

***Answers:***

1. `हम विद्यार्थी छी।` - `Ham vidyārthī chhī.`
2. `हम शिक्षक छी।` - `Ham shikshak chhī.`
3. `हमर नाम रीता अछि।` - `Hamar nām Rītā achhi.`
4. `प्रणाम।` - `Praṇām.`

### Why these answers work

The first two have the speaker as subject. The third identifies the speaker's name and therefore uses a third-person nonhonorific form for that noun phrase. The greeting is deliberately a complete social expression rather than a sentence requiring an added verb.

### Retrieve and recombine

Greet someone, introduce your role, and give your own name. Then explain why the name sentence does not end exactly like the sentence beginning with “I.” Keep the introduction short enough to understand each part.

## Lesson 2: Locate yourself and an object

### Outcome and location

You will say where you are and where a book is. `हम घर मे छी।` (ham ghar me chhī) means “I am at home.” The location marker `मे` follows the place noun. English normally places “in” or “at” before a noun; Maithili places this marker after. In this course, these short postpositions are written as separate words.

### Think before reading on

If `घर मे` means “at home,” where would “in the room” place the marker? Build the phrase before reading the sentence. `कोठरी मे` (koṭharī me) means “in the room,” and `पोथी कोठरी मे अछि।` (pothī koṭharī me achhi) means “The book is in the room.” The object being located is third person, so the final form differs from “I am.”

### Places you can use immediately

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| घर | ghar | house; home |
| कोठरी | koṭharī | room |
| मे | me | in; at |
| एतय | etay | here |
| ओतय | otay | there |
| कतय | katay | where |
| पोथी | pothī | book |
| अछि | achhi | is; exists, nonhonorific third person |

### Substitute the missing location

`पोथी एतय अछि।` (pothī etay achhi) means “The book is here.” `पोथी कतय अछि?` (pothī katay achhi) means “Where is the book?” The question word simply fills the unknown location slot. You do not need to put the linking verb before the subject as English does in many questions.

`हम एतय छी।` (ham etay chhī) means “I am here.” Notice that `एतय` already functions as a location expression; it does not need `मे` added after it in this simple pattern. A noun such as “room” needs the postposition to express the relation, while “here” supplies that relation lexically.

The same broad linking family can serve identity, state, and existence functions, but Maithili has additional copular forms and regional choices. This course will introduce a useful equative form later rather than forcing a rule borrowed from Nepali or another language onto all uses of English “be.”

### Common trap

Do not substitute Hindi `में` mechanically for standard Maithili `मे` in these examples. They are related-looking forms, but the chosen written convention should remain consistent. Also do not let the English translation “I am” hide the distinction between a person and the person's possessions.

### Practice

Use the location pattern.

1. I am at home.
2. The book is in the room.
3. The book is here.
4. Where is the book?

***Answers:***

1. `हम घर मे छी।` - `Ham ghar me chhī.`
2. `पोथी कोठरी मे अछि।` - `Pothī koṭharī me achhi.`
3. `पोथी एतय अछि।` - `Pothī etay achhi.`
4. `पोथी कतय अछि?` - `Pothī katay achhi?`

### Why these answers work

The first locates the speaker. The remaining answers concern a book and therefore use `अछि`. A question about location retains the same basic frame as a location statement. The postposition follows a place noun but is not inserted after every word translated by English “where” or “here.”

### Retrieve and recombine

Locate yourself in a room, then locate a book there. Finally, ask where the book is and answer with “there.” Watch the subject before choosing the final form.

## Lesson 3: Choose respectful address

### Outcome and honorifics

You will address someone respectfully and recognise that Maithili politeness has more than two levels. The practical respectful form in this course is `अहाँ` (ahã), “you.” `अहाँ विद्यार्थी छी।` (ahã vidyārthī chhī) means “You are a student.” Its ending is identical to the first-person form in this selected pattern, so the pronoun and context tell you who is meant.

### Think before reading on

If both “I am” and respectful “you are” can end in `छी`, can the ending alone identify the speaker? No. Compare `हम एतय छी` and `अहाँ एतय छी`. Shared verbal forms do not make the pronouns synonyms. Always interpret the sentence as a whole rather than assigning a unique English word to every ending.

### Relationship vocabulary

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| अहाँ | ahã | you, respectful |
| अपने | apne | you, very deferential in appropriate use |
| ओ | o | he/she; that |
| छथि | chhathi | is/are, honorific third person |
| जी | jī | respectful title particle |
| बाबूजी | bābūjī | respectful address/reference to father or an elder |
| शिक्षक | shikshak | teacher |
| नीक | nīk | good; well |

### Address and reference are different

`अहाँ कतय छी?` (ahã katay chhī) asks “Where are you?” respectfully. `ओ एतय छथि।` (o etay chhathi) means “He or she is here,” referring respectfully to a person. With nonhonorific reference, the corresponding ordinary third-person linking form is `अछि`. The pronoun `ओ` itself does not encode a male/female distinction.

`बाबूजी घर मे छथि।` (bābūjī ghar me chhathi) says “Father is at home,” with respectful reference. It refers to one person; the honorific ending does not prove plurality. Maithili agreement can track social status rather than grammatical number in ways that differ from English and Hindi.

Forms of `तोँ` are used at other familiarity or honorific levels, with different endings. A full account must distinguish those levels instead of labelling every non-`अहाँ` expression merely “informal.” For this foundation, use `अहाँ` in new adult interactions unless a fluent speaker helps you choose a locally appropriate alternative. `अपने` can be more deferential, but it is not necessary to alternate randomly between respectful pronouns.

### Common trap

Do not use the respectful third-person `छथि` after `अहाँ` just because both concern respected people. Addressing someone and referring to someone are different grammatical persons. Also avoid interpreting the shared `छी` form as evidence that Maithili lacks agreement.

### Practice

Maintain the stated relationship.

1. You are a student. (respectful)
2. Where are you? (respectful)
3. She is here. (honorific reference)
4. Father is at home. (use bābūjī)

***Answers:***

1. `अहाँ विद्यार्थी छी।` - `Ahã vidyārthī chhī.`
2. `अहाँ कतय छी?` - `Ahã katay chhī?`
3. `ओ एतय छथि।` - `O etay chhathi.`
4. `बाबूजी घर मे छथि।` - `Bābūjī ghar me chhathi.`

### Why these answers work

The first two address a respected listener. The last two refer to a respected third person. None requires more than one person. The English “she” in the third prompt is supplied by context; the Maithili pronoun can refer to either gender.

### Retrieve and recombine

Ask a teacher where they are, then tell another person that the teacher is in the room. The location is unchanged, but the grammatical perspective changes from address to third-person reference.

## Lesson 4: Ask what something is and who someone is

### Outcome and question words

You will distinguish “what,” “who,” and a yes-or-no question. `की` (kī) means “what,” while `के` (ke) means “who” in the question patterns here. A learner who knows Hindi should pay particular attention: the English-looking shortcut “kya means what” does not supply the Maithili form. Learn the contrast from a full sentence.

### Think before reading on

If you can see an object but do not know its identity, are you asking about a person or a thing? `ई की थिक?` (ī kī thik) asks “What is this?” The equative `थिक` is useful when identifying something. It is not the same word as `ठीक` (ṭhīk), “all right,” whose first consonant is retroflex.

### The question set

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ई | ī | this |
| ओ | o | that; he/she |
| की | kī | what; or in some questions |
| के | ke | who |
| थिक | thik | equative is, in this pattern |
| ठीक | ṭhīk | all right; correct |
| कलम | kalam | pen |
| कखन | kakhan | when |

### Work through the alternatives

`ई पोथी थिक।` (ī pothī thik) identifies the object as a book. `ई पोथी थिक?` with questioning intonation asks whether it is a book. The ordinary order can remain in place. `ई की थिक?` instead asks the listener to supply the identity. These questions make different demands even when both concern the same object.

`ओ के छथि?` (o ke chhathi) means “Who is he or she?” with respectful reference. It uses the honorific linking form already learned. A person's name or role can answer the question, but the reference should remain respectful. This course does not generalise the inanimate `थिक` example into a complete honorific paradigm.

`अहाँ ठीक छी?` (ahã ṭhīk chhī) means “Are you all right?” This checks a person's state, not their identity. The difference between `थिक` and `ठीक` is therefore both phonetic and grammatical. Pronounce the dental tongue position in the first and the curled-back retroflex position in the second.

### Common trap

Do not write `ई की ठीक?` when you mean “What is this?” That would confuse the adjective “correct/all right” with the equative form. Also avoid rearranging every question into English auxiliary-first order. The missing information usually stays in the relevant sentence slot.

### Practice

Ask or state the specified identity or condition.

1. What is this?
2. This is a book. (use thik)
3. Who is she? (honorific reference)
4. Are you all right? (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `ई की थिक?` - `Ī kī thik?`
2. `ई पोथी थिक।` - `Ī pothī thik.`
3. `ओ के छथि?` - `O ke chhathi?`
4. `अहाँ ठीक छी?` - `Ahã ṭhīk chhī?`

### Why these answers work

The first two identify a visible object. The third asks for a person's identity while keeping respect. The fourth predicates a condition with an adjective. The spellings `थिक` and `ठीक` must remain distinct; treating them as merely optional romanisation variants would erase the lesson's central contrast.

### Retrieve and recombine

Point to a pen and identify it. Then ask who a respected visitor is. Finally, ask that visitor whether they are all right. Each question concerns different missing information, so choose its pattern intentionally.

## Lesson 5: Negate a statement without changing its subject

### Outcome and negation

You will say that you are not at home and that a book is absent. Standard written Maithili commonly uses `नहि` (nahi) for negation; conversational varieties frequently use forms represented as nai. This course keeps the written `नहि` consistently. It is not an instruction to treat all regional pronunciations as errors.

### Think before reading on

If you negate `हम घर मे छी`, has the speaker changed? No. The first-person linking form stays in place while the negative is added: `हम घर मे नहि छी।` (ham ghar me nahi chhī), “I am not at home.” Negation changes the proposition, not the identity of its subject.

### Words for a correction

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| नहि | nahi | not; no |
| एतय | etay | here |
| ओतय | otay | there |
| घर | ghar | home |
| विद्यालय | vidyālaya | school |
| पोथी | pothī | book |
| कापी | kāpī | notebook |
| ठीक | ṭhīk | all right; correct |

### Preserve agreement in a negative

`पोथी एतय नहि अछि।` (pothī etay nahi achhi) means “The book is not here.” `अहाँ घर मे नहि छी।` (ahã ghar me nahi chhī) says “You are not at home,” respectfully. `बाबूजी घर मे नहि छथि।` (bābūjī ghar me nahi chhathi) says that Father is not at home. Compare the final forms across these sentences: the negative does not flatten the honorific distinctions.

A correction usually becomes more useful when you supply the true information. `हम घर मे नहि छी। हम विद्यालय मे छी।` (ham ghar me nahi chhī; ham vidyālaya me chhī) means “I am not at home. I am at school.” Two short statements are enough. There is no need to invent an unfamiliar contrastive conjunction before you can correct a misunderstanding.

You can also negate a state: `हम ठीक नहि छी।` (ham ṭhīk nahi chhī), “I am not all right.” Use it when that meaning is intended; it is broader than any particular symptom. The position before the final linking verb is a useful neutral model for these examples. Emphasis can motivate other placements in natural speech.

### Common trap

Do not substitute Hindi `नहीं हूँ` after the Maithili subject merely because the English sentence begins “I am not.” Also avoid deleting the honorific ending when the statement becomes negative. Respect remains part of how you refer to the person.

### Practice

Negate the statement requested.

1. I am not at home.
2. The book is not here.
3. Father is not at home. (use bābūjī)
4. I am not all right.

***Answers:***

1. `हम घर मे नहि छी।` - `Ham ghar me nahi chhī.`
2. `पोथी एतय नहि अछि।` - `Pothī etay nahi achhi.`
3. `बाबूजी घर मे नहि छथि।` - `Bābūjī ghar me nahi chhathi.`
4. `हम ठीक नहि छी।` - `Ham ṭhīk nahi chhī.`

### Why these answers work

The negative is added while the person and honorific choices remain visible. The book uses `अछि`, the speaker uses `छी`, and the respected third person uses `छथि`. The fourth negates a condition, so it retains the adjective `ठीक` rather than confusing it with equative `थिक`.

### Retrieve and recombine

Correct a mistaken location for yourself and for a respected relative. In each case, deny the wrong location and supply another. Keep the negative and affirmative versions side by side in your mind.

## Lesson 6: Describe everyday reading, eating, and drinking

### Outcome and the imperfective pattern

You will describe familiar activities with an action form followed by `छी`. `हम पोथी पढ़ैत छी।` (ham pothī paṛhait chhī) means “I read a book” or “I am reading a book,” depending on context. The form in `ऐत` presents the action imperfectively, without viewing it simply as a completed whole. It is broader than a single English tense label.

### Think before reading on

If English says “I read a book,” where should the object go in the Maithili sentence? Put it before the verb group: speaker, book, reading, am. The object does not move after `छी` merely because English puts it after “read.” Try the same frame with a drink before checking the examples.

### Small action vocabulary

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| पढ़ैत | paṛhait | reading; studying |
| लिखैत | likhait | writing |
| खाइत | khāit | eating |
| पिबैत | pibait | drinking |
| करैत | karait | doing |
| बाजैत | bājait | speaking |
| पानि | pāni | water |
| भात | bhāt | cooked rice |
| चाह | chāh | tea |

### Recombine before adding more vocabulary

`हम पानि पिबैत छी।` (ham pāni pibait chhī) means “I drink water.” `हम भात खाइत छी।` (ham bhāt khāit chhī) means “I eat rice.” Maithili distinguishes these ordinary eating and drinking verbs; do not transfer Nepali's frequent beverage use of “eat” into every Maithili sentence.

`हम मैथिली बाजैत छी।` (ham maithilī bājait chhī) means “I speak Maithili.” `हम काज करैत छी।` (ham kāj karait chhī) means “I work.” The object or complement can be a concrete thing, a language, or the noun `काज`, “work.” The overall verb-last structure remains useful across these contexts.

The action form is not always created by attaching the same visible suffix to an English-style dictionary stem. Compare `पिबैत`, with a b, and `खाइत`, without it. The language's stem and suffix combinations have their own sound patterns. Learn the small set as meaningful pairs rather than inventing forms from another language's infinitives.

### Common trap

Do not change `पढ़ैत` into Hindi-style `पढ़ता` or `पढ़ती` according to the speaker's gender. The selected Maithili present pattern does not use that contrast. Also retain the aspirated retroflex flap represented by `ṛh` in `पढ़ैत`; spelling-oriented romanisation is not a license to drop consonant distinctions.

### Practice

Use the imperfective action pattern.

1. I read a book.
2. I drink water.
3. I eat rice.
4. I speak Maithili.

***Answers:***

1. `हम पोथी पढ़ैत छी।` - `Ham pothī paṛhait chhī.`
2. `हम पानि पिबैत छी।` - `Ham pāni pibait chhī.`
3. `हम भात खाइत छी।` - `Ham bhāt khāit chhī.`
4. `हम मैथिली बाजैत छी।` - `Ham maithilī bājait chhī.`

### Why these answers work

Every answer puts the object or language before an imperfective action and first-person auxiliary. The eating and drinking stems differ. A woman and a man can use the same selected first-person present forms; an English translation does not justify adding a Hindi gender ending.

### Retrieve and recombine

Replace water with tea, and replace the book with a notebook. Then say that you work at home by adding the location phrase from Lesson 2 before the final verb group.

## Lesson 7: Ask about an activity and deny a habit

### Outcome and action questions

You will ask what a respected person does and say what you do not do. `अहाँ की करैत छी?` (ahã kī karait chhī) means “What do you do?” or “What are you doing?” in a suitable context. The question word occupies the missing activity slot. The shared `छी` form is appropriate for respectful address, as it was for identity and location.

### Think before reading on

If you change “I work” into “I do not work,” does the activity suddenly require an existence-only sentence? Keep the action structure: `हम काज नहि करैत छी।` (ham kāj nahi karait chhī), “I do not work.” The negative comes before the verb group here and leaves the subject relationship intact.

### Routine vocabulary

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| काज | kāj | work |
| की | kī | what |
| नहि | nahi | not |
| चाह | chāh | tea |
| दूध | dūdh | milk |
| विद्यालय | vidyālaya | school |
| पढ़ैत छी | paṛhait chhī | I/respectful you read |
| पढ़ैत छथि | paṛhait chhathi | reads, honorific third person |

### Move between perspectives

`अहाँ चाह पिबैत छी?` (ahã chāh pibait chhī) asks respectfully “Do you drink tea?” `हम चाह नहि पिबैत छी।` (ham chāh nahi pibait chhī) replies “I do not drink tea.” The question need not assume the answer. A positive question is often the clearest way to discover a habit without implying surprise.

`बाबूजी पोथी पढ़ैत छथि।` (bābūjī pothī paṛhait chhathi) means “Father reads a book,” respectfully. The action form remains imperfective, while the auxiliary changes for honorific third-person reference. `बाबूजी चाह नहि पिबैत छथि।` (bābūjī chāh nahi pibait chhathi) keeps that honorific in a negative sentence.

The form in this lesson can describe a routine or an unfolding activity in context. If the English prompt says “doing,” do not automatically replace it with an unlearned construction. Later, we will add an explicit progressive that makes ongoing activity particularly clear. Different languages divide the meanings of routine and ongoing action differently.

### Common trap

Do not assume a single Hindi question word and verb pattern will work after `अहाँ`. Also resist the temptation to turn the respectful auxiliary into a plural-only label. One respected person can be the subject of `पढ़ैत छथि`.

### Practice

Ask about or negate an activity.

1. What do you do? (respectful)
2. Do you drink tea? (respectful)
3. I do not drink tea.
4. Father reads a book. (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `अहाँ की करैत छी?` - `Ahã kī karait chhī?`
2. `अहाँ चाह पिबैत छी?` - `Ahã chāh pibait chhī?`
3. `हम चाह नहि पिबैत छी।` - `Ham chāh nahi pibait chhī.`
4. `बाबूजी पोथी पढ़ैत छथि।` - `Bābūjī pothī paṛhait chhathi.`

### Why these answers work

The first asks for an activity, while the second offers one for confirmation. The third supplies a negative habit without changing the first-person auxiliary. The fourth changes grammatical perspective to a respected third person and therefore uses the corresponding auxiliary.

### Retrieve and recombine

Ask a teacher whether they drink milk. Answer for yourself with a negative, then describe a respected relative's reading habit. Identify whether each sentence addresses someone, refers to someone, or describes the speaker.

## Lesson 8: Identify an owner

### Outcome and possessive phrases

You will identify your book and ask a respectful question about someone else's name. `हमर पोथी` (hamar pothī) means “my book.” `अहाँक नाम` (ahãk nām) means “your name,” respectfully. Maithili has its own possessive forms; copying Hindi `मेरा` and `आपका` would not teach the same language.

### Think before reading on

If a woman says “my book” and a man says “my book,” should the possessor form change just because the speaker's gender changes? The selected Maithili form remains `हमर`. Unlike the Hindi pattern many learners know, the ordinary possessives shown here do not alternate through a masculine/feminine/plural table according to the possessed noun.

### Possessive vocabulary

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| हमर | hamar | my |
| अहाँक | ahãk | your, respectful |
| ओकर | okar | his/her, nonhonorific |
| हुनक | hunak | his/her, honorific |
| नाम | nām | name |
| पोथी | pothī | book |
| घर | ghar | house; home |
| कलम | kalam | pen |

### Ownership inside a larger sentence

`ई हमर पोथी थिक।` (ī hamar pothī thik) means “This is my book.” `अहाँक नाम की अछि?` (ahãk nām kī achhi) asks respectfully “What is your name?” The person is respected through `अहाँक`, but the grammatical subject is the name. It takes the ordinary third-person form for that noun phrase.

`हुनक घर एतय अछि।` (hunak ghar etay achhi) means “His or her house is here,” respectfully referring to the owner. The house itself is not a respected person. This is a useful distinction between respect inside a noun phrase and the grammatical subject of the clause.

`हमर कलम कोठरी मे अछि।` (hamar kalam koṭharī me achhi) locates your pen. Nothing in “my pen” forces the sentence to be an identity sentence. A possessive phrase can function as a subject, an object, or an identity expression in a larger structure. Its job is to specify whose item is under discussion.

### Common trap

Do not attach the same genitive ending mechanically to every pronoun. `हमर`, `अहाँक`, `ओकर`, and `हुनक` have established shapes. Also avoid changing the final verb to first person merely because the English sentence contains “my.” The pen or name is the subject in those examples.

### Practice

Identify or locate the possession.

1. This is my book.
2. What is your name? (respectful)
3. His house is here. (honorific owner)
4. My pen is in the room.

***Answers:***

1. `ई हमर पोथी थिक।` - `Ī hamar pothī thik.`
2. `अहाँक नाम की अछि?` - `Ahãk nām kī achhi?`
3. `हुनक घर एतय अछि।` - `Hunak ghar etay achhi.`
4. `हमर कलम कोठरी मे अछि।` - `Hamar kalam koṭharī me achhi.`

### Why these answers work

The possessor form identifies an owner, but the rest of the sentence determines whether the item is being identified or located. The respectful owner does not make the house a human honorific subject. The final answer locates a pen, so it uses ordinary third-person `अछि`.

### Retrieve and recombine

Ask for a respected person's name, then identify a pen as yours. Finally, locate that person's book using an appropriate respectful possessive. Do not confuse ownership with the location of the owner.

## Lesson 9: Express where an item is available

### Outcome and having at hand

You will say that you have an item available and that you lack one. A useful Maithili possession frame places the item near or with the possessor: `हमरा लग कलम अछि।` (hamrā lag kalam achhi), “I have a pen.” The phrase `हमरा लग` is not the same as the subject `हम`; it establishes the person with whom the item is available.

### Think before reading on

Could you have a borrowed book without owning it? Yes. Compare `ई हमर पोथी थिक`, identifying ownership, with `हमरा लग पोथी अछि`, indicating that you have a book. Both can concern the same object, but they make different claims about your relation to it.

### Availability words

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| हमरा | hamrā | me, oblique form |
| लग | lag | near; with, in this possession frame |
| हमरा लग | hamrā lag | with me; I have |
| अहाँ लग | ahã lag | with you, respectful |
| पैसा | paisā | money |
| समय | samay | time |
| पोथी | pothī | book |
| नहि अछि | nahi achhi | is not; is absent |

### A statement, negative, and question

`हमरा लग पोथी अछि।` (hamrā lag pothī achhi) means “I have a book.” `हमरा लग कलम नहि अछि।` (hamrā lag kalam nahi achhi) means “I do not have a pen.” The existence of the item is affirmed or denied. The final verb is not a first-person action, even though English translates the clause with “I have.”

`अहाँ लग कलम अछि?` (ahã lag kalam achhi) asks respectfully “Do you have a pen?” The question can be answered with the full availability sentence, making clear what is available. If you then want the item, a request is a separate conversational step. Possession does not by itself imply permission to take something.

`हमरा लग समय नहि अछि।` (hamrā lag samay nahi achhi) means “I do not have time.” The construction extends to resources that are not physical objects. Nevertheless, English “have” covers many situations, including meals and experiences, so this one frame should not be used for every English expression containing that verb.

### Common trap

Do not treat `हमरा` as a longer stylistic alternative to `हम` in every position. It is a marked pronoun form that appears in particular grammatical environments. Maithili's wider agreement system can reflect more than one participant; the selected availability examples should not be extrapolated into an invented universal possession paradigm.

### Practice

Use the availability construction.

1. I have a pen.
2. I have a book.
3. I do not have a pen.
4. Do you have a pen? (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `हमरा लग कलम अछि।` - `Hamrā lag kalam achhi.`
2. `हमरा लग पोथी अछि।` - `Hamrā lag pothī achhi.`
3. `हमरा लग कलम नहि अछि।` - `Hamrā lag kalam nahi achhi.`
4. `अहाँ लग कलम अछि?` - `Ahã lag kalam achhi?`

### Why these answers work

The person is placed in a possession phrase with `लग`, while the item is paired with an existence verb. The negative denies availability. The respectful question preserves the addressee form while keeping the item as the thing whose existence is at issue.

### Retrieve and recombine

Say that you have a notebook but no pen, using two sentences. Then ask a respected colleague whether they have a pen. Contrast each availability statement with an ownership statement using `हमर`.

## Lesson 10: Use from, with, and on

### Outcome and postpositions

You will describe a starting point, accompaniment, and a surface location. Maithili postpositions follow their noun phrases. `सँ` (sã) can mark source, means, accompaniment, or a comparison standard in different constructions. Its meaning is not exhausted by one English preposition. Read it together with the noun and the action.

### Think before reading on

If someone comes `घर सँ`, is the house a traveling companion or a starting point? The motion context makes it a source: “from home.” If someone goes `मित्र सँ`, the human noun can support a “with a friend” interpretation. The surrounding construction helps determine the relationship.

### Relations and movement

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| सँ | sã | from; with; by, by context |
| पर | par | on |
| आबैत | ābait | coming |
| जाइत | jāit | going |
| बाजार | bājār | market |
| मित्र | mitra | friend |
| मेज | mej | table |
| गाम | gām | village |

### Work with concrete locations

`हम घर सँ आबैत छी।` (ham ghar sã ābait chhī) means “I come from home.” `हम बाजार जाइत छी।` (ham bājār jāit chhī) means “I go to the market.” A common destination can be unmarked in this motion construction. Do not add every English preposition mechanically.

`पोथी मेज पर अछि।` (pothī mej par achhi) means “The book is on the table.” `कलम कोठरी मे अछि।` locates a pen in a room. Compare `पर` and `मे`: one highlights a surface relation, and the other an interior or general location. The correct choice depends on how the objects are situated, not simply on their nouns.

`हम मित्र सँ बाजैत छी।` (ham mitra sã bājait chhī) means “I speak with a friend.” Here `सँ` connects a conversation partner, not a source of motion. This sentence also shows why a marker should be learned across several useful contexts instead of as a rigid translation equivalence.

### Common trap

Do not replace nasal `सँ` with unnasalised `स` or Hindi `से` in the written practice. The romanisation uses a precomposed nasal vowel to preserve the distinction. Also remember that `आबैत` and `जाइत` have different stems; a general verb-last pattern does not make all verbs morphologically identical.

### Practice

Choose the relation required by the meaning.

1. I come from home.
2. I go to the market.
3. The book is on the table.
4. I speak with a friend.

***Answers:***

1. `हम घर सँ आबैत छी।` - `Ham ghar sã ābait chhī.`
2. `हम बाजार जाइत छी।` - `Ham bājār jāit chhī.`
3. `पोथी मेज पर अछि।` - `Pothī mej par achhi.`
4. `हम मित्र सँ बाजैत छी।` - `Ham mitra sã bājait chhī.`

### Why these answers work

The first marks a source, the second uses a common bare destination, and the third describes a surface location. The fourth uses the same marker as the first for a different relation established by the speaking verb and human participant.

### Retrieve and recombine

Say that you come from the village, then locate a pen on the table. Finally, describe speaking with a teacher. Preserve the chosen person frame and change only the relation-bearing phrase.

## Lesson 11: Put an activity in time

### Outcome and a daily schedule

You will state when you study and ask when someone comes. `हम भोर मे पढ़ैत छी।` (ham bhor me paṛhait chhī) means “I study in the morning.” A time phrase can be placed before the action group, just as a place phrase can. The auxiliary still follows the action and reflects the speaker or respectful addressee.

### Think before reading on

Would adding “every day” to a reading sentence change who reads? No. `हम सभ दिन पढ़ैत छी।` (ham sabh din paṛhait chhī) means “I study every day.” The time phrase makes the habitual interpretation clear. The imperfective construction itself remains available for broader uses in other contexts.

### Time vocabulary

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| आइ | āi | today |
| एखन | ekhan | now |
| भोर | bhor | morning; dawn |
| राति | rāti | night |
| सभ दिन | sabh din | every day |
| कखन | kakhan | when |
| काल्हि | kālhi | yesterday or tomorrow, by context |
| साँझ | sãjh | evening |

### Questions about time

`अहाँ कखन आबैत छी?` (ahã kakhan ābait chhī) asks respectfully “When do you come?” A reply can use a time phrase: `हम भोर मे आबैत छी।` (ham bhor me ābait chhī), “I come in the morning.” The question word replaces the missing time, not the person or destination.

`हम राति नहि पढ़ैत छी।` (ham rāti nahi paṛhait chhī) means “I do not study at night.” This denies the activity in that period; it does not necessarily deny studying at other times. Time and negation together make a more precise habit than a bare “I do not study.”

`काल्हि` can refer to yesterday or tomorrow. The tense and the situation disambiguate it. This is different from Nepali, which has separate everyday words for those days. For now, recognise the ambiguity and avoid pretending that the time word alone supplies the direction. Later past and future forms will let you use it clearly in both senses.

The written sequence `आइ` is the word for today, not a typographical error for Hindi `आज`. Likewise, `भोर` and `साँझ` are useful ordinary time words with their own phonological shape. Familiar Indo-Aryan roots can help you remember them, but the actual Maithili forms remain the target.

### Common trap

Do not put a nonpast activity after “yesterday” and assume that the time word automatically repairs the tense. The selected imperfective can be broad in aspect, but it still combines with an auxiliary that locates it in time. A later past auxiliary changes that relation.

### Practice

State or ask about a habitual time.

1. I study in the morning.
2. I study every day.
3. When do you come? (respectful)
4. I do not study at night.

***Answers:***

1. `हम भोर मे पढ़ैत छी।` - `Ham bhor me paṛhait chhī.`
2. `हम सभ दिन पढ़ैत छी।` - `Ham sabh din paṛhait chhī.`
3. `अहाँ कखन आबैत छी?` - `Ahã kakhan ābait chhī?`
4. `हम राति नहि पढ़ैत छी।` - `Ham rāti nahi paṛhait chhī.`

### Why these answers work

Time information precedes the verb group. The first two clearly describe routines. The third asks for the missing time and maintains respectful address. The fourth limits a negative statement to nighttime, leaving other study times open.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe one morning habit and one evening habit. Ask a respected acquaintance when they come to the village. Finally, explain why `काल्हि` needs a tense or an established context before you can translate it confidently.

## Lesson 12: Invite someone to act politely

### Outcome and requests

You will invite someone to come or sit, and ask them to speak. Maithili polite imperatives often use forms in `उ`, including `बैसू` (baisū), “Please sit,” and `बाजू` (bājū), “Please speak.” These forms are requests or instructions, not descriptions of what the listener habitually does.

### Think before reading on

A visitor is standing beside a chair. Would `अहाँ बैसैत छी`, “You sit,” perform the same function as an invitation? For a direct polite invitation, choose `बैसू।` The addressee is understood. You do not have to pronounce `अहाँ` before every imperative to make it respectful.

### Useful imperative forms

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| बैसू | baisū | please sit |
| आउ | āu | please come |
| जाउ | jāu | please go |
| बाजू | bājū | please speak |
| सुनू | sunū | please listen |
| देखू | dekhū | please look/see |
| दिअ | dia | please give |
| लिअ | lia | please take |

### A request with an object

`पानि दिअ।` (pāni dia) means “Please give water,” with a recipient normally recoverable from context. `ई पोथी लिअ।` (ī pothī lia) means “Please take this book.” The object still precedes the requested action. You have changed the sentence's purpose without abandoning verb-last organisation.

`एतय बैसू।` (etay baisū) means “Please sit here.” `पोथी देखू।` (pothī dekhū) means “Please look at the book.” An English preposition such as “at” does not require an identical separate word in every Maithili expression. The verb and object construction carries the relation.

Polite forms are established word shapes. `दिअ` and `लिअ` show why you should not attach `उ` mechanically to every stem and assume you have produced a natural request. Likewise, `आउ` and `जाउ` should be recognised as complete forms. The examples use a respectful teaching register suitable for a first encounter, while real social relationships can support other imperative levels.

### Common trap

Do not replace Maithili requests with Hindi `बैठिए` or `दीजिए` while preserving only the surrounding nouns. Also remember that politeness is not solely a morphological ending: voice, timing, and the setting affect how a request is received. The course teaches the grammatical choice, not a guarantee of social appropriateness in every situation.

### Practice

Make the polite request taught in the lesson.

1. Please sit here.
2. Please give water.
3. Please take this book.
4. Please listen.

***Answers:***

1. `एतय बैसू।` - `Etay baisū.`
2. `पानि दिअ।` - `Pāni dia.`
3. `ई पोथी लिअ।` - `Ī pothī lia.`
4. `सुनू।` - `Sunū.`

### Why these answers work

The requested action comes last, and the addressee is understood. The first includes a location, the next two include an object, and the last is a complete one-word request. None needs an extra present auxiliary after the imperative.

### Retrieve and recombine

Welcome a visitor by asking them to come and sit. Then offer a book with “Please take this book.” Finally, ask them to look at a pen. Keep each request short and purposeful.

## Lesson 13: Repair a conversation you did not understand

### Outcome and communication repair

You will request repetition and slower speech. These are productive language tools, not phrases to save until an imagined advanced level. `फेर बाजू।` (pher bājū) means “Please speak again.” It combines a repeat adverb with the polite imperative from Lesson 12. You can reuse the same imperative with a manner expression.

### Think before reading on

If you recognise the topic but miss one fast sentence, is your problem lack of knowledge about the topic or inability to hear the words clearly? Ask for the specific repair you need. `धीरे बाजू।` (dhīre bājū), “Please speak slowly,” requests a change in delivery rather than a different subject.

### Repair vocabulary

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| फेर | pher | again |
| धीरे | dhīre | slowly |
| कहू | kahū | please say/tell |
| लिखू | likhū | please write |
| बुझैत | bujhait | understanding |
| जनैत | janait | knowing |
| मैथिली | maithilī | the Maithili language |
| अंग्रेजी | aṅgrejī | English |

### State what is missing

`हम नहि बुझैत छी।` (ham nahi bujhait chhī) means “I do not understand.” `हम नहि जनैत छी।` (ham nahi janait chhī) means “I do not know.” These differ: you may understand a question but not know its answer, or know the subject but fail to understand the wording. Choose the statement that actually describes your difficulty.

`ई लिखू।` (ī likhū) means “Please write this.” In a clear context, the demonstrative can refer to the word or information just mentioned. If the referent is unclear, point to it or ask for a specific item such as a name. This keeps the grammatical request simple without pretending context never matters.

`मैथिली मे बाजू।` (maithilī me bājū) means “Please speak in Maithili.” `अंग्रेजी मे कहू।` (aṅgrejī me kahū) asks “Please say it in English.” The same postposition `मे` that marks location also marks the language medium in this construction. It is a useful extension from a physical “in” to a communicative “in.”

### Common trap

Do not answer every difficult question with “I do not know” when you actually need it repeated. That response can close a conversation unnecessarily. Also keep Maithili `बुझैत` separate from nearby languages' forms: recognising a shared root does not tell you the correct inflection.

### Practice

Choose a repair line for the situation.

1. Please speak slowly.
2. Please say it again. (use kahū)
3. I do not understand.
4. Please speak in Maithili.

***Answers:***

1. `धीरे बाजू।` - `Dhīre bājū.`
2. `फेर कहू।` - `Pher kahū.`
3. `हम नहि बुझैत छी।` - `Ham nahi bujhait chhī.`
4. `मैथिली मे बाजू।` - `Maithilī me bājū.`

### Why these answers work

The first two change the manner or repetition of speech through imperatives. The third describes the learner's understanding using a first-person imperfective negative. The fourth specifies the language medium before the requested action. Each line solves a different communication problem.

### Retrieve and recombine

Imagine missing a person's name. Ask them to say it again, then write it. Next, ask for a slow Maithili explanation. Combine known pieces in short requests instead of memorising an entire fixed exchange.

## Lesson 14: Describe qualities without importing a gender table

### Outcome and description

You will describe a book, house, or person using a small set of adjectives. `पोथी नीक अछि।` (pothī nīk achhi) means “The book is good.” The quality appears before the final linking form. `नीक पोथी` (nīk pothī), by contrast, is the phrase “a good book,” without a complete assertion.

### Think before reading on

If the adjective “good” describes a book and then a house, must Maithili produce Hindi `अच्छी` and `अच्छा`? In the selected examples, `नीक` remains `नीक`. Maithili does not have Hindi's pervasive inanimate gender agreement system. That does not mean biological gender and all agreement distinctions disappear from the language.

### Qualities to recombine

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| नीक | nīk | good; well |
| पैघ | paigh | big |
| छोट | chhoṭ | small |
| नव | nav | new |
| पुरान | purān | old, not new |
| गरम | garam | hot |
| ठंढा | ṭhaṇḍhā | cold |
| सुन्नर | sunnar | beautiful |

### Place the adjective where its job belongs

`घर पैघ अछि।` (ghar paigh achhi) means “The house is big.” `ई नव पोथी थिक।` (ī nav pothī thik) means “This is a new book.” The second identifies an object through a noun phrase containing an adjective. It is not identical in structure to “The book is new,” although both convey related information.

`पानि गरम अछि।` (pāni garam achhi) means “The water is hot.” `बाबूजी नीक छथि।` (bābūjī nīk chhathi) says “Father is well,” respectfully. The adjective may remain unchanged while the linking form reflects respectful reference. This is an important separation between adjective shape and the honorific grammar of the sentence.

Many descriptions depend on context. `नीक` can describe quality or wellness; `पुरान` commonly contrasts with newness and is not automatically a considerate description of an elderly person. Vocabulary is not a set of interchangeable English labels. The noun and situation guide the meaning you should infer.

### Common trap

Do not invent feminine endings for every adjective because the English referent happens to be female. Maithili does show restricted gender agreement in parts of its verbal system, especially certain nonpresent honorific forms, but that does not create a Hindi-style rule for every adjective in the present lesson.

### Practice

Describe the quality or identify the modified noun.

2. The book is good.
3. The water is hot.
4. This is a new book.

***Answers:***

1. `घर पैघ अछि।` - `Ghar paigh achhi.`
2. `पोथी नीक अछि।` - `Pothī nīk achhi.`
3. `पानि गरम अछि।` - `Pāni garam achhi.`
4. `ई नव पोथी थिक।` - `Ī nav pothī thik.`

### Why these answers work

The first three predicate a quality with the linking verb. The last identifies a thing using a noun phrase that already contains “new.” No Hindi-style adjective agreement is introduced. The distinction between a phrase and a sentence remains visible in the placement of the linking form.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe a small house and a new pen, then identify one object as a good book. Finally, say respectfully that a relative is well. Track what stays constant in the adjective and what changes in the linking verb.

## Lesson 15: Count things with a classifier

### Outcome and quantities

You will request a small number of items using the classifier `टा`. `एकटा पोथी` (ekṭā pothī) means “one book” or “a book,” according to context. With a larger number, the count is explicit: `दू टा पोथी` (dū ṭā pothī), “two books.” The classifier is part of a noun phrase, not an action.

### Think before reading on

If English says “two books,” must you translate the plural ending separately after already stating two? The simple Maithili counting phrase does not require that. `दू टा पोथी` gives the quantity. Adding a plural marker automatically would stack information without first checking the language's actual usage.

### Small-number vocabulary

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| एक | ek | one |
| दू | dū | two |
| तीन | tīn | three |
| चारि | chāri | four |
| पाँच | pā̃ch | five |
| टा | ṭā | classifier in ordinary object phrases |
| एकटा | ekṭā | one; a, with a counted noun |
| कतेक | katek | how much; how many |

### Transfer a phrase into a request

`दू टा कलम दिअ।` (dū ṭā kalam dia) means “Please give two pens.” `एकटा पोथी लिअ।` (ekṭā pothī lia) means “Please take a book.” The request pattern is already familiar. Today adds a quantity phrase that you can place before its final imperative.

`तीन टा कापी` (tīn ṭā kāpī) means “three notebooks.” `चारि टा कलम` (chāri ṭā kalam) means “four pens.” The classifier's written spacing may vary in running Maithili text, especially in frequent combinations such as `एकटा`. This course joins that familiar one-item form and separates the others for clarity.

Maithili has other classifiers and person-counting conventions. The present lesson deliberately uses ordinary objects, where the selected `टा` pattern is useful. Do not extend it into a claim that every human, animal, mass noun, or measurement uses exactly the same construction. A small dependable counting pattern is more valuable than an inaccurate universal rule.

### Common trap

`दू` and `चारि` are the forms taught here; replacing them with a Hindi number automatically can conceal that you have stopped producing Maithili. Keep the retroflex `ṭ` in `टा` distinct from a dental t. Counting does not remove pronunciation contrasts.

### Practice

Give a count phrase or a request as specified.

1. Two books.
2. Three notebooks.
3. Please give two pens.
4. Please take one book.

***Answers:***

1. `दू टा पोथी` - `Dū ṭā pothī`
2. `तीन टा कापी` - `Tīn ṭā kāpī`
3. `दू टा कलम दिअ।` - `Dū ṭā kalam dia.`
4. `एकटा पोथी लिअ।` - `Ekṭā pothī lia.`

### Why these answers work

The first two are intentionally noun phrases. The final two combine a counted object with an established polite imperative. The number and classifier already provide the count, so no automatic English-style plural ending has been translated separately.

### Retrieve and recombine

Ask for four pens and three notebooks in two short requests. Then offer one book to a visitor. Explain why the quantity expression can be reused with different request verbs without changing its internal structure.

## Lesson 16: Refer to a group with सभ

### Outcome and plural reference

You will distinguish the individual speaker from a group including the speaker. `हम सभ` (ham sabh) means “we.” Earlier `हम` was introduced as “I,” so this plural expression makes the group explicit. `हम सभ विद्यार्थी छी।` (ham sabh vidyārthī chhī) means “We are students.” The selected verb does not acquire a new plural ending merely because more than one person is involved.

### Think before reading on

If “I am a student” and “we are students” both end in `छी`, where is the number distinction visible? It is in `सभ` and the broader noun phrase, not an English-style am/are contrast. Maithili person and honorific agreement should not be rewritten as a simple singular/plural table.

### Group expressions

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| सभ | sabh | all; plural marker by context |
| हम सभ | ham sabh | we |
| अहाँ सभ | ahã sabh | you all, respectful |
| पोथी सभ | pothī sabh | the books; books as a group |
| विद्यार्थी सभ | vidyārthī sabh | students as a group |
| एक संग | ek saṅg | together |
| एतय | etay | here |
| विद्यालय | vidyālaya | school |

### Group meaning and agreement

`हम सभ एतय छी।` (ham sabh etay chhī) means “We are here.” `हम सभ मैथिली पढ़ैत छी।` (ham sabh maithilī paṛhait chhī) means “We study Maithili.” You have changed the subject phrase while keeping the first-person agreement pattern. The group includes the speaker, which is what makes it first person.

`अहाँ सभ कतय छी?` (ahã sabh katay chhī) asks respectfully “Where are you all?” The plural addressee is explicit, while the same respectful address form remains. Do not conclude that `सभ` itself is a politeness marker; its role here is group reference.

With a noun, `पोथी सभ` refers to books as a plurality. The marker can also mean “all” in other constructions, including `सभ दिन`, “every day,” from Lesson 11. The interpretation depends on where it appears and what expression it forms. English likewise uses “all” in more than one grammatical way, so contextual reading is a useful transferable skill.

### Common trap

Do not assume that Maithili `हम` must always mean plural “we” because that is a familiar Hindi use. Conversely, do not claim Maithili has no way to mark plurality because the verb remains unchanged in these examples. Number can be represented in the noun phrase rather than in an English-like verb contrast.

### Practice

Make the group explicit with sabh.

1. We are students.
2. We are here.
3. We study Maithili.
4. Where are you all? (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `हम सभ विद्यार्थी छी।` - `Ham sabh vidyārthī chhī.`
2. `हम सभ एतय छी।` - `Ham sabh etay chhī.`
3. `हम सभ मैथिली पढ़ैत छी।` - `Ham sabh maithilī paṛhait chhī.`
4. `अहाँ सभ कतय छी?` - `Ahã sabh katay chhī?`

### Why these answers work

The noun phrase makes the plurality visible. The first three remain first person because the speaker is included. The fourth remains respectful second person. The selected auxiliary is shared across these frames, so participants must be interpreted from the complete sentence.

### Retrieve and recombine

Say that you study alone in one sentence and that you and friends study in another, using the singular and plural subject phrases. Then address the whole group respectfully and ask where they are.

## Lesson 17: Make an ongoing action explicit

### Outcome and progressive aspect

You will say that an activity is happening now. Maithili's imperfective forms can already have ongoing meanings in context, but a construction with `रहल` makes the progressive perspective explicit. `हम जा रहल छी।` (ham jā rahal chhī) means “I am going.” The action is viewed as in progress rather than simply described as a routine.

### Think before reading on

Does “I study every day” necessarily tell your listener that you are studying at this moment? No. Add the explicit progressive and the word “now”: `हम एखन पढ़ि रहल छी।` (ham ekhan paṛhi rahal chhī), “I am studying now.” The action's form and auxiliary work together.

### Progressive pieces

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| रहल | rahal | progressive element |
| जा रहल | jā rahal | going |
| पढ़ि रहल | paṛhi rahal | reading/studying |
| लिखि रहल | likhi rahal | writing |
| खा रहल | khā rahal | eating |
| कऽ रहल | ka rahal | doing |
| एखन | ekhan | now |
| चिट्ठी | chiṭṭhī | letter |

### Keep the auxiliary at the end

`हम चिट्ठी लिखि रहल छी।` (ham chiṭṭhī likhi rahal chhī) means “I am writing a letter.” `अहाँ की कऽ रहल छी?` (ahã kī ka rahal chhī) asks respectfully “What are you doing?” The reduced `कऽ` is a Maithili written form used here with the progressive; the avagraha sign belongs to the spelling and should not be silently discarded from the native text.

`बाबूजी पोथी पढ़ि रहल छथि।` (bābūjī pothī paṛhi rahal chhathi) means “Father is reading a book,” respectfully. The progressive element does not itself carry the final person and honorific information. That is visible in `छी` versus `छथि` at the end.

Spoken Maithili has vowel movement and stem alternations around progressive forms, and orthographic traditions differ in how transparently they represent them. The course uses the written forms in the table consistently. This is not the Hindi masculine/feminine `रहा/रही` pattern: do not replace `रहल` based on the speaker's gender in these present examples.

### Common trap

Do not append `रहल` after an already complete `पढ़ैत छी` sentence and call the result a progressive. Choose the construction as a whole. Also avoid treating every English “-ing” expression as progressive; “Reading is useful” names an activity and requires a different structure.

### Practice

Describe an action happening now.

1. I am going.
2. I am studying now.
3. I am writing a letter.
4. What are you doing? (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `हम जा रहल छी।` - `Ham jā rahal chhī.`
2. `हम एखन पढ़ि रहल छी।` - `Ham ekhan paṛhi rahal chhī.`
3. `हम चिट्ठी लिखि रहल छी।` - `Ham chiṭṭhī likhi rahal chhī.`
4. `अहाँ की कऽ रहल छी?` - `Ahã kī ka rahal chhī?`

### Why these answers work

The progressive element follows the action and precedes the auxiliary. The first three describe the speaker, while the fourth addresses a respected listener. The same final `छी` is appropriate in these selected frames, but the subject phrase still determines who is involved.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe reading a book at home right now, then state that you read every morning. Explain the difference in viewpoint. Finally, change the ongoing sentence into a respectful report about Father.

## Lesson 18: Describe where you went and where you were

### Outcome and past motion

You will describe a completed journey and a past location. `हम गेलहुँ।` (ham gelahũ) means “I went.” `हम घर मे छलहुँ।` (ham ghar me chhalahũ) means “I was at home.” These are different kinds of past statement: one reports movement, while the other gives a state at a past time.

### Think before reading on

If you were at home yesterday, does that necessarily mean you went home yesterday? No. You may already have been there. Choose the form that expresses the event you intend: `गेलहुँ` for going, `छलहुँ` for being. An English past tense alone does not identify the Maithili predicate you need.

### Past forms to recognise whole

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| गेलहुँ | gelahũ | I/respectful you went |
| एलहुँ | elahũ | I/respectful you came |
| छलहुँ | chhalahũ | I/respectful you was/were |
| गेलाह | gelāh | he went, honorific masculine |
| काल्हि | kālhi | yesterday here, with a past context |
| बाजार | bājār | market |
| गाम | gām | village |
| घर मे | ghar me | at home |

### Put the event on the past timeline

`हम काल्हि बाजार गेलहुँ।` (ham kālhi bājār gelahũ) means “I went to the market yesterday.” `अहाँ काल्हि कतय गेलहुँ?` (ahã kālhi katay gelahũ) asks respectfully “Where did you go yesterday?” The first-person and respectful second-person forms coincide in this selected past paradigm. The pronoun still supplies the difference in participant.

`हम काल्हि घर मे छलहुँ।` (ham kālhi ghar me chhalahũ) means “I was at home yesterday.” The past form disambiguates `काल्हि` toward yesterday. In a future construction later, the same time word can mean tomorrow. This is a useful example of sentence-level interpretation rather than isolated word substitution.

`बाबूजी गाम गेलाह।` (bābūjī gām gelāh) means “Father went to the village.” The honorific masculine label is deliberate. Maithili can show gender distinctions in third-person honorific nonpresent intransitives. Do not extend this masculine form uncritically to every female referent; our first-person and respectful-address examples avoid that unintroduced contrast.

### Common trap

Do not add Hindi `ने` or Nepali `ले` simply because the sentence is past. The motion examples here use plain `हम`. Also keep the final nasal vowel in `गेलहुँ` and `छलहुँ` visible. A spelling change can erase the chosen person form.

### Practice

Use the past motion or state construction.

1. I went to the market yesterday.
2. I came yesterday.
3. I was at home yesterday.
4. Where did you go yesterday? (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `हम काल्हि बाजार गेलहुँ।` - `Ham kālhi bājār gelahũ.`
2. `हम काल्हि एलहुँ।` - `Ham kālhi elahũ.`
3. `हम काल्हि घर मे छलहुँ।` - `Ham kālhi ghar me chhalahũ.`
4. `अहाँ काल्हि कतय गेलहुँ?` - `Ahã kālhi katay gelahũ?`

### Why these answers work

The first two report movement; the third reports a past location. The fourth asks for a destination in a completed journey. `काल्हि` is interpreted as yesterday because the surrounding grammar is past. The shared ending still requires attention to the subject pronoun.

### Retrieve and recombine

Say where you were yesterday, then where you went. Ask a respected listener the same destination question. Keep motion and state distinct instead of telling a story by changing only the place noun.

## Lesson 19: Report a completed action with an object

### Outcome and a short past report

You will say that you bought a book or completed some work. `हम पोथी किनलहुँ।` (ham pothī kinalahũ) means “I bought a book.” The first-person subject remains `हम`. Maithili does not require the Hindi or Nepali past agent marking that a multilingual learner might be tempted to insert here.

### Think before reading on

Does the presence of an object automatically make a Maithili past sentence begin with an ergative-marked pronoun? No. The selected first-person form can report the completed transitive action directly. Compare `हम बाजार गेलहुँ` with `हम पोथी किनलहुँ`: the verbs differ, but neither needs a borrowed Hindi `ने`.

### Completed actions with ordinary objects

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| किनलहुँ | kinalahũ | I bought |
| पढ़लहुँ | paṛhalahũ | I read/studied |
| लिखलहुँ | likhalahũ | I wrote |
| कएलहुँ | kaelahũ | I did |
| चिट्ठी | chiṭṭhī | letter |
| काज | kāj | work |
| पोथी | pothī | book |
| काल्हि | kālhi | yesterday, in these past sentences |

### Build the event from participants

`हम काल्हि पोथी पढ़लहुँ।` (ham kālhi pothī paṛhalahũ) means “I read a book yesterday.” `हम चिट्ठी लिखलहुँ।` (ham chiṭṭhī likhalahũ) means “I wrote a letter.” `हम काज कएलहुँ।` (ham kāj kaelahũ) means “I did the work.” The object precedes the completed action, as it preceded the imperfective action earlier.

This lesson deliberately uses first-person subjects with ordinary inanimate objects. Maithili has extensive agreement interactions involving other participants, especially pronominal objects and honorific reference. It would be inaccurate to turn this small set into a claim that every subject takes the same ending or that objects never matter for agreement.

The verb meaning “do” shows an altered past form `कएलहुँ`, rather than a form created by attaching the ending to an unchanged English-style “kar” stem. The same principle appeared in present `खाइत` and `पिबैत`: common verbs should be learned through a few useful contrasts before you try to infer wider morphological rules.

### Common trap

Do not copy a Hindi perfective and simply add `छी`, nor assume every completed action must be expressed through a present perfect construction. A simple past report and a result-relevant perfect can differ in form and use. Today teaches the simple past selected here, not all possible ways to describe completion.

### Practice

Report one completed action as the speaker.

1. I bought a book.
2. I read a book yesterday.
3. I wrote a letter.
4. I did the work.

***Answers:***

1. `हम पोथी किनलहुँ।` - `Ham pothī kinalahũ.`
2. `हम काल्हि पोथी पढ़लहुँ।` - `Ham kālhi pothī paṛhalahũ.`
3. `हम चिट्ठी लिखलहुँ।` - `Ham chiṭṭhī likhalahũ.`
4. `हम काज कएलहुँ।` - `Ham kāj kaelahũ.`

### Why these answers work

All four use the selected first-person past form with an ordinary object. The subject remains unmarked. The action's lexical stem still matters, especially in “did.” These examples establish a dependable production frame without pretending to describe every person and object combination.

### Retrieve and recombine

Tell a two-sentence story about going to the market and buying a pen. Next, say that you wrote a letter at home. Explain why you did not introduce Hindi or Nepali agent markers merely because the events are completed.

## Lesson 20: Make a future plan

### Outcome and future forms

You will state what you will do tomorrow. `हम काल्हि जाएब।` (ham kālhi jāeb) means “I will go tomorrow.” The future form disambiguates `काल्हि` toward tomorrow, just as the past form made it mean yesterday in Lesson 18. The time word and verb cooperate to establish the intended day.

### Think before reading on

If `हम काल्हि गेलहुँ` means a past trip, can the same verb stay unchanged when you intend tomorrow's trip? No. Replace the past with the future `जाएब`. A word that covers two adjacent days needs the surrounding grammar to clarify which direction you mean.

### A small future set

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| जाएब | jāeb | I/respectful you will go |
| आएब | āeb | I/respectful you will come |
| पढ़ब | paṛhab | I/respectful you will read/study |
| लिखब | likhab | I/respectful you will write |
| करब | karab | I/respectful you will do |
| किनब | kinab | I/respectful you will buy |
| कखन | kakhan | when |
| काल्हि | kālhi | tomorrow, with these future forms |

### Plan and question

`हम काल्हि पोथी पढ़ब।` (ham kālhi pothī paṛhab) means “I will read a book tomorrow.” `हम चिट्ठी लिखब।` (ham chiṭṭhī likhab) means “I will write a letter.” `अहाँ कखन आएब?` (ahã kakhan āeb) asks respectfully “When will you come?” The future verb stands as the finite predicate; it does not need the present auxiliary `छी` added after it in these sentences.

`हम काल्हि काज नहि करब।` (ham kālhi kāj nahi karab) means “I will not work tomorrow.” The negative stays before the action in this neutral pattern. Compare it with `हम काज नहि करैत छी`, which denies a present routine. The object is the same, but the temporal and aspectual claim differs.

The first-person and respectful second-person future forms coincide in this selected frame. Third-person forms and forms involving other agreement participants require further distinctions. A future ending is not a universal suffix you can attach to every pronoun without examining the relationship and the verb.

### Common trap

Do not build an English-style independent “will” before a present Maithili verb. The future information is expressed in the verb form here. Also distinguish `जाएब`, “will go,” from the polite imperative `जाउ`, “please go”: a request and a prediction or commitment are different speech acts.

### Practice

State a future plan or ask about one.

1. I will go tomorrow.
2. I will read a book tomorrow.
3. When will you come? (respectful)
4. I will not work tomorrow.

***Answers:***

1. `हम काल्हि जाएब।` - `Ham kālhi jāeb.`
2. `हम काल्हि पोथी पढ़ब।` - `Ham kālhi pothī paṛhab.`
3. `अहाँ कखन आएब?` - `Ahã kakhan āeb?`
4. `हम काल्हि काज नहि करब।` - `Ham kālhi kāj nahi karab.`

### Why these answers work

The future forms establish the forward-looking time. The first two retain ordinary verb-last order. The third asks for a missing time with respectful address. The fourth negates a future action rather than a present habit, so its final form differs from the earlier imperfective negative.

### Retrieve and recombine

State a plan for tomorrow, then make a related negative plan. Finally, contrast “I went yesterday” with “I will go tomorrow,” using the same time word and explaining how the verb resolves its meaning.

## Lesson 21: Separate wanting from being able

### Outcome and two kinds of possibility

You will say that you want to go somewhere and that you can go. `हम जाए चाहैत छी।` (ham jāe chāhait chhī) means “I want to go.” `हम जाए सकैत छी।` (ham jāe sakait chhī) means “I can go.” The first reports a desire; the second reports ability or practical possibility. They are not interchangeable promises.

### Think before reading on

Can you want to visit a friend but be unable to travel? If so, your sentence needs to keep desire and ability distinct. The action `जाए` appears before the following predicate. It is not the finite future `जाएब`, so do not retain the future ending and then append another full verb group.

### Useful modal pieces

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| जाए | jāe | to go, in this complement pattern |
| आए | āe | to come, in this complement pattern |
| चाहैत | chāhait | wanting |
| सकैत | sakait | being able |
| एखन | ekhan | now |
| काल्हि | kālhi | tomorrow in the planned context |
| गाम | gām | village |
| बाजार | bājār | market |

### A destination inside the desired action

`हम गाम जाए चाहैत छी।` (ham gām jāe chāhait chhī) means “I want to go to the village.” `हम एखन जाए सकैत छी।` (ham ekhan jāe sakait chhī) means “I can go now.” The destination or time belongs to the proposed action, while the final verb group says whether that action is desired or possible.

`हम एखन नहि जाए सकैत छी।` (ham ekhan nahi jāe sakait chhī) means “I cannot go now.” This need not deny all future possibility. The time word limits the statement. `अहाँ जाए सकैत छी?` (ahã jāe sakait chhī) asks respectfully “Can you go?” It is a question about the addressee's ability or practical possibility, not automatically an imperative.

Maithili modal constructions use nonfinite complements whose forms depend on the verb and construction. This lesson teaches the clear motion complements `जाए` and `आए` rather than claiming every verb is converted by attaching one universal ending. Once you know a new complement from a dependable source, it can be inserted into the same larger meaning pattern.

### Common trap

Do not confuse “I will go” with “I want to go.” A wish is not the same as a firm plan, and ability is not the same as either. Also avoid inventing a Hindi-style infinitive with `ना` simply because English translates the complement with “to.”

### Practice

Express desire or ability as specified.

1. I want to go.
2. I want to go to the village.
3. I can go now.
4. Can you go? (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `हम जाए चाहैत छी।` - `Ham jāe chāhait chhī.`
2. `हम गाम जाए चाहैत छी।` - `Ham gām jāe chāhait chhī.`
3. `हम एखन जाए सकैत छी।` - `Ham ekhan jāe sakait chhī.`
4. `अहाँ जाए सकैत छी?` - `Ahã jāe sakait chhī?`

### Why these answers work

The nonfinite motion complement precedes the predicate of wanting or ability. The first two express a desire, the third an available possibility, and the fourth asks about that possibility respectfully. The future finite ending is absent because another verb group supplies the clause's finiteness.

### Retrieve and recombine

Say you want to go to the market, but then state in a separate sentence that you cannot go now. Offer a future plan for tomorrow. Explain how the three statements differ in commitment and possibility.

## Lesson 22: Compare two objects and connect a contrast

### Outcome and comparison

You will compare two houses or books and connect two contrasting clauses. Maithili can use `सँ` to mark a comparison standard. `ई घर ओहि घर सँ पैघ अछि।` (ī ghar ohi ghar sã paigh achhi) means “This house is bigger than that house.” The adjective does not acquire an English comparative “-er” ending.

### Think before reading on

You have learned `सँ` as source or accompaniment. Does that guarantee “from” or “with” in every new sentence? Here the descriptive predicate and two comparable objects create the comparison reading. A postposition's meaning emerges from the construction, not from a single English word memorised beside it.

### Comparing and connecting

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| एहि | ehi | this, oblique form before a following relation |
| ओहि | ohi | that, oblique form before a following relation |
| सँ | sã | than, in a comparison |
| पैघ | paigh | big |
| छोट | chhoṭ | small |
| नीक | nīk | good |
| आ | ā | and |
| मुदा | mudā | but |

### A comparison is a structured relation

`ई पोथी ओहि पोथी सँ नीक अछि।` (ī pothī ohi pothī sã nīk achhi) means “This book is better than that book.” The comparison standard is the book marked with `सँ`. `ओहि` is the oblique demonstrative form used in that marked phrase. Do not preserve the unmarked `ओ` mechanically in every grammatical position.

`हम पढ़ैत छी आ काज करैत छी।` (ham paṛhait chhī ā kāj karait chhī) means “I study and work.” The second clause shares the speaker as an understood subject. `हम पढ़ैत छी, मुदा चाह नहि पिबैत छी।` (ham paṛhait chhī, mudā chāh nahi pibait chhī) means “I study, but I do not drink tea,” in a context where tea drinking was expected during study. A conjunction can be grammatically correct while its relevance still depends on the situation.

A more direct contrast is `ई घर पैघ अछि, मुदा ओ घर छोट अछि।` (ī ghar paigh achhi, mudā o ghar chhoṭ achhi), “This house is big, but that house is small.” Both clauses retain their own complete descriptive structure. You can understand each before joining them.

### Common trap

Do not create a new comparative adjective by importing a Hindi or English suffix. Also distinguish the plain demonstrative `ओ` from the oblique `ओहि` in a marked comparison phrase. Pronoun and demonstrative forms can change even when the English translation continues to say “that.”

### Practice

Compare or connect the clauses requested.

1. This house is bigger than that house.
2. This book is better than that book.
3. I study and work.
4. This house is big, but that house is small.

***Answers:***

1. `ई घर ओहि घर सँ पैघ अछि।` - `Ī ghar ohi ghar sã paigh achhi.`
2. `ई पोथी ओहि पोथी सँ नीक अछि।` - `Ī pothī ohi pothī sã nīk achhi.`
3. `हम पढ़ैत छी आ काज करैत छी।` - `Ham paṛhait chhī ā kāj karait chhī.`
4. `ई घर पैघ अछि, मुदा ओ घर छोट अछि।` - `Ī ghar paigh achhi, mudā o ghar chhoṭ achhi.`

### Why these answers work

The first two mark a comparison standard and retain an ordinary adjective. The third links compatible actions with a shared subject. The fourth makes a contrast between two complete descriptions. Each clause's internal grammar remains intact after connection.

### Retrieve and recombine

Compare two rooms for size, then connect a positive and negative routine with “but.” Before speaking, check whether you need a comparison standard marked with `सँ` or two independent clauses joined by a conjunction.

## Lesson 23: Make a simple if-then arrangement

### Outcome and a condition

You will make a future plan conditional on another event. Maithili uses `जँ` (jã), “if,” with a following result often introduced by `तऽ` (ta). `जँ अहाँ आएब, तऽ हम पढ़ब।` (jã ahã āeb, ta ham paṛhab) means “If you come, then I will study.” The first clause states a condition, not a claim that the visit is certain.

### Think before reading on

If someone says “If you come,” have they said that you definitely will come? No. The condition sets a possible situation in which the second statement applies. Keep the conditional relationship separate from an ordinary sequence such as “You will come, and I will study.” The two sentences make different commitments.

### Condition and result vocabulary

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| जँ | jã | if |
| तऽ | ta | then, result or discourse particle |
| आएब | āeb | will come, first/respectful second person |
| जाएब | jāeb | will go, first/respectful second person |
| पढ़ब | paṛhab | will read/study |
| करब | karab | will do |
| एतय | etay | here |
| काल्हि | kālhi | tomorrow, in this future context |

### A condition using a present state

`जँ अहाँ एतय छी, तऽ हम आएब।` (jã ahã etay chhī, ta ham āeb) means “If you are here, then I will come.” The condition uses a present state, while the result uses a future action. A conditional sentence does not force every clause to use an identical tense form.

`जँ अहाँ काल्हि आएब, तऽ हम घर मे रहब।` (jã ahã kālhi āeb, ta ham ghar me rahab) means “If you come tomorrow, then I will stay at home.” The new form `रहब` (rahab), “I will stay/remain,” follows the first-person future pattern and is useful for arranging availability. It is not interchangeable with “I will go home,” which would describe motion.

These are ordinary open conditions about plausible plans. They do not teach counterfactual past meanings such as “If you had come, I would have stayed.” Maithili has richer conditional and mood constructions for those meanings. Keeping the scope clear lets you produce a useful arrangement without overclaiming that one particle solves every English “if” sentence.

### Common trap

Retain the nasal sign in `जँ`; the word is not simply an unmarked “ja.” Preserve the avagraha in native `तऽ` as well, even though the reading aid writes `ta`. Also avoid an English rule that future forms can never appear after “if”: the Maithili clause has its own grammatical possibilities.

### Practice

Build an open condition and result.

1. If you come, then I will study. (respectful)
2. If you are here, then I will come. (respectful)
3. I will stay at home.
4. If you come tomorrow, then I will stay at home. (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `जँ अहाँ आएब, तऽ हम पढ़ब।` - `Jã ahã āeb, ta ham paṛhab.`
2. `जँ अहाँ एतय छी, तऽ हम आएब।` - `Jã ahã etay chhī, ta ham āeb.`
3. `हम घर मे रहब।` - `Ham ghar me rahab.`
4. `जँ अहाँ काल्हि आएब, तऽ हम घर मे रहब।` - `Jã ahã kālhi āeb, ta ham ghar me rahab.`

### Why these answers work

The conditional marker introduces the proposed circumstance, and the result particle leads to the consequent plan. The second answer demonstrates a present condition with a future result. The third isolates the stay-at-home statement so you can reuse it without always making it conditional.

### Retrieve and recombine

Arrange a visit contingent on the other person being at home. Then state one unconditional plan for tomorrow. Explain why the conditional version leaves part of the situation unsettled.

## Lesson 24: Rebuild a visit from useful exchanges

### Outcome and conversation management

You will greet a visitor, ask about them, offer a seat, and respond when you miss a word. The task is choosing the right known construction at the right moment. A conversation is not one long sentence, and you do not need an unfamiliar tense to make a short encounter useful.

### Think before reading on

A visitor introduces themselves too quickly. Should you guess the name, or use a repair request you know? Ask `फेर कहू।` Then, if needed, ask for the name in writing. Communication succeeds when you obtain the missing information, not when you finish a memorised script regardless of understanding.

### Conversation pieces

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| प्रणाम | praṇām | respectful greeting |
| एतय बैसू | etay baisū | please sit here |
| अहाँक नाम | ahãk nām | your name, respectful |
| चाह | chāh | tea |
| लेब | leb | will take, first/respectful second person |
| धन्यवाद | dhanyavād | thank you |
| फेर कहू | pher kahū | please say it again |
| धीरे बाजू | dhīre bājū | please speak slowly |

### A short exchange with replaceable parts

Host: `प्रणाम। एतय बैसू।` (praṇām; etay baisū), “Hello. Please sit here.” Visitor: `धन्यवाद।` (dhanyavād), “Thank you.” Host: `अहाँक नाम की अछि?` (ahãk nām kī achhi), “What is your name?” Visitor: `हमर नाम रीता अछि।` (hamar nām rītā achhi), “My name is Rita.”

The host can offer tea with `अहाँ चाह लेब?` (ahã chāh leb), “Will you take tea?” This uses the respectful future of taking in an offer. A clear reply is `हम चाह लेब।` (ham chāh leb), “I will take tea,” or `हम चाह नहि लेब।` (ham chāh nahi leb), “I will not take tea.” The social tone of a refusal depends on delivery and context; the grammar supplies the choice rather than a universal etiquette formula.

If the visitor asks about your activity, `हम मैथिली पढ़ैत छी` explains that you study Maithili. If you cannot follow the reply, `हम नहि बुझैत छी` states the problem. These are ordinary first-person forms, so you can move from hospitality to learning without a new grammatical system.

### Common trap

Do not assume that a polite future offer is the same speech act as an imperative. `चाह लिअ` invites someone to take tea directly; `अहाँ चाह लेब?` asks about their choice. Both can be useful, but the listener's expected response differs.

### Practice

Supply one line appropriate to the situation.

1. Ask a visitor to sit here.
2. Ask the visitor's name respectfully.
3. Offer tea using a respectful future question.
4. Say that you will not take tea.

***Answers:***

1. `एतय बैसू।` - `Etay baisū.`
2. `अहाँक नाम की अछि?` - `Ahãk nām kī achhi?`
3. `अहाँ चाह लेब?` - `Ahã chāh leb?`
4. `हम चाह नहि लेब।` - `Ham chāh nahi leb.`

### Why these answers work

The first is an imperative, the second an information question, the third an offer framed through the future, and the fourth a negative future choice. Their meanings come from both grammar and the practical context of a visit.

### Retrieve and recombine

Repeat the exchange with a different name and milk instead of tea. Add one question about where the visitor comes from, then use a repair request if the imagined response is unclear. Preserve respectful address throughout.

## Lesson 25: Tell a small story and ask a real question

### Outcome and cumulative construction

You will connect yesterday's event, today's activity, and tomorrow's plan. The goal is to select forms by meaning, participant, and relationship. This is not a claim that the course covers Maithili's whole agreement system. It gives you a set of dependable starting frames that can be extended through reading, listening, and fluent-speaker feedback.

### Think before reading on

You went to the market, bought a book, are reading it now, and will study tomorrow. Which verb belongs to each moment? Try building the four clauses before checking the account below. A time word alone cannot turn a present routine into every kind of past or future statement.

### Your construction map

| Maithili | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| हम छी | ham chhī | I am |
| हमरा लग अछि | hamrā lag achhi | I have it |
| हम पढ़ैत छी | ham paṛhait chhī | I read/study |
| हम पढ़ि रहल छी | ham paṛhi rahal chhī | I am reading/studying |
| हम गेलहुँ | ham gelahũ | I went |
| हम किनलहुँ | ham kinalahũ | I bought |
| हम पढ़ब | ham paṛhab | I will read/study |
| अहाँ कखन आएब | ahã kakhan āeb | when will you come, respectful |

### A worked account

`हम काल्हि बाजार गेलहुँ।` (ham kālhi bājār gelahũ), “I went to the market yesterday.” `हम पोथी किनलहुँ।` (ham pothī kinalahũ), “I bought a book.” `हम एखन पोथी पढ़ि रहल छी।` (ham ekhan pothī paṛhi rahal chhī), “I am reading a book now.” `हम काल्हि मैथिली पढ़ब।` (ham kālhi maithilī paṛhab), “I will study Maithili tomorrow.”

The same `काल्हि` is interpreted differently in the first and fourth sentences because the verbs establish opposite temporal directions. The completed transitive action does not import a Hindi agent marker. The progressive uses its own action-plus-`रहल` frame, and the future stands without an added present auxiliary.

Now invite the other person into the conversation: `अहाँ काल्हि कतय गेलहुँ?` (ahã kālhi katay gelahũ), “Where did you go yesterday?” A short answer may be enough; if it contains an unfamiliar word, ask for repetition. The useful outcome is an exchange where you understand the participant roles and time, not an uninterrupted recital.

### Common trap

Do not assume that because the first-person examples are stable, every third-person or two-person event will use the same endings. Maithili's multiple-participant agreement and honorific distinctions remain important beyond this foundation. A gender or respect distinction absent in one present pattern may matter in a later past or future form.

### Practice

Recombine the course patterns with the changed details.

1. I went to the village yesterday.
2. I bought a pen.
3. I am reading a book now.
4. When will you come tomorrow? (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `हम काल्हि गाम गेलहुँ।` - `Ham kālhi gām gelahũ.`
2. `हम कलम किनलहुँ।` - `Ham kalam kinalahũ.`
3. `हम एखन पोथी पढ़ि रहल छी।` - `Ham ekhan pothī paṛhi rahal chhī.`
4. `अहाँ काल्हि कखन आएब?` - `Ahã kālhi kakhan āeb?`

### Why these answers work

The first two use completed past forms but different lexical actions. The third explicitly presents ongoing reading. The last asks for a time within tomorrow's planned visit and maintains respectful address. The questions and answers transfer familiar grammar into new details rather than copying the worked story unchanged.

### Retrieve and recombine

Make an original four-sentence account with an identity or location, an available possession, a past event, and a future plan. Add one respectful question and one repair request. Identify any form you cannot explain, then return to its lesson. Continue with authentic listening and speaker review; completing this draft does not certify proficiency.
