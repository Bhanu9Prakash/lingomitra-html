# Telugu: A pattern-first foundation

This course uses Telugu script and a broadly shared modern educated conversational Telugu, with conventional spelling. Andhra Pradesh and Telangana contain important regional varieties; literary Telugu uses additional forms not presented as everyday speech here. Romanisation marks long vowels ā ī ū ē ō and retroflex ṭ ḍ ṇ ḷ. Double consonants last longer; c sounds approximately like English ch. The spelling ñc and ṅk indicates the nasal before the following consonant. Romanisation supports reading but does not replace the Telugu alphabet.

## Lesson 1: Identity without an English copula

### Build the pattern

You will introduce yourself and identify a nearby object. Telugu normally puts the identifying description after the person or thing. In simple present identity statements, English am or is often has no separate equivalent. Start with నేను nēnu, I, and a name. This construction does not require a verb ending that changes with the speaker's gender. For objects, ఇది idi points to something nearby, while అది adi points farther away or back to something already mentioned. Neither word is an article meaning a or the. Telugu does not require an indefinite article before every count noun. Native writing preserves vowels that English readers may be tempted to swallow.

### Think before reading on

If నేను nēnu means I and రవి Ravi is a name, what would carry the meaning am in an introduction?

Nothing additional is needed in this pattern. Put the name after the pronoun. The relationship between the two parts supplies identification. This does not mean that Telugu never uses a verb for being: existence and location will have their own construction.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| నేను రవి. | Nēnu Ravi. | I am Ravi. |
| నేను సీత. | Nēnu Sīta. | I am Sita. |
| ఇది పుస్తకం. | Idi pustakaṁ. | This is a book. |
| అది ఇల్లు. | Adi illu. | That is a house. |
| ఇది నీరు. | Idi nīru. | This is water. |
| అది బడి. | Adi baḍi. | That is a school. |

### Worked examples

`నేను రవి.` - `Nēnu Ravi.` means “I am Ravi.” The speaker supplies a name directly; there is no missing word that must be pronounced between pronoun and name.

`నేను సీత.` - `Nēnu Sīta.` means “I am Sita.” A different speaker name leaves the basic sentence frame intact, without importing masculine or feminine Hindi copula patterns.

`ఇది పుస్తకం.` - `Idi pustakaṁ.` means “This is a book.” The object is identified by its category, and the absence of an article is ordinary Telugu rather than telegraphic English.

### Common trap

Do not add ఉన్నాను unnānu automatically after every identity noun. That form is useful for being somewhere or in a state, but it is not an English am that must accompany every introduction.

### Practice

Build these meanings in Telugu. Keep the stated person and register.

1. I am Ravi.
2. I am Sita.
3. This is a book.
4. That is a house.
5. This is water.
6. That is a school.

***Answers:***

1. `నేను రవి.` - `Nēnu Ravi.`
2. `నేను సీత.` - `Nēnu Sīta.`
3. `ఇది పుస్తకం.` - `Idi pustakaṁ.`
4. `అది ఇల్లు.` - `Adi illu.`
5. `ఇది నీరు.` - `Idi nīru.`
6. `అది బడి.` - `Adi baḍi.`

### Why these answers work

Answer 4: The distant demonstrative changes the reference, while illu retains its doubled consonant and should not be reduced to ilu.

Answer 5: The long vowel in nīru belongs to the word; the demonstrative identifies the substance without an English article.

Answer 6: The retroflex ḍ is made farther back than English d, and the sentence identifies a place rather than locating someone.

### Retrieve and recombine

Introduce yourself, point to a book, then identify a distant house. Change the pointing word while preserving the noun, and notice that distance changes without changing the object.


## Lesson 2: Questions that keep the sentence shape

### Build the pattern

You will ask what something is and whether an identification is correct. Telugu information questions normally keep the question word in the position of the missing information. ఏంటి ēṇṭi is a frequent conversational what; ఏమిటి ēmiṭi is a fuller standard form. Here we use ఏంటి consistently. For a yes-or-no identity question, the interrogative vowel ā can attach to the final identifying word. The ending may change the written shape of the noun. A question mark represents intonation, but the grammatical question ending is audible. Use అవును avunu for yes when confirming a proposition. కాదు kādu means is not in the identity construction.

### Think before reading on

Compare ఇది పుస్తకం idi pustakaṁ with ఇది పుస్తకమా idi pustakamā. Which part tells the listener that you are checking your guess?

The final ā turns the identification into a yes-or-no question. For an open question, ఏంటి replaces the unknown category instead. These are different requests for information: one proposes an answer, while the other asks the listener to supply it.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ఇది ఏంటి? | Idi ēṇṭi? | What is this? |
| అది ఏంటి? | Adi ēṇṭi? | What is that? |
| ఇది పుస్తకమా? | Idi pustakamā? | Is this a book? |
| అది పుస్తకమా? | Adi pustakamā? | Is that a book? |
| అవును. | Avunu. | Yes. |
| కాదు. | Kādu. | No, it is not. |

### Worked examples

`ఇది ఏంటి?` - `Idi ēṇṭi?` means “What is this?” The unknown identification follows the demonstrative, so the question word occupies the same position that pustakaṁ occupied earlier.

`అది ఏంటి?` - `Adi ēṇṭi?` means “What is that?” Only the demonstrative changes; the farther object does not require a different question word or an added verb.

`ఇది పుస్తకమా?` - `Idi pustakamā?` means “Is this a book?” The noun ends in the interrogative vowel after its linking m, creating a spoken question rather than punctuation alone.

### Common trap

Do not put an English-style do before the subject. Telugu has no question-support word matching do here. Also avoid translating every English no as లేదు; identification uses కాదు.

### Practice

Build these meanings in Telugu. Keep the stated person and register.

1. What is this?
2. What is that?
3. Is this a book?
4. Is that a book?
5. Yes.
6. No, it is not.

***Answers:***

1. `ఇది ఏంటి?` - `Idi ēṇṭi?`
2. `అది ఏంటి?` - `Adi ēṇṭi?`
3. `ఇది పుస్తకమా?` - `Idi pustakamā?`
4. `అది పుస్తకమా?` - `Adi pustakamā?`
5. `అవును.` - `Avunu.`
6. `కాదు.` - `Kādu.`

### Why these answers work

Answer 4: The distant demonstrative changes which object is being discussed, while the same interrogative noun form checks its identification as a book.

Answer 5: This confirms the proposed identification; in a fuller answer you can repeat the affirmative statement after the confirmation.

Answer 6: The short reply rejects identity and is not the ordinary negative existential used for saying that something is absent.

### Retrieve and recombine

Imagine an unfamiliar object on a desk. Ask what it is, offer a book as your guess, and reject that guess. Use the same pointing word throughout this exchange.


## Lesson 3: People, respect and names

### Build the pattern

You will ask a person's name respectfully and distinguish informal from respectful address. మీరు mīru can refer to several people or to one person respectfully. నువ్వు nuvvu addresses one familiar person and is not the safest default with strangers. The possessive forms are మీ mī, your respectfully or plurally, and నీ nī, your familiarly. నా nā means my. Telugu commonly asks the name as your name what, not with an English verb do. పేరు pēru means name. A person's name followed by గారు gāru is respectful; it is a social title rather than part of the person's personal name.

### Think before reading on

If మీరు mīru is respectful you, should your name begin with మీరు or with a different form?

Possession uses మీ mī. Telugu distinguishes the subject form from the possessive form, just as English distinguishes I from my. Keeping these forms separate makes later phrases such as your house and your book immediately available.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| మీ పేరు ఏంటి? | Mī pēru ēṇṭi? | What is your name? (respectful) |
| నా పేరు రవి. | Nā pēru Ravi. | My name is Ravi. |
| మీరు రవిగారా? | Mīru Ravigārā? | Are you Mr Ravi? |
| నీ పేరు ఏంటి? | Nī pēru ēṇṭi? | What is your name? (familiar) |
| ఇది మీ పుస్తకమా? | Idi mī pustakamā? | Is this your book? (respectful) |
| ఇది నా పుస్తకం. | Idi nā pustakaṁ. | This is my book. |

### Worked examples

`మీ పేరు ఏంటి?` - `Mī pēru ēṇṭi?` means “What is your name? (respectful)” The possessive mī modifies pēru, while the final question word asks for the name rather than the person's identity category.

`నా పేరు రవి.` - `Nā pēru Ravi.` means “My name is Ravi.” The name phrase functions as the subject of an identity statement, so no separate present copula is required.

`మీరు రవిగారా?` - `Mīru Ravigārā?` means “Are you Mr Ravi?” The respectful title receives the question ending, and mīru agrees socially with the respectful form of address.

### Common trap

Do not use nuvvu merely because English uses one you for everybody. Do not attach gāru to your own name as a routine self-introduction; honorific reference is directed toward others.

### Practice

Build these meanings in Telugu. Keep the stated person and register.

1. What is your name? (respectful)
2. My name is Ravi.
3. Are you Mr Ravi?
4. What is your name? (familiar)
5. Is this your book? (respectful)
6. This is my book.

***Answers:***

1. `మీ పేరు ఏంటి?` - `Mī pēru ēṇṭi?`
2. `నా పేరు రవి.` - `Nā pēru Ravi.`
3. `మీరు రవిగారా?` - `Mīru Ravigārā?`
4. `నీ పేరు ఏంటి?` - `Nī pēru ēṇṭi?`
5. `ఇది మీ పుస్తకమా?` - `Idi mī pustakamā?`
6. `ఇది నా పుస్తకం.` - `Idi nā pustakaṁ.`

### Why these answers work

Answer 4: The grammar remains the same but nī explicitly marks familiar singular address, appropriate only where that relationship is established.

Answer 5: The question ending attaches to the noun, not the possessive; mī can refer to one respected owner or several owners.

Answer 6: Ownership is expressed before the noun, and the same identity frame now identifies whose book the object is.

### Retrieve and recombine

Ask an unfamiliar visitor their name, give your own name, and ask whether a nearby book belongs to them. Retain respectful address even when the sentence becomes shorter.


## Lesson 4: Where people and things are

### Build the pattern

You will locate yourself, a man, a woman and an object. Telugu uses forms of ఉండు uṇḍu for existence and location. నేను pairs with ఉన్నాను unnānu; అతను atanu, he, with ఉన్నాడు unnāḍu; ఆమె āme, she, with ఉంది undi. A nonhuman singular also takes ఉంది. Respectful మీరు takes ఉన్నారు unnāru. This is not a claim that women are grammatically objects: Telugu's singular agreement groups differ from English gender labels. ఇక్కడ ikkaḍa means here, అక్కడ akkaḍa there, and ఎక్కడ ekkaḍa where. The place expression typically precedes the final verb.

### Think before reading on

Would replacing he with she leave ఉన్నాడు unnāḍu unchanged in a neutral singular location statement?

No. The ordinary forms here are అతను ఉన్నాడు and ఆమె ఉంది. In respectful reference, human honorific agreement can use ఉన్నారు. Learn these contrasts with their pronouns instead of assuming a single third-person ending covers every subject.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| నేను ఇక్కడ ఉన్నాను. | Nēnu ikkaḍa unnānu. | I am here. |
| అతను అక్కడ ఉన్నాడు. | Atanu akkaḍa unnāḍu. | He is there. |
| ఆమె ఇక్కడ ఉంది. | Āme ikkaḍa undi. | She is here. |
| పుస్తకం అక్కడ ఉంది. | Pustakaṁ akkaḍa undi. | The book is there. |
| మీరు ఎక్కడ ఉన్నారు? | Mīru ekkaḍa unnāru? | Where are you? (respectful) |
| నేను బాగున్నాను. | Nēnu bāgunnānu. | I am well. |

### Worked examples

`నేను ఇక్కడ ఉన్నాను.` - `Nēnu ikkaḍa unnānu.` means “I am here.” The location sits before a first-person finite verb, which makes the speaker recoverable even when conversation omits the pronoun.

`అతను అక్కడ ఉన్నాడు.` - `Atanu akkaḍa unnāḍu.` means “He is there.” The masculine singular ending marks the referent as he in this nonhonorific example, independently of the distant place word.

`ఆమె ఇక్కడ ఉంది.` - `Āme ikkaḍa undi.` means “She is here.” The ordinary feminine singular agreement form is undi; changing the place word would not change that agreement requirement.

### Common trap

Do not use undi for every English is, or unnāḍu for every human. Identity without a copula, location, and respectful reference involve different choices that English hides.

### Practice

Build these meanings in Telugu. Keep the stated person and register.

1. I am here.
2. He is there.
3. She is here.
4. The book is there.
5. Where are you? (respectful)
6. I am well.

***Answers:***

1. `నేను ఇక్కడ ఉన్నాను.` - `Nēnu ikkaḍa unnānu.`
2. `అతను అక్కడ ఉన్నాడు.` - `Atanu akkaḍa unnāḍu.`
3. `ఆమె ఇక్కడ ఉంది.` - `Āme ikkaḍa undi.`
4. `పుస్తకం అక్కడ ఉంది.` - `Pustakaṁ akkaḍa undi.`
5. `మీరు ఎక్కడ ఉన్నారు?` - `Mīru ekkaḍa unnāru?`
6. `నేను బాగున్నాను.` - `Nēnu bāgunnānu.`

### Why these answers work

Answer 4: The nonhuman singular noun also selects undi, while the context supplies whether English would say a book or the book.

Answer 5: The question word asks for location without moving the finite verb away from its usual final position in the sentence.

Answer 6: The contracted expression combines bāgā, well, with unnānu; it describes a state rather than identifying a name or profession.

### Retrieve and recombine

Locate yourself here, locate a book there, then ask a respected visitor where they are. Explain which final word changes because of the subject rather than the place.


## Lesson 5: Absence and not being something

### Build the pattern

You will distinguish negative identity from absence. కాదు kādu rejects a classification: this is not a book. లేదు lēdu says something is absent or does not exist in the relevant place. First-person absence uses లేను lēnu, and respectful second-person absence uses లేరు lēru. Telugu therefore does not have a single invariant word that replaces every English not. The positive construction predicts the negative family. If the positive is an identity statement without a verb, expect kādu; if it contains an existential form such as undi, expect the corresponding negative existential. Context can supply the place, but explicit place words make the contrast easier to hear.

### Think before reading on

How would you distinguish that is not water from there is no water, although both sentences deny something about water?

The first rejects the identity of a visible substance and uses kādu. The second reports absence and uses lēdu. Asking which positive sentence you are denying is more reliable than translating the English word not in isolation.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ఇది పుస్తకం కాదు. | Idi pustakaṁ kādu. | This is not a book. |
| అది నీరు కాదు. | Adi nīru kādu. | That is not water. |
| ఇక్కడ నీరు లేదు. | Ikkaḍa nīru lēdu. | There is no water here. |
| నేను అక్కడ లేను. | Nēnu akkaḍa lēnu. | I am not there. |
| మీరు ఇక్కడ లేరు. | Mīru ikkaḍa lēru. | You are not here. (respectful) |
| ఆమె ఇక్కడ లేదు. | Āme ikkaḍa lēdu. | She is not here. |

### Worked examples

`ఇది పుస్తకం కాదు.` - `Idi pustakaṁ kādu.` means “This is not a book.” The demonstrative has a visible referent; kādu denies the category assigned to it rather than denying the object's existence.

`అది నీరు కాదు.` - `Adi nīru kādu.` means “That is not water.” The substance is present but has been misidentified, so an absence negative would communicate a different claim.

`ఇక్కడ నీరు లేదు.` - `Ikkaḍa nīru lēdu.` means “There is no water here.” The place expression limits the absence claim; the sentence does not assert that water is unavailable everywhere.

### Common trap

Do not combine undi and lēdu as if one meant is and the other merely not. In these elementary clauses the negative existential replaces the positive verb.

### Practice

Build these meanings in Telugu. Keep the stated person and register.

1. This is not a book.
2. That is not water.
3. There is no water here.
4. I am not there.
5. You are not here. (respectful)
6. She is not here.

***Answers:***

1. `ఇది పుస్తకం కాదు.` - `Idi pustakaṁ kādu.`
2. `అది నీరు కాదు.` - `Adi nīru kādu.`
3. `ఇక్కడ నీరు లేదు.` - `Ikkaḍa nīru lēdu.`
4. `నేను అక్కడ లేను.` - `Nēnu akkaḍa lēnu.`
5. `మీరు ఇక్కడ లేరు.` - `Mīru ikkaḍa lēru.`
6. `ఆమె ఇక్కడ లేదు.` - `Āme ikkaḍa lēdu.`

### Why these answers work

Answer 4: The negative existential has its own first-person form instead of retaining unnānu and adding a separate negative particle.

Answer 5: The ending maintains respectful or plural agreement even in a negative statement, because negation does not remove social reference.

Answer 6: The ordinary feminine singular negative uses lēdu, matching the positive agreement grouping encountered in the location lesson.

### Retrieve and recombine

A visitor points to tea and asks whether it is water. Reject the identification, then say that water is not here. Explain why the two negatives differ.


## Lesson 6: Doing something now

### Build the pattern

You will describe an action in progress using a small set of complete present progressive forms. Telugu keeps the object before the verb in the neutral pattern. చదువుతున్నాను caduvutunnānu means I am reading or studying, and తాగుతున్నాను tāgutunnānu means I am drinking. These forms include the ongoing-action material and first-person agreement. Replacing the subject with respectful you changes the ending to -āru. He takes -āḍu; she takes -di in the examples here. Do not try to build every progressive by attaching an ending to an English-style dictionary stem, because Telugu stem changes need to be learned alongside each verb.

### Think before reading on

If the object changes from water to tea while the speaker remains I, which part of the sentence should remain stable?

The finite verb tāgutunnānu remains first-person and progressive. Only the object changes. By contrast, changing I to respectful you keeps the drinking meaning but changes the personal ending. Separate vocabulary substitution from agreement substitution.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| నేను నీరు తాగుతున్నాను. | Nēnu nīru tāgutunnānu. | I am drinking water. |
| నేను టీ తాగుతున్నాను. | Nēnu ṭī tāgutunnānu. | I am drinking tea. |
| అతను చదువుతున్నాడు. | Atanu caduvutunnāḍu. | He is studying. |
| ఆమె చదువుతోంది. | Āme caduvutōndi. | She is studying. |
| మీరు ఏం చేస్తున్నారు? | Mīru ēṁ cēstunnāru? | What are you doing? (respectful) |
| నేను పని చేస్తున్నాను. | Nēnu pani cēstunnānu. | I am working. |

### Worked examples

`నేను నీరు తాగుతున్నాను.` - `Nēnu nīru tāgutunnānu.` means “I am drinking water.” The object comes before the final verb, whose ending already identifies the speaker and the ongoing character of the action.

`నేను టీ తాగుతున్నాను.` - `Nēnu ṭī tāgutunnānu.` means “I am drinking tea.” Changing the drink leaves the progressive form untouched; ṭī is an everyday loanword written directly in Telugu script.

`అతను చదువుతున్నాడు.` - `Atanu caduvutunnāḍu.` means “He is studying.” The same verb can mean reading or studying according to its object and context; the masculine ending identifies this subject.

### Common trap

A form ending in -tānu is not automatically an action happening right now. This lesson's -tunnānu makes ongoing activity explicit; habitual and future forms come later.

### Practice

Build these meanings in Telugu. Keep the stated person and register.

1. I am drinking water.
2. I am drinking tea.
3. He is studying.
4. She is studying.
5. What are you doing? (respectful)
6. I am working.

***Answers:***

1. `నేను నీరు తాగుతున్నాను.` - `Nēnu nīru tāgutunnānu.`
2. `నేను టీ తాగుతున్నాను.` - `Nēnu ṭī tāgutunnānu.`
3. `అతను చదువుతున్నాడు.` - `Atanu caduvutunnāḍu.`
4. `ఆమె చదువుతోంది.` - `Āme caduvutōndi.`
5. `మీరు ఏం చేస్తున్నారు?` - `Mīru ēṁ cēstunnāru?`
6. `నేను పని చేస్తున్నాను.` - `Nēnu pani cēstunnānu.`

### Why these answers work

Answer 4: This common contracted progressive form is used for the ordinary feminine singular, rather than mechanically copying the masculine ending.

Answer 5: The shortened question word ēṁ asks about the activity, while cēstunnāru supplies respectful agreement and the progressive action frame.

Answer 6: Pani means work and combines with doing, giving an everyday expression whose structure differs from a single English verb.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe yourself drinking tea and a woman reading a book. Then address that woman respectfully and ask what she is doing. Keep third-person description distinct from second-person address.


## Lesson 7: Objects and people receiving the action

### Build the pattern

You will identify the person or thing affected by an action. Telugu often marks human and specific objects with -ని ni or -ను nu; ordinary nonspecific substance objects can remain unmarked. The pronoun నేను becomes నన్ను nannu as an object, and respectful మీరు becomes మిమ్మల్ని mimmalni. These are whole forms worth recognising. A book can appear as పుస్తకం in a general reading activity, but పుస్తకాన్ని pustakānni points to an identifiable book. The endings are not English articles, and object marking cannot be reduced to one rule about English the. The verb still comes at the end of a neutral sentence.

### Think before reading on

Why does I see you need something different from the respectful subject pronoun మీరు mīru?

You are the object of seeing, so the respectful object form is మిమ్మల్ని mimmalni. Telugu marks the participant's role directly. The respectful relationship remains, but the grammatical form changes according to the role in the sentence.

`నేను మిమ్మల్ని చూస్తున్నాను.` - `Nēnu mimmalni cūstunnānu.` means “I am looking at you. (respectful)” The respectful object form marks the person being looked at; the first-person verb continues to identify who is looking.

`అతను నన్ను చూస్తున్నాడు.` - `Atanu nannu cūstunnāḍu.` means “He is looking at me.” Nannu replaces nēnu because the speaker receives the action, while the masculine ending belongs to the person doing the looking.

`నేను పుస్తకాన్ని చదువుతున్నాను.` - `Nēnu pustakānni caduvutunnānu.` means “I am reading the book.” The marked object makes the identifiable book explicit, and the noun's final shape changes before the accusative ending.

### Common trap

Do not attach ni indiscriminately to every written noun without considering its stem. Pustakaṁ becomes pustakānni, and personal pronouns may change substantially rather than simply taking a suffix.

## Lesson 8: Polite requests and negative commands

### Build the pattern

You will invite someone to come, sit, read and wait. Many polite Telugu commands end in -ండి aṇḍi, but their stems differ: రండి raṇḍi means come, while కూర్చోండి kūrcōṇḍi means sit. These should be learned as usable forms rather than generated from an assumed universal stem. A bare familiar command can sound abrupt when addressed to a stranger. Negative commands use forms such as రాకండి rākaṇḍi, do not come, and మాట్లాడకండి māṭlāḍakaṇḍi, do not speak. They do not use the ordinary identity negative kādu. Requests can be softened through context, tone and దయచేసి dayacēsi, please.

### Think before reading on

Would a polite prohibition be made by putting kādu after the positive command?

No. A prohibition tells someone not to carry out an action; it needs a negative command form. The respectful ending remains important, but the verb material before it changes. Compare raṇḍi and rākaṇḍi as a useful contrasting pair.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ఇక్కడికి రండి. | Ikkaḍiki raṇḍi. | Come here. (polite) |
| కూర్చోండి. | Kūrcōṇḍi. | Sit down. (polite) |
| పుస్తకం చదవండి. | Pustakaṁ cadavaṇḍi. | Read a book. (polite) |
| కొంచెం ఆగండి. | Koñceṁ āgaṇḍi. | Wait a little. (polite) |
| ఇక్కడికి రాకండి. | Ikkaḍiki rākaṇḍi. | Do not come here. (polite) |
| ఇప్పుడు మాట్లాడకండి. | Ippuḍu māṭlāḍakaṇḍi. | Do not speak now. (polite) |

### Worked examples

`ఇక్కడికి రండి.` - `Ikkaḍiki raṇḍi.` means “Come here. (polite)” Ikkaḍiki expresses movement toward here, unlike ikkaḍa, which simply names the place where someone or something is located.

`కూర్చోండి.` - `Kūrcōṇḍi.` means “Sit down. (polite)” The command is already respectful; a separate pronoun is unnecessary when the person being addressed is evident in the situation.

`పుస్తకం చదవండి.` - `Pustakaṁ cadavaṇḍi.` means “Read a book. (polite)” The reading stem appears as cadava- before this ending, so it should not be formed by clipping the progressive arbitrarily.

### Common trap

Do not assume the shortest command is socially neutral. A form suitable for a child or close friend may be inappropriate for a visitor, colleague or older person.

Answer 4: Koñceṁ limits the requested waiting and can make the request less abrupt without changing the polite command morphology.

## Lesson 9: Habitual actions and future intentions

### Build the pattern

You will say what you usually do and what you will do tomorrow. Telugu forms such as వస్తాను vastānu and చదువుతాను caduvutānu can express habitual activity or future action, with context deciding the reading. రోజూ rōjū, every day, points to a routine. రేపు rēpu, tomorrow, points to a future event. This differs from the explicit ongoing forms taught earlier. First-person -ānu does not distinguish male and female speakers. Respectful మీరు uses -āru, and ordinary masculine singular uses -āḍu. Keep the entire familiar form of come, వస్తాను, because the stem differs from the polite command రండి.

### Think before reading on

What changes the interpretation of చదువుతాను from a regular habit to a future plan when the verb itself remains the same?

A time expression and the wider situation do the work. Every day gives a repeated reading, while tomorrow locates a planned event. Telugu does not need to insert a separate word equivalent to English will in these affirmative examples.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| నేను రోజూ చదువుతాను. | Nēnu rōjū caduvutānu. | I study every day. |
| నేను రేపు చదువుతాను. | Nēnu rēpu caduvutānu. | I will study tomorrow. |
| నేను రేపు వస్తాను. | Nēnu rēpu vastānu. | I will come tomorrow. |
| అతను రోజూ వస్తాడు. | Atanu rōjū vastāḍu. | He comes every day. |
| మీరు రేపు వస్తారా? | Mīru rēpu vastārā? | Will you come tomorrow? (respectful) |
| ఆమె రేపు వస్తుంది. | Āme rēpu vastundi. | She will come tomorrow. |

### Worked examples

`నేను రోజూ చదువుతాను.` - `Nēnu rōjū caduvutānu.` means “I study every day.” The repeated-time expression supports a habitual interpretation of a form that can also appear in a future statement.

`నేను రేపు చదువుతాను.` - `Nēnu rēpu caduvutānu.` means “I will study tomorrow.” Rēpu directs the same finite verb toward a future occasion without introducing an independent auxiliary equivalent to English will.

`నేను రేపు వస్తాను.` - `Nēnu rēpu vastānu.` means “I will come tomorrow.” Coming uses the stem vas- in this form, which cannot be predicted merely by adding the ending to the polite command.

### Common trap

Do not claim that the form has only future meaning. Equally, do not use it as the sole model for everything English calls present: an action unfolding now has a progressive construction.

### Why these answers work

Answer 4: The masculine singular ending changes with the subject, while the routine meaning comes from rōjū rather than the ending alone.

Answer 5: The interrogative vowel follows the respectful verb ending and turns the future statement into a question about the listener's plan.

## Lesson 10: Saying what you do not do

### Build the pattern

You will decline an action or report that it does not happen. Telugu has negative finite forms such as రాను rānu, I do not or will not come, and తాగను tāganu, I do not or will not drink. As with the affirmative habitual-future, time and context help determine whether the negative concerns a habit or a future occasion. The negative first-person ending must not be confused with the affirmative -tānu. Respectful రారు rāru differs from first-person rānu. Ordinary masculine రాడు rāḍu differs from both. This lesson uses a small set of verbs to make those contrasts audible before adding more vocabulary.

### Think before reading on

Compare వస్తాను vastānu and రాను rānu. Is the negative made by adding a word after the affirmative?

No. The negative is a different finite form. It carries person information itself. The stem of come also changes, so learning the positive-negative pair is safer than imagining that every negative has a single detachable syllable.

`నేను టీ తాగను.` - `Nēnu ṭī tāganu.` means “I do not drink tea.” Without a particular future occasion, this sentence naturally states a general practice or preference concerning tea consumption.

## Lesson 11: To a place and in a place

### Build the pattern

You will distinguish destination from location. Telugu often uses -లో lō for in and -కి ki or -కు ku for to. Noun stems can change when an ending is attached. ఇల్లు illu becomes ఇంట్లో iṇṭlō, at home or in the house, and ఇంటికి iṇṭiki, to the house or home. బడి baḍi gives బడిలో baḍilō and బడికి baḍiki. Movement verbs such as వెళ్తాను veḷtānu can take a destination, while ఉన్నాను unnānu reports where someone is. These suffixes follow their noun rather than appearing before it as English prepositions do.

### Think before reading on

If a friend asks where you are, would ఇంటికి iṇṭiki answer the same question as ఇంట్లో iṇṭlō?

No. Iṇṭiki identifies a destination, while iṇṭlō identifies a location. Being at home and going home share the noun but assign it different roles. Telugu makes that distinction inside the place expression as well as through the verb.

`నేను ఇంట్లో ఉన్నాను.` - `Nēnu iṇṭlō unnānu.` means “I am at home.” The house stem changes before the locative expression, and the existential verb reports a current position rather than movement.

`నేను ఇంటికి వెళ్తాను.` - `Nēnu iṇṭiki veḷtānu.` means “I will go home.” The destination form iṇṭiki accompanies going, while the finite verb carries first-person agreement and a future or habitual reading.

`ఆమె బడిలో ఉంది.` - `Āme baḍilō undi.` means “She is at school.” The location ending follows the school noun directly, and the finite verb keeps ordinary feminine singular agreement.

### Common trap

Do not build illulō mechanically from illu in this everyday form. The stem iṇṭ- is used in these house expressions, and its shape must be learned with the noun.

## Lesson 12: Having something and needing something

### Build the pattern

You will say that you have a book, want water or need help. Telugu commonly describes possession with a dative possessor and an existential: నాకు పుస్తకం ఉంది nāku pustakaṁ undi. నాకు nāku means to me, while మీకు mīku means to you respectfully or plurally. The possessed object determines the existential form, not the possessor's gender. కావాలి kāvāli expresses wanting or needing and takes the same dative experiencer in these examples. అవసరం avasaraṁ means necessity or need, but we first use the more practical kāvāli construction. ఇష్టం iṣṭaṁ, liking, will later use a similar experiencer pattern.

### Think before reading on

Why does I have a book begin with నాకు nāku rather than నేను nēnu in this construction?

The sentence presents a book as existing for or to the speaker. English chooses have as its verb; Telugu assigns the possessor a dative form and uses existence. The two languages express the same practical fact through different grammatical arrangements.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| నాకు పుస్తకం ఉంది. | Nāku pustakaṁ undi. | I have a book. |
| మీకు పుస్తకం ఉందా? | Mīku pustakaṁ undā? | Do you have a book? (respectful) |
| నాకు నీరు కావాలి. | Nāku nīru kāvāli. | I want water. |
| నాకు సహాయం కావాలి. | Nāku sahāyaṁ kāvāli. | I need help. |
| నాకు పుస్తకం లేదు. | Nāku pustakaṁ lēdu. | I do not have a book. |
| మీకు ఏం కావాలి? | Mīku ēṁ kāvāli? | What do you need? (respectful) |

### Worked examples

`నాకు పుస్తకం ఉంది.` - `Nāku pustakaṁ undi.` means “I have a book.” Nāku names the possessor, while the singular book is what exists; this explains why the final verb is undi.

`మీకు పుస్తకం ఉందా?` - `Mīku pustakaṁ undā?` means “Do you have a book? (respectful)” The question ending attaches to the existential verb, and mīku preserves respectful reference in the dative role.

`నాకు నీరు కావాలి.` - `Nāku nīru kāvāli.` means “I want water.” The desired substance appears before kāvāli, with the experiencer in the same dative form already used for possession.

### Common trap

Do not combine nēnu with kāvāli simply because English I want starts with I. The person experiencing a need uses nāku here, and respectful you becomes mīku.

### Practice

Build these meanings in Telugu. Keep the stated person and register.

1. I have a book.
2. Do you have a book? (respectful)
3. I want water.
4. I need help.
5. I do not have a book.
6. What do you need? (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `నాకు పుస్తకం ఉంది.` - `Nāku pustakaṁ undi.`
2. `మీకు పుస్తకం ఉందా?` - `Mīku pustakaṁ undā?`
3. `నాకు నీరు కావాలి.` - `Nāku nīru kāvāli.`
4. `నాకు సహాయం కావాలి.` - `Nāku sahāyaṁ kāvāli.`
5. `నాకు పుస్తకం లేదు.` - `Nāku pustakaṁ lēdu.`
6. `మీకు ఏం కావాలి?` - `Mīku ēṁ kāvāli?`

### Why these answers work

Answer 4: Kāvāli covers practical need as well as wanting; the surrounding situation determines which English wording is most natural.

Answer 5: Negating the existential denies possession, without needing a separate Telugu verb corresponding to English have in this pattern.

Answer 6: The unknown desired thing is replaced by ēṁ, while the experiencer and need expression remain in their usual positions.

### Retrieve and recombine

At a reading room, say you have a book but need water. Transfer the need pattern to help at a ticket counter, keeping the dative experiencer unchanged.


## Lesson 13: Numbers and amounts

### Build the pattern

You will request small quantities and distinguish counting people from counting ordinary objects. ఒకటి okaṭi, రెండు reṇḍu and మూడు mūḍu are one, two and three. Before an ordinary noun, one commonly becomes ఒక oka. Telugu has dedicated human counting forms such as ఇద్దరు iddaru, two people, and ముగ్గురు mugguru, three people. Books form the plural పుస్తకాలు pustakālu, while people can be referred to as మనుషులు manuṣulu. Plural noun shapes are not always produced by adding lu to the unchanged singular. In shopping, an amount expression can stand as the thing wanted before kāvāli.

### Think before reading on

Would two people use precisely the same number expression as two books?

No. Reṇḍu is used with ordinary objects, but iddaru counts two human beings. This distinction is a Telugu counting pattern, not a distinction between English singular and plural alone. Learn human counting forms as expressions with their own shapes.

## Lesson 14: Describing things and comparing choices

### Build the pattern

You will describe a book, a house and a price. Telugu descriptive words normally precede the noun. మంచి mañci means good, పెద్ద pedda big, and చిన్న cinna small. They do not acquire the same gender endings that appear on Telugu finite verbs. Predicative descriptions can use forms such as బాగుంది bāgundi, is good, and పెద్దది peddadi, a big one or is big for a nonhuman singular referent. This lesson separates the word used before a noun from a complete description of the noun. ధర dhara means price; ఎక్కువ ekkuva means more or high in a price context, and తక్కువ takkuva means less or low.

### Think before reading on

Does the English sentence the book is good imply that Telugu must put mañci after the noun as an independent predicate?

Not necessarily. Mañci is particularly useful before the noun, as in a good book. A natural complete assessment is pustakaṁ bāgundi. Learn descriptive constructions as phrases rather than assuming every adjective has identical attributive and predicative behaviour.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |

| ధర తక్కువ. | Dhara takkuva. | The price is low. |

### Worked examples

`ఇది మంచి పుస్తకం.` - `Idi mañci pustakaṁ.` means “This is a good book.” The descriptive word precedes the noun inside an identity statement, with no English-style article required before the whole phrase.

`అది పెద్ద ఇల్లు.` - `Adi pedda illu.` means “That is a big house.” Pedda describes the house without changing its ending to match a masculine or feminine owner who might live there.

`ఇది చిన్న పుస్తకం.` - `Idi cinna pustakaṁ.` means “This is a small book.” The doubled consonant in cinna is meaningful pronunciation information, and the descriptive word occupies the same position as mañci.

### Common trap

Do not add masculine verb agreement just because a house is large or a book is important. Nonhuman singular reference stays nonhuman; English adjective meanings do not determine Telugu agreement.

## Lesson 15: Completed actions with first-person forms

### Build the pattern

You will report a small set of completed actions. Telugu past forms change their stems and endings, so begin with వచ్చాను vaccānu, I came, వెళ్లాను veḷḷānu, I went, and తిన్నాను tinnānu, I ate. నిన్న ninna means yesterday. The time word supports a past narrative but does not create the past tense by itself. చదివాను cadivānu means I read or studied in the past. The final long vowel and first-person ending should remain audible. English read is ambiguous in writing, but Telugu caduvutānu and cadivānu make the distinction visible. A destination phrase can be retained when changing a going sentence from future to past.

### Think before reading on

Can you turn I will go home into I went home simply by replacing tomorrow with yesterday and leaving veḷtānu untouched?

For an completed-event statement, the verb must change to veḷḷānu. Time expressions and tense work together; a time adverb is not a substitute for learning the finite past form. The destination iṇṭiki remains unchanged.

`నేను నిన్న వచ్చాను.` - `Nēnu ninna vaccānu.` means “I came yesterday.” The past stem vacc- differs from the habitual-future vas-, while ninna locates the completed arrival on the previous day.

## Lesson 16: Past events with other people

### Build the pattern

You will ask respectfully about a past event and report what a man or woman did. The past of come includes వచ్చాడు vaccāḍu for he, వచ్చింది vaccindi for she in ordinary singular reference, and వచ్చారు vaccāru for respectful you or human plural reference. Respectful reference to a woman can also use vaccāru. The pronoun and social situation therefore matter as much as the English translation. A yes-or-no question adds the question vowel to the final verb. Use ఎప్పుడు eppuḍu, when, when the date or time itself is unknown. The past stem remains past when the sentence becomes a question.

### Think before reading on

If మీరు వచ్చారు mīru vaccāru means you came respectfully, where should the question ending go to ask whether the arrival happened?

It goes on the final verb, giving vaccārā. There is no Telugu equivalent of English did placed before the subject. In a when-question, eppuḍu supplies the unknown time and the ordinary past finite form remains available.

## Lesson 17: Past negatives and unfinished expectations

### Build the pattern

You will say that a past action did not happen. Telugu commonly uses forms such as రాలేదు rālēdu, did not come, and తినలేదు tinalēdu, did not eat. In this negative past construction the same form is used with the first-person and third-person subjects illustrated here. That contrasts with the positive past endings in the preceding lesson. ఇంకా iṅkā means still or yet according to context; ఇంకా రాలేదు can mean has not come yet. English may choose a simple past or present perfect translation depending on whether the statement is tied to a finished past time or an expectation continuing now.

### Think before reading on

Will changing I did not come to he did not come require changing rālēdu to a masculine form in this pattern?

No. Rālēdu stays the same in these examples. Telugu positive and negative paradigms are not mirror images. The explicit pronoun tells the listener who failed to come, while the common negative past expression says what did not happen.

| ఆమె ఇంకా రాలేదు. | Āme iṅkā rālēdu. | She has not come yet. |

| మీరు టీ తాగలేదా? | Mīru ṭī tāgalēdā? | Did you not drink tea? (respectful) |

### Worked examples

`నేను నిన్న రాలేదు.` - `Nēnu ninna rālēdu.` means “I did not come yesterday.” The past negative form replaces the affirmative vaccānu, and ninna anchors the nonoccurrence in a finished past day.

`అతను నిన్న రాలేదు.` - `Atanu ninna rālēdu.` means “He did not come yesterday.” Changing the subject leaves rālēdu intact in this construction, unlike the person and gender changes of the positive past.

`ఆమె ఇంకా రాలేదు.` - `Āme iṅkā rālēdu.` means “She has not come yet.” Iṅkā connects the negative arrival to a continuing expectation, making an English present-perfect translation natural in this situation.

### Common trap

Do not attach the positive masculine ending to lēdu to manufacture a past negative. Also distinguish rālēdu, did not come, from rāḍu, he will not or does not come.

Answer 4: The eating negative uses tina- before lēdu rather than the positive past stem tinn- followed by a personal ending.

Answer 6: The question vowel attaches to the past negative and can express a genuine check or surprise depending on intonation.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe a missed visit yesterday and an arrival still expected today. Use ninna for the first and iṅkā for the second, noticing why English translations may choose different tenses.


## Lesson 18: Ability and permission

### Build the pattern

You will say that you can or cannot do something and ask permission politely. Telugu uses forms such as చదవగలను cadavagalanu, I can read, and చదవలేను cadavalēnu, I cannot read. The ability expression is not the same as the ordinary negative habit. Permission questions can use రావచ్చా rāvaccā, may I come, and కూర్చోవచ్చా kūrcōvaccā, may I sit. Their interpretation depends on the subject and situation, so the course uses explicit first-person pronouns when useful. కొంచెం koñceṁ means a little and can qualify a language ability. తెలుగు telugu names both the language and the cultural-linguistic adjective in context.

### Think before reading on

What is the difference between I do not read and I cannot read, even when both result in no reading occurring?

The first reports a habit, choice or future nonaction. The second says that ability or possibility is missing. Telugu expresses that distinction with different verb forms; a single negative word cannot safely represent both meanings.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| నేను తెలుగు చదవగలను. | Nēnu telugu cadavagalanu. | I can read Telugu. |
| నేను తెలుగు చదవలేను. | Nēnu telugu cadavalēnu. | I cannot read Telugu. |
| నేను కొంచెం తెలుగు మాట్లాడగలను. | Nēnu koñceṁ telugu māṭlāḍagalanu. | I can speak a little Telugu. |
| నేను రావచ్చా? | Nēnu rāvaccā? | May I come? |
| నేను కూర్చోవచ్చా? | Nēnu kūrcōvaccā? | May I sit down? |
| నేను రేపు రావచ్చా? | Nēnu rēpu rāvaccā? | May I come tomorrow? |

### Worked examples

`నేను తెలుగు చదవగలను.` - `Nēnu telugu cadavagalanu.` means “I can read Telugu.” The ability element follows the reading stem, and the first-person ending identifies whose ability is being described.

`నేను తెలుగు చదవలేను.` - `Nēnu telugu cadavalēnu.` means “I cannot read Telugu.” The negative ability form denies capacity or possibility, rather than merely reporting a personal habit of not reading.

`నేను కొంచెం తెలుగు మాట్లాడగలను.` - `Nēnu koñceṁ telugu māṭlāḍagalanu.` means “I can speak a little Telugu.” Koñceṁ limits the claimed ability, giving a useful modest self-description without implying comprehensive command of the language.

### Common trap

Do not use a permission question as a claim of skill. Rāvaccā asks whether coming is allowed or possible in the situation; cadavagalanu describes an ability of the speaker.

## Lesson 19: Likes, knowledge and experience

### Build the pattern

You will express liking and knowing with experiencer constructions. Telugu commonly uses నాకు ఇష్టం nāku iṣṭaṁ for I like something and నాకు తెలుసు nāku telusu for I know something. The dative person form is familiar from wanting and possession. తెలియదు teliyadu means do not know in these examples. A language name with telusu can mean knowing that language; it does not specify exactly which skills the person has. The course therefore also retains the more precise ability statements from the previous lesson. Questions use మీకు mīku for the respectful experiencer and can attach ā to the final expression.

### Think before reading on

Why might I know Telugu begin with nāku, even though I speak Telugu begins with nēnu in the earlier action pattern?

Knowing is expressed here as a state applying to an experiencer, while speaking presents the speaker as the doer of an action. English uses the same subject pronoun for both, but Telugu distinguishes the constructions. Follow the verb or predicate, not the English pronoun alone.

### Useful language in context

| మీకు టీ ఇష్టమా? | Mīku ṭī iṣṭamā? | Do you like tea? (respectful) |
| నాకు తెలుగు తెలుసు. | Nāku telugu telusu. | I know Telugu. |

### Worked examples

`నాకు టీ ఇష్టం.` - `Nāku ṭī iṣṭaṁ.` means “I like tea.” The liked item follows the dative experiencer and precedes iṣṭaṁ, forming a preference statement without a verb meaning English like.

`మీకు టీ ఇష్టమా?` - `Mīku ṭī iṣṭamā?` means “Do you like tea? (respectful)” The question ending attaches to iṣṭaṁ through its linking m, while the respectful experiencer remains in the dative form.

`నాకు తెలుగు తెలుసు.` - `Nāku telugu telusu.` means “I know Telugu.” The language name is what is known, but the sentence leaves the speaker's exact level and balance of skills unspecified.

### Common trap

Do not infer reading, writing and fluent conversation from a bare claim that someone knows a language. When a practical task matters, ask about the relevant ability using a more specific verb.

### Why these answers work

Answer 4: The quantity word qualifies the extent of knowledge, making the claim more limited without changing the experiencer construction.

Answer 5: The unknown matter is supplied by context, so the sentence can answer a question without restating its entire content.

Answer 6: The interrogative form asks about the listener's knowledge of the current topic, with no need for an English-style do auxiliary.

## Lesson 20: With someone and from somewhere

### Build the pattern

You will identify a companion and a starting point. Telugu uses -తో tō for with, including accompaniment and instruments in suitable contexts. Pronouns have special forms: నాతో nātō, with me, and మీతో mītō, with you respectfully. From can be expressed with నుంచి nuñci or నుండి nuṇḍi; this course chooses nuñci consistently for everyday examples. A source phrase differs from both a location and a destination. ఇంటి నుంచి iṇṭi nuñci means from home, using the house stem already encountered. మాట్లాడండి māṭlāḍaṇḍi means speak politely, and can take a with-phrase naming the conversational partner.

### Think before reading on

How do with me and to me differ, given that both involve a relationship to the speaker?

Nātō expresses accompaniment or interaction with the speaker, while nāku marks a dative relation such as recipient or experiencer. English prepositions are clues to meaning, but the Telugu forms have to match the actual relationship in the event.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |

### Worked examples

`నాతో రండి.` - `Nātō raṇḍi.` means “Come with me. (polite)” Nātō identifies the companion, while the command itself supplies the movement and respectful address without requiring an overt you pronoun.

`మీతో మాట్లాడవచ్చా?` - `Mītō māṭlāḍavaccā?` means “May I speak with you? (respectful)” The with-form names the conversational partner, and the permission question makes the approach less abrupt than a bare command.

`నేను ఇంటి నుంచి వచ్చాను.` - `Nēnu iṇṭi nuñci vaccānu.` means “I came from home.” The source expression uses the oblique house stem before nuñci and combines naturally with a completed arrival verb.

### Common trap

Do not use a destination ending when reporting a source. Coming from school and going to school have opposite spatial relationships even if the same school appears in both sentences.

## Lesson 21: Connecting actions in order

### Build the pattern

You will connect two actions performed by the same person. Telugu frequently uses a nonfinal verb form before the final finite verb: వచ్చి vacci, having come, and తిని tini, having eaten, can introduce an action that precedes another. The final verb supplies the tense and person information for the whole sequence. This is more compact than repeating the subject and joining two complete English-style clauses with and. The relationship can be understood as coming and then doing something or doing something after arrival. The course begins with transparent sequential examples and does not assume that every nonfinal verb expresses a strictly completed earlier event.

### Think before reading on

In వచ్చి కూర్చోండి vacci kūrcōṇḍi, which word tells you that the sentence is a polite request rather than a past report?

The final verb kūrcōṇḍi is the polite command. Vacci itself does not carry the sentence's final tense or command force. That is why the same nonfinal action can appear before different final verbs and participate in different kinds of sentences.

### Useful language in context

| --- | --- | --- |

| పుస్తకం తీసుకుని రండి. | Pustakaṁ tīsukuni raṇḍi. | Bring a book. (polite) |
| నేను తిని వచ్చాను. | Nēnu tini vaccānu. | I ate and came. |
| ఆమె వచ్చి కూర్చుంది. | Āme vacci kūrcundi. | She came and sat down. |

| నేను పుస్తకం తీసుకుని వచ్చాను. | Nēnu pustakaṁ tīsukuni vaccānu. | I brought a book. |

### Worked examples

`ఇక్కడికి వచ్చి కూర్చోండి.` - `Ikkaḍiki vacci kūrcōṇḍi.` means “Come here and sit down. (polite)” Vacci introduces arrival, while the final command supplies respectful request force for the connected sequence of actions.

`పుస్తకం తీసుకుని రండి.` - `Pustakaṁ tīsukuni raṇḍi.` means “Bring a book. (polite)” The expression literally combines taking with coming, giving the practical meaning bring through a sequence whose final verb is come.

`నేను తిని వచ్చాను.` - `Nēnu tini vaccānu.` means “I ate and came.” Tini marks the eating as nonfinal, and vaccānu supplies first-person past information for the report of the connected events.

### Common trap

Do not give the first verb an independent first-person ending and then call it the same construction. Two finite clauses are possible elsewhere, but this pattern deliberately leaves the first action nonfinal.

***Answers:***

1. `ఇక్కడికి వచ్చి కూర్చోండి.` - `Ikkaḍiki vacci kūrcōṇḍi.`
2. `పుస్తకం తీసుకుని రండి.` - `Pustakaṁ tīsukuni raṇḍi.`

### Why these answers work

Answer 4: The final sitting form carries ordinary feminine singular past agreement, while vacci remains unchanged from the command example.

## Lesson 22: Reasons and simple conditions

### Build the pattern

You will connect a reason to a result and make a simple conditional invitation. అందుకే andukē means that is why or therefore in these examples. కానీ kānī means but and introduces a contrast. For a basic if-clause, వస్తే vastē means if someone comes, and ఉంటే uṇṭē means if there is or if someone is. The final clause states what follows from that condition. These nonfinal conditional forms do not by themselves identify every participant, so pronouns or context become important. సమయం samayaṁ means time and can combine with the existential to describe having time available.

### Think before reading on

Does if you come assert that the listener has already arrived, simply because the sentence contains the verb come?

No. The conditional form presents arrival as a condition to be considered. The following clause describes the consequence or response. A conditional differs from both a past report and an unconditional future promise, even when the same people and actions appear.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| నాకు సమయం లేదు. | Nāku samayaṁ lēdu. | I do not have time. |
| అందుకే నేను రాను. | Andukē nēnu rānu. | That is why I will not come. |
| నాకు సమయం ఉంది కానీ నేను రాను. | Nāku samayaṁ undi kānī nēnu rānu. | I have time, but I will not come. |
| మీరు వస్తే నేను వస్తాను. | Mīru vastē nēnu vastānu. | If you come, I will come. (respectful) |
| సమయం ఉంటే రండి. | Samayaṁ uṇṭē raṇḍi. | Come if there is time. (polite) |
| మీకు సమయం ఉందా? | Mīku samayaṁ undā? | Do you have time? (respectful) |

### Worked examples

`నాకు సమయం లేదు.` - `Nāku samayaṁ lēdu.` means “I do not have time.” The possession pattern treats time as unavailable to the speaker, creating a complete reason that a following sentence can refer back to.

`అందుకే నేను రాను.` - `Andukē nēnu rānu.` means “That is why I will not come.” Andukē links the refusal to a stated reason instead of presenting the negative arrival as an unrelated fact.

`నాకు సమయం ఉంది కానీ నేను రాను.` - `Nāku samayaṁ undi kānī nēnu rānu.` means “I have time, but I will not come.” Kānī marks the contrast between available time and the decision not to come, without pretending that the first clause causes the second.

### Common trap

Do not translate andukē as an all-purpose and. It points back to a reason. Kānī signals contrast, while a conditional form links a possible circumstance to a result.

### Practice

6. Do you have time? (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `నాకు సమయం లేదు.` - `Nāku samayaṁ lēdu.`
2. `అందుకే నేను రాను.` - `Andukē nēnu rānu.`
3. `నాకు సమయం ఉంది కానీ నేను రాను.` - `Nāku samayaṁ undi kānī nēnu rānu.`
4. `మీరు వస్తే నేను వస్తాను.` - `Mīru vastē nēnu vastānu.`
5. `సమయం ఉంటే రండి.` - `Samayaṁ uṇṭē raṇḍi.`
6. `మీకు సమయం ఉందా?` - `Mīku samayaṁ undā?`

### Why these answers work

Answer 4: The explicit subjects clarify who belongs to the conditional clause and who makes the resulting future commitment.

Answer 5: The existential conditional makes available time the condition, while the final respectful command supplies the invitation.

Answer 6: The earlier possession question now gathers information needed before making a conditional invitation or proposing a meeting.

### Retrieve and recombine

## Lesson 23: At a shop or tea stall

### Build the pattern

You will complete a short purchase conversation by combining price, quantity, need and polite requests. ఎంత enta asks how much, and రూపాయలు rūpāyalu means rupees. ఇవ్వండి ivvaṇḍi is the polite command give. A buyer can state a desired item with nāku and kāvāli, ask its price, then request that it be handed over. A seller may omit material already obvious in context, but the first practice sentences remain explicit. వేడి vēḍi means hot, and చల్లని callani means cold as a modifier. These descriptions affect what is requested; they are not instructions about whether a beverage is medically suitable.

### Think before reading on

Why can the price question be ఇది ఎంత idi enta rather than a word-for-word version of how much does this cost?

The object is already identified by the demonstrative, and enta asks for its amount or price in the shopping context. Telugu can use this compact question without an extra support verb equivalent to does. The setting helps identify the amount being requested.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ఇది ఎంత? | Idi enta? | How much is this? |
| నాకు ఒక టీ కావాలి. | Nāku oka ṭī kāvāli. | I want one tea. |
| కొంచెం నీరు ఇవ్వండి. | Koñceṁ nīru ivvaṇḍi. | Give me a little water. (polite) |
| నాకు వేడి టీ కావాలి. | Nāku vēḍi ṭī kāvāli. | I want hot tea. |
| నాకు రెండు పుస్తకాలు ఇవ్వండి. | Nāku reṇḍu pustakālu ivvaṇḍi. | Give me two books. (polite) |
| ధర పది రూపాయలు. | Dhara padi rūpāyalu. | The price is ten rupees. |

### Worked examples

`ఇది ఎంత?` - `Idi enta?` means “How much is this?” The demonstrative identifies the item, and the shop setting makes the requested amount a price rather than a measurement of size.

`నాకు ఒక టీ కావాలి.` - `Nāku oka ṭī kāvāli.` means “I want one tea.” A serving of tea can be counted in the practical ordering context, although tea as a substance is not ordinarily a count noun.

`కొంచెం నీరు ఇవ్వండి.` - `Koñceṁ nīru ivvaṇḍi.` means “Give me a little water. (polite)” The recipient is understood from the request situation, while koñceṁ specifies a small amount and ivvaṇḍi maintains polite address.

### Common trap

Do not confuse asking how much with asking how many people. Enta is useful for amount and price here; human counting and object quantities follow the patterns already introduced.

### Practice

Build these meanings in Telugu. Keep the stated person and register.

1. How much is this?
2. I want one tea.
3. Give me a little water. (polite)
4. I want hot tea.
5. Give me two books. (polite)
6. The price is ten rupees.

***Answers:***

1. `ఇది ఎంత?` - `Idi enta?`
2. `నాకు ఒక టీ కావాలి.` - `Nāku oka ṭī kāvāli.`
3. `కొంచెం నీరు ఇవ్వండి.` - `Koñceṁ nīru ivvaṇḍi.`
4. `నాకు వేడి టీ కావాలి.` - `Nāku vēḍi ṭī kāvāli.`
5. `నాకు రెండు పుస్తకాలు ఇవ్వండి.` - `Nāku reṇḍu pustakālu ivvaṇḍi.`
6. `ధర పది రూపాయలు.` - `Dhara padi rūpāyalu.`

### Why these answers work

Answer 4: The descriptive word precedes the beverage inside the desired-object phrase, reusing the adjective position introduced with books and houses.

Answer 5: Nāku now functions as recipient rather than experiencer, while the quantity phrase remains the thing being transferred.

Answer 6: Padi supplies ten and the plural currency noun names the unit, producing a simple quoted price without an extra copula.

### Retrieve and recombine

## Lesson 24: Repairing a conversation and arranging a visit

### Build the pattern

You will ask for slower speech, request repetition and arrange a visit. మళ్లీ maḷḷī means again, నెమ్మదిగా nemmadigā means slowly, and చెప్పండి ceppaṇḍi means say or tell politely. Understanding can be expressed through అర్థం arthaṁ: నాకు అర్థం కాలేదు nāku arthaṁ kālēdu means I did not understand. This is a useful whole expression, not a direct model for every negative verb. Combine it with a clear repair request so your partner knows how to help. A visit can then be negotiated with rēpu, permission questions and respectful future questions, all already introduced in earlier lessons.

### Think before reading on

If you did not understand, what extra request would help your partner more than simply repeating I do not know?

Ask them to say it again or speak slowly. Knowledge and understanding are different conversational problems: teliyadu denies knowing, while arthaṁ kālēdu reports that the message was not understood. A specific repair request gives the other speaker an actionable next step.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| నాకు అర్థం కాలేదు. | Nāku arthaṁ kālēdu. | I did not understand. |
| మళ్లీ చెప్పండి. | Maḷḷī ceppaṇḍi. | Say it again. (polite) |
| నెమ్మదిగా మాట్లాడండి. | Nemmadigā māṭlāḍaṇḍi. | Speak slowly. (polite) |
| నేను రేపు రావచ్చా? | Nēnu rēpu rāvaccā? | May I come tomorrow? |
| మీరు రేపు వస్తారా? | Mīru rēpu vastārā? | Will you come tomorrow? (respectful) |
| దయచేసి మళ్లీ చెప్పండి. | Dayacēsi maḷḷī ceppaṇḍi. | Please say it again. |

### Worked examples

`నాకు అర్థం కాలేదు.` - `Nāku arthaṁ kālēdu.` means “I did not understand.” The dative experiencer identifies whose understanding failed, and the whole expression is appropriate after a message that was not understood.

`మళ్లీ చెప్పండి.` - `Maḷḷī ceppaṇḍi.` means “Say it again. (polite)” Maḷḷī requests repetition of the preceding content, and the respectful command avoids the abruptness of a bare familiar imperative.

`నెమ్మదిగా మాట్లాడండి.` - `Nemmadigā māṭlāḍaṇḍi.` means “Speak slowly. (polite)” The manner word modifies the speaking action, specifying the kind of adjustment that would help the listener follow the conversation.

### Common trap

Do not pretend that one memorised repair phrase guarantees comprehension of any reply. Use it to narrow the conversation, confirm the day, and ask one manageable question at a time.

### Practice

Build these meanings in Telugu. Keep the stated person and register.

1. I did not understand.
2. Say it again. (polite)
3. Speak slowly. (polite)
4. May I come tomorrow?
5. Will you come tomorrow? (respectful)
6. Please say it again.

***Answers:***

1. `నాకు అర్థం కాలేదు.` - `Nāku arthaṁ kālēdu.`
2. `మళ్లీ చెప్పండి.` - `Maḷḷī ceppaṇḍi.`
3. `నెమ్మదిగా మాట్లాడండి.` - `Nemmadigā māṭlāḍaṇḍi.`
4. `నేను రేపు రావచ్చా?` - `Nēnu rēpu rāvaccā?`
5. `మీరు రేపు వస్తారా?` - `Mīru rēpu vastārā?`
6. `దయచేసి మళ్లీ చెప్పండి.` - `Dayacēsi maḷḷī ceppaṇḍi.`

### Why these answers work

Answer 4: The permission question proposes a visit on a specific day rather than merely asking whether the listener predicts an arrival.

Answer 5: This question concerns the other person's future action, contrasting with the preceding request for permission about the speaker's own action.

Answer 6: The additional politeness expression can soften the request, but the respectful verb form remains the central grammatical signal of address.

### Retrieve and recombine

Construct a visit dialogue: ask to come tomorrow, fail to understand the answer, request repetition, then confirm tomorrow. Keep respectful forms consistent even while asking for help.


## Lesson 25: Cumulative review through a small story

### Build the pattern

You will connect identity, possession, place and time in an account rather than learning another tense table. Imagine Ravi preparing to visit a school. He has a book, studied yesterday, is at home now, and will go tomorrow. The story requires several different constructions because English be, have and do not share one Telugu equivalent. Before each sentence, identify whether you are naming someone, locating them, assigning possession, reporting a completed event or making a future statement. This functional choice is more useful than searching for the English verb word by word. Keep ordinary third-person forms distinct from respectful address when Ravi speaks to a teacher.

### Think before reading on

Which parts must change when I am at home becomes I went home yesterday: only the time, only the verb, or also the house expression?

Both the relationship to home and the event change. Iṇṭlō expresses location, while iṇṭiki expresses destination; unnānu becomes the past movement verb veḷḷānu. Ninna supplies yesterday. A coherent transformation considers meaning at every level, not just the visible verb ending.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| నా పేరు రవి. | Nā pēru Ravi. | My name is Ravi. |
| నాకు ఒక పుస్తకం ఉంది. | Nāku oka pustakaṁ undi. | I have one book. |
| నేను ఇప్పుడు ఇంట్లో ఉన్నాను. | Nēnu ippuḍu iṇṭlō unnānu. | I am at home now. |
| నేను నిన్న పుస్తకం చదివాను. | Nēnu ninna pustakaṁ cadivānu. | I read a book yesterday. |
| నేను రేపు బడికి వెళ్తాను. | Nēnu rēpu baḍiki veḷtānu. | I will go to school tomorrow. |
| మీకు సహాయం కావాలా? | Mīku sahāyaṁ kāvālā? | Do you need help? (respectful) |

### Worked examples

`నా పేరు రవి.` - `Nā pēru Ravi.` means “My name is Ravi.” The opening identifies the speaker by name through a possessive noun phrase and does not need an existential verb.

`నాకు ఒక పుస్తకం ఉంది.` - `Nāku oka pustakaṁ undi.` means “I have one book.” Possession combines a dative possessor, a counted object and the singular existential, keeping three earlier patterns in one sentence.

`నేను ఇప్పుడు ఇంట్లో ఉన్నాను.` - `Nēnu ippuḍu iṇṭlō unnānu.` means “I am at home now.” The time expression and locative house form support a current location statement, distinct from either identity or movement toward home.

### Common trap

Do not interpret a successful review as proof of unrestricted fluency. These patterns support a bounded foundation; longer authentic speech adds vocabulary, regional pronunciation, discourse choices and constructions beyond this course.

### Practice

2. I have one book.
3. I am at home now.
4. I read a book yesterday.
5. I will go to school tomorrow.
6. Do you need help? (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `నా పేరు రవి.` - `Nā pēru Ravi.`
2. `నాకు ఒక పుస్తకం ఉంది.` - `Nāku oka pustakaṁ undi.`
3. `నేను ఇప్పుడు ఇంట్లో ఉన్నాను.` - `Nēnu ippuḍu iṇṭlō unnānu.`
4. `నేను నిన్న పుస్తకం చదివాను.` - `Nēnu ninna pustakaṁ cadivānu.`
5. `నేను రేపు బడికి వెళ్తాను.` - `Nēnu rēpu baḍiki veḷtānu.`

### Why these answers work

Answer 4: The explicit past time and reading past agree in temporal interpretation, while the object precedes the final finite verb.

Answer 5: A destination replaces the location relation, and tomorrow makes the habitual-future form a plan for a particular later day.

Answer 6: The final question transfers the need construction to another person, retaining the dative role and respectful social reference together.

### Retrieve and recombine
