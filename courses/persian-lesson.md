# Persian: A pattern-first foundation

Editorial status: original AI-assisted draft; fluent-speaker review pending.

This course teaches contemporary Iranian Persian in the Persian alphabet, using a clear standard written register. Colloquial Iranian forms are labelled when introduced. Dari and Tajik are related standards with their own pronunciation, usage and, for Tajik, writing system; this course does not claim to teach all three. Persian is not Arabic: shared letters and borrowed words do not imply shared grammar.

Read the English explanation, pause at the thinking question, construct a sentence aloud, and then inspect the example or answer. The romanisation is a learning aid: `ā` represents the vowel in آب, `u` the vowel in دور, `i` the vowel in شیر; `a, e, o` are the shorter vowels. `kh`, `sh`, `ch` and `zh` represent خ، ش، چ، ژ. `q` represents ق and `gh` غ, whose pronunciations commonly merge in Iranian Persian. An apostrophe marks a relevant break, not English possession. A hyphen makes a grammatical boundary audible, particularly the linking sound called ezafe. It is not necessarily a written hyphen in Persian.

Persian text runs right to left, but numbers have their own order. Most short vowels are omitted in ordinary writing. Learn a new word through its spelling, meaning and pronunciation together; do not attempt to pronounce unfamiliar text by supplying arbitrary vowels. This course preserves Persian `ی` and `ک` and uses the zero-width non-joiner where appropriate, as in `می‌روم`. Typed romanisation is accepted in practice. A device voice is optional and its accent is not an assessment of your pronunciation.

## Lesson 1: Make a sentence before learning the whole alphabet

### Outcome

Introduce yourself with a profession or a nationality and understand which word supplies the meaning “am”. You do not need every letter before producing a useful sentence. Start with a small set of words whose sounds and written shapes you can recognise.

### Build from meaning

English puts “am” between a person and a description: “I am a teacher.” A simple Persian identity sentence commonly places the description before the verb: `من معلم هستم.` Read it `man mo'allem hastam`: I, teacher, am. The final word tells us that the speaker is talking about themself. Persian has no indefinite article corresponding exactly to English “a” in this profession sentence. Do not insert an extra word just because the English translation contains one.

| Persian | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| من | man | I |
| هستم | hastam | I am |
| معلم | mo'allem | teacher |
| دانشجو | dāneshju | university student |
| پزشک | pezeshk | physician |
| مهندس | mohandes | engineer |
| ایرانی | irāni | Iranian |
| هندی | hendi | Indian |

> **Think first:** If `من مهندس هستم.` identifies an engineer, what single part would change to identify a physician? Say the whole new sentence before reading on.

You replace the profession: `من پزشک هستم.` (`man pezeshk hastam`). Neither the pronoun nor the verb needs to change. The grammatical frame and the information placed inside it are separate decisions. This is more useful than memorising eight complete introductions independently.

### Read and recombine

`من دانشجو هستم.` (`man dāneshju hastam`) means “I am a university student.” The same structure gives `من هندی هستم.` (`man hendi hastam`), “I am Indian.” `من معلم هستم.` (`man mo'allem hastam`) works whether the teacher is a man or a woman. Persian does not change this pronoun or verb to agree with the speaker's gender.

Notice the first and last letters of `من`. Their shapes differ from isolated alphabet charts because letters join in different positions. Recognising a whole familiar word provides a useful anchor while you gradually learn those shapes. It does not replace studying the script, but it makes that study meaningful immediately.

### Common trap

Do not interpret the English translation as a word-for-word assembly instruction. “I am an engineer” has four English words; its Persian model has three. Also, `hastam` is one word, not the English expression “has them”. The romanisation represents Persian sounds.

### Practice

Introduce yourself in standard Persian.

1. I am an engineer.
2. I am a university student.
3. I am Indian.
4. I am a physician.

***Answers:***

1. `من مهندس هستم.` - `Man mohandes hastam.`
2. `من دانشجو هستم.` - `Man dāneshju hastam.`
3. `من هندی هستم.` - `Man hendi hastam.`
4. `من پزشک هستم.` - `Man pezeshk hastam.`

### Why these answers work

Each answer keeps `man` and `hastam` and changes the description between them. No masculine or feminine agreement is required. To retrieve the pattern in a new context, introduce yourself with two separate true statements, then invent an introduction for a fictional teacher. Keep each statement short enough to say without reading.

## Lesson 2: Change the person and let the ending follow

### Outcome

Describe yourself, a listener and other people while keeping person and verb agreement connected. English distinguishes “am”, “is” and “are”; Persian also gives the verb information about the person and number of its subject.

### Pronouns and respectful address

`تو` (`to`) addresses one familiar person. `شما` (`shomā`) addresses more than one person and is also used respectfully to one person. For an unfamiliar adult, the respectful form is a useful default. This is a social choice, not a claim that Persian speakers always use identical levels of formality.

| Person | Identity verb | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| من | هستم | man hastam | I am |
| تو | هستی | to hasti | you are, familiar singular |
| او | است | u ast | he or she is |
| ما | هستیم | mā hastim | we are |
| شما | هستید | shomā hastid | you are, respectful or plural |
| آنها | هستند | ānhā hastand | they are |

Use `است` (`ast`) for ordinary third-person identification: `او معلم است.` (`u mo'allem ast`), “She/he is a teacher.” `هست` exists too, but its use can foreground existence or presence; it is not a replacement to insert mechanically into every identity sentence. The course uses the neutral identity form in this lesson.

> **Think first:** You have said `من مهندس هستم.` How many grammatical pieces need changing to say “You are an engineer” respectfully?

Both the subject and the verb change: `شما مهندس هستید.` (`shomā mohandes hastid`). The profession remains the same. When building a sentence, choose the person first and hold that choice until you reach the end.

### Worked contrasts

`تو دانشجو هستی.` (`to dāneshju hasti`) addresses a familiar student. `ما دانشجو هستیم.` (`mā dāneshju hastim`) identifies us as students. A profession used predicatively can remain unmarked here; do not add an English-style plural ending to its romanisation. `آنها ایرانی هستند.` (`ānhā irāni hastand`) identifies a group as Iranian.

`او` does not encode the English he/she distinction. Context supplies it when relevant. If you are translating a sentence that mentions a woman, a different Persian third-person pronoun is not needed. This also means a Persian sentence alone may not tell you someone's gender.

### Common trap

Do not combine `شما` with `هستی` simply because the listener is one person. Respectful singular address still takes the `shomā` verb form taught here. Persian can also omit an understood subject pronoun because the verb ending identifies it, but keeping the pronoun during these first lessons makes the agreement pattern visible.

### Practice

Use the person and level of address specified.

1. You are a teacher. Use respectful address.
2. We are engineers.
3. She is a university student.
4. You are Iranian. Address one familiar person.

***Answers:***

1. `شما معلم هستید.` - `Shomā mo'allem hastid.`
2. `ما مهندس هستیم.` - `Mā mohandes hastim.`
3. `او دانشجو است.` - `U dāneshju ast.`
4. `تو ایرانی هستی.` - `To irāni hasti.`

### Why these answers work

The profession or nationality describes the subject, and the final verb agrees with that subject. Now move the same description through three people without looking: I am a teacher, we are teachers, and she is a teacher. Explain aloud why the third sentence does not require a feminine verb.

## Lesson 3: Describe a thing and connect words with ezafe

### Outcome

Distinguish a complete description such as “The room is small” from a noun phrase such as “the small room”. These have different jobs, so they use different structures.

### Start with the noun

In `اتاق کوچک است.` (`otāq kuchek ast`), the speaker says that the room is small. The final `ast` makes a statement. To name a particular kind of room inside another sentence, use `اتاق کوچک` (`otāq-e kuchek`), “the small room”. The linking vowel `-e`, called ezafe, joins the noun to a following modifier. It is normally heard even when ordinary spelling does not show a short-vowel mark.

| Persian | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| اتاق | otāq | room |
| کوچک | kuchek | small |
| بزرگ | bozorg | big |
| کتاب | ketāb | book |
| جدید | jadid | new |
| خوب | khub | good |
| خانه | khāne | house, home |
| دوست | dust | friend |

> **Think first:** In “a new book”, which English word names the thing? Put that idea first in Persian, then link the description to it.

The phrase is `کتاب جدید` (`ketāb-e jadid`). The link tells the listener that another word still belongs to the same noun phrase. Compare the complete statement `کتاب جدید است.` (`ketāb jadid ast`), “The book is new.” In that statement `jadid` is the predicate, so you do not put ezafe between the subject `ketāb` and its predicate.

### Add an owner

The same link can attach an owner: `دوست من` (`dust-e man`), “my friend”. To describe the friend and identify whose friend, build a chain: `دوست خوب من` (`dust-e khub-e man`), “my good friend”. The linking sound belongs after each connected element except the final one. Think in terms of one expanding phrase, not a sequence of complete sentences.

After final `ه` pronounced `e`, this course writes the link as `خانهٔ من` (`khāne-ye man`), “my house”. You may encounter alternative spelling conventions. The written mark and the linking pronunciation are distinct issues. Do not pronounce the final `ه` as a strong English h in `khāne`.

### Common trap

Ezafe is not a separate Persian word meaning only “of”. Sometimes its English translation uses “of”, sometimes an English possessive, and sometimes just an adjective before a noun. Translating it with one fixed English word hides its actual function: connecting the noun phrase.

### Practice

Build the requested phrase or complete sentence.

1. My book.
2. My good friend.
3. The room is small.
4. Your house. Use respectful address.

***Answers:***

1. `کتاب من` - `Ketāb-e man`
2. `دوست خوب من` - `Dust-e khub-e man`
3. `اتاق کوچک است.` - `Otāq kuchek ast.`
4. `خانهٔ شما` - `Khāne-ye shomā`

### Why these answers work

Answers one, two and four name something; they are noun phrases. Answer three makes a statement and therefore ends with `ast`. For transfer, describe a big room as a phrase, then turn it into a statement. Say the difference between `otāq-e bozorg` and `otāq bozorg ast` slowly enough to hear where the linking vowel does and does not belong.

## Lesson 4: Ask a question without importing English do

### Outcome

Ask yes/no questions and simple information questions while preserving the Persian sentence frame. You can seek information before learning a separate question word for every situation.

### Turn a statement into a question

`شما معلم هستید.` states “You are a teacher.” With question intonation, `شما معلم هستید؟` asks “Are you a teacher?” Standard written Persian can also introduce an explicit yes/no question with `آیا` (`āyā`): `آیا شما معلم هستید؟` In ordinary conversation `āyā` is often omitted. It is not the equivalent of English “do”, and the verb does not move to the front.

| Persian | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| آیا | āyā | explicit yes/no question marker |
| چه | che | what; which, in appropriate phrases |
| کی | ki | who; also “when” in some contexts |
| کجا | kojā | where |
| چرا | cherā | why |
| چطور | chetor | how |
| بله | bale | yes |
| نه | na | no |

> **Think first:** You know `این کتاب است.` means “This is a book.” If the identity is unknown, which idea should be replaced by “what”?

Replace the unknown description: `این چیست؟` (`in chist?`), “What is this?” Here `چیست` is a common combined form of `چی` and `است`. Learn it as a useful question while still noticing the role of `ast`. `این` (`in`) means “this”; `آن` (`ān`) means “that”. These words can also stand before nouns: `این کتاب` (`in ketāb`), “this book”, without ezafe after `in`.

### Locate a person

`دوست شما کجاست؟` (`dust-e shomā kojāst?`) means “Where is your friend?” The phrase `dust-e shomā` stays together; it is the person whose location you are asking about. `کجاست` joins `kojā` and `ast`. `او کیست؟` (`u kist?`) asks “Who is she/he?” The question does not specify gender beyond what the context provides.

A learner may know enough vocabulary to understand a question yet need time to answer. Use `ببخشید` (`bebakhshid`), “excuse me”, to reopen the exchange politely. A repair phrase is part of communication, not an admission that the conversation has failed.

### Common trap

Do not reverse subject and verb just because English asks “Are you…?” Also keep the roles of `چه` and `آیا` separate. A yes/no marker does not replace the missing information in “Where is the book?” Question intonation and punctuation help, but the content of the question must still be clear.

### Practice

Ask the following questions in the standard forms taught here.

1. Are you an engineer? Use آیا and respectful address.
2. What is this?
3. Where is your friend? Use respectful address.
4. Who is she?

***Answers:***

1. `آیا شما مهندس هستید؟` - `Āyā shomā mohandes hastid?`
2. `این چیست؟` - `In chist?`
3. `دوست شما کجاست؟` - `Dust-e shomā kojāst?`
4. `او کیست؟` - `U kist?`

### Why these answers work

The first question tests a complete proposition. The other questions replace an unknown identity or location. Recombine by asking about a teacher's location, then ask whether that person is your friend. Keep the respectful choice for “your” consistent throughout the exchange.

## Lesson 5: Make a negative and correct one detail

### Outcome

Deny a description, give a contrasting correction, and answer a question with more than a bare “no”. A useful negative keeps the intended meaning clear instead of merely attaching an English word to Persian.

### The negative of being

For the identity and descriptive sentences learnt so far, use the negative stem `نیست` (`nist`) with the appropriate person ending. `من معلم نیستم.` (`man mo'allem nistam`) means “I am not a teacher.” The negative information is already inside `nistam`; do not add a second word corresponding to English “not”.

| Subject | Negative verb | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| من | نیستم | nistam | I am not |
| تو | نیستی | nisti | you are not, familiar |
| او | نیست | nist | he/she is not |
| ما | نیستیم | nistim | we are not |
| شما | نیستید | nistid | you are not, respectful/plural |
| آنها | نیستند | nistand | they are not |

> **Think first:** Someone has wrongly said that you are a teacher. Which part of `من معلم هستم` changes to deny that? Which part then changes if you add that you are an engineer?

The denial is `من معلم نیستم.` (`man mo'allem nistam`). The positive correction is `من مهندس هستم.` (`man mohandes hastam`). The two clauses have different jobs: one rejects an assumption, the other supplies the correct information. Use `اما` (`ammā`), “but”, when you want an explicit contrast.

### Extend the correction

`این اتاق بزرگ نیست.` (`in otāq bozorg nist`) means “This room is not big.” `این اتاق کوچک است.` (`in otāq kuchek ast`) adds a positive description. The demonstrative `in` remains before `otāq`; the adjective is a predicate here, so it is not joined to the subject by ezafe.

`نه، او پزشک نیست.` (`na, u pezeshk nist`) answers “No, she/he is not a physician.” You can then say `او معلم است.` (`u mo'allem ast`). Both people and objects use the same basic negative strategy in these copular sentences. The verb does not acquire a gender ending.

### Common trap

This lesson does not teach the negative of every Persian verb. `nistam` negates being, not all actions. When you later learn “I go”, its negative will be built differently. Also distinguish the answer `نه` (“no”) from the negative verb: they may appear together in a reply, but they are not interchangeable inside the sentence.

### Practice

Correct the statements using the specified negative.

1. I am not a teacher.
2. We are not university students.
3. This room is not big.
4. You are not Iranian. Use respectful address.

***Answers:***

1. `من معلم نیستم.` - `Man mo'allem nistam.`
2. `ما دانشجو نیستیم.` - `Mā dāneshju nistim.`
3. `این اتاق بزرگ نیست.` - `In otāq bozorg nist.`
4. `شما ایرانی نیستید.` - `Shomā irāni nistid.`

### Why these answers work

Each sentence changes the copula while preserving its person agreement. No separate English-style auxiliary is inserted. Finish with a two-sentence correction about a fictional friend: deny one profession and give another. Then turn the positive statement into a respectful question to that person, retrieving the earlier question pattern as well as today's negative.

## Lesson 6: Possession with dāshtan

### Outcome

Say what you have and ask a listener whether they have an everyday object. Keep two meanings separate: “my book” names an object, whereas “I have a book” states possession.

### A new verb family

The infinitive `داشتن` (`dāshtan`) means “to have”. Its simple present uses the stem `dār` and personal endings. Unlike many ordinary action verbs introduced next, this possession verb does not normally need `mi-` in the simple present. `من کتاب دارم.` (`man ketāb dāram`) means “I have a book” or “I have books”, depending on context; a bare noun need not specify the same definiteness or number as an English translation.

| Persian | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| دارم | dāram | I have |
| داری | dāri | you have, familiar |
| دارد | dārad | he/she has |
| داریم | dārim | we have |
| دارید | dārid | you have, respectful/plural |
| دارند | dārand | they have |
| قلم | qalam | pen |
| وقت | vaqt | time |

> **Think first:** `ما وقت داریم` means “We have time.” Which ending would you use if you ask one unfamiliar person whether they have time?

Use `شما وقت دارید؟` (`shomā vaqt dārid?`). The written question keeps the same word order as a statement. You can add `āyā` for an explicit standard yes/no question, but a spoken question does not require it. The respectful pronoun and the respectful verb ending still travel together.

### Negate possession

Add `na-` to these present forms: `ندارم` (`nadāram`), “I do not have”; `ندارید` (`nadārid`), “you do not have”. `من قلم ندارم.` (`man qalam nadāram`) says you lack a pen. It does not mean that you are not a pen; that mistaken interpretation would come from choosing the negative copula instead of the possession verb.

`دوست من کتاب دارد.` (`dust-e man ketāb dārad`) means “My friend has a book.” Here ezafe connects “friend” and “me”, while `dārad` reports what that friend has. One sentence can use both structures without them competing. `ما یک اتاق داریم.` (`mā yek otāq dārim`) specifies one room, using `یک` (`yek`), “one”.

### Common trap

Do not add `hastam` after `dāram`. The possession verb is already finite: it carries person and present meaning. English “I have” also needs only one finite verb here. The important difference is the position of the Persian object before that verb.

### Practice

State or ask about possession.

1. I have a pen.
2. We do not have time.
3. Does your friend have a book? Use respectful your.
4. You have one room. Use respectful address.

***Answers:***

1. `من قلم دارم.` - `Man qalam dāram.`
2. `ما وقت نداریم.` - `Mā vaqt nadārim.`
3. `دوست شما کتاب دارد؟` - `Dust-e shomā ketāb dārad?`
4. `شما یک اتاق دارید.` - `Shomā yek otāq dārid.`

### Why these answers work

The verb agrees with the possessor, not the possessed object. In answer three, the subject is the singular friend, so the verb is `dārad`, even though `shomā` occurs inside the possessive phrase. Retrieve this distinction by saying “I have a book” and “My friend has my book” as two different ideas; the second also prepares the object-marking distinction coming later.

## Lesson 7: Build everyday actions from a present stem

### Outcome

Describe ordinary actions with a present verb. Learn a verb's present stem as useful information instead of trying to predict it from an English translation.

### Find the reusable parts

Persian verbs have present and past stems. Some are visibly related; others are not predictable enough for a beginner to derive safely. `رفتن` (`raftan`), “to go”, uses the present stem `رو` (`rav`). Its ordinary present forms combine `mi-`, the stem and an ending: `می‌روم` (`miravam`), “I go” or “I am going”, as context requires.

| Form | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| می‌روم | miravam | I go |
| می‌روی | miravi | you go, familiar |
| می‌رود | miravad | he/she goes |
| می‌رویم | miravim | we go |
| می‌روید | miravid | you go, respectful/plural |
| می‌روند | miravand | they go |
| می‌خوانم | mikhānam | I read |
| می‌نویسم | minevisam | I write |

> **Think first:** The ending in `miravam` points to “I”. If the action becomes “read” while the person stays the same, which part should remain and which part must be replaced?

Keep the ending `-am`, but use the present stem `khān`: `می‌خوانم` (`mikhānam`). The infinitive is `خواندن` (`khāndan`). Writing uses `نوشتن` (`neveshtan`) with present stem `نویس` (`nevis`). Store these as meaningful pairs, not as an invented rule that all infinitives lose a fixed set of letters.

### Put the object before the action

`من کتاب می‌خوانم.` (`man ketāb mikhānam`) means “I read books” or “I am reading a book” in an appropriate context. `ما نامه می‌نویسیم.` (`mā nāme minevisim`) means “We write letters” or “We are writing a letter”. `نامه` (`nāme`) means “letter”. The Persian present can cover situations for which English chooses either a simple or progressive present.

To negate these forms, use `نمی‌` (`nemi-`): `من نمی‌روم.` (`man nemiravam`), “I am not going.” The negative is part of the verb expression. It is not built with `nistam`, which you used for identity and description.

### Common trap

Do not mechanically add `mi-` to the possession forms from Lesson 6. Verb classes and uses matter. Also preserve the written separation in `می‌روم`: the zero-width non-joiner prevents the prefix and stem from visually joining, although the spoken verb remains one unit. Ordinary handwriting and digital conventions may vary, but deleting meaningful letters is not a shortcut.

### Practice

Describe the action using the standard written forms.

1. I read a book.
2. We write a letter.
3. She is not going.
4. You read a book. Use respectful address.

***Answers:***

1. `من کتاب می‌خوانم.` - `Man ketāb mikhānam.`
2. `ما نامه می‌نویسیم.` - `Mā nāme minevisim.`
3. `او نمی‌رود.` - `U nemiravad.`
4. `شما کتاب می‌خوانید.` - `Shomā ketāb mikhānid.`

### Why these answers work

Each verb contains a present stem and an ending that matches the subject. The negative in answer three replaces `mi-` with `nemi-`. Transfer by changing “I read” to “we read”, then changing only the action to “we write”. Naming what changed is more valuable than repeating the result without understanding its parts.

## Lesson 8: Mark an identified direct object with rā

### Outcome

Distinguish a general activity from an action involving an identified object. This lesson introduces a frequent use of `را` (`rā`), without pretending that one English article predicts every Persian object-marking choice.

### Compare the messages

`من کتاب می‌خوانم.` can describe book-reading or reading a nonspecific book. If a particular book is already identified, say `من این کتاب را می‌خوانم.` (`man in ketāb rā mikhānam`), “I am reading this book.” `rā` comes after the complete direct-object phrase. It is not the verb, and it does not precede the object like an English preposition.

| Persian | Romanisation | Role or meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| را | rā | marker after an identified/specific direct object |
| این کتاب | in ketāb | this book |
| آن نامه | ān nāme | that letter |
| دوست من | dust-e man | my friend |
| می‌بینم | mibinam | I see |
| می‌بینید | mibinid | you see, respectful/plural |
| می‌خواند | mikhānad | he/she reads |
| می‌نویسد | minevisad | he/she writes |

> **Think first:** In “I see my friend”, is `my` part of the person being seen? If so, should `rā` split “friend” from “my”, or follow the whole phrase?

It follows the complete phrase: `من دوست خود را می‌بینم.` (`man dust-e khod rā mibinam`) means “I see my own friend.” `خود` (`khod`) points back to the subject. For an uncomplicated phrase with an explicitly named possessor, `من دوست شما را می‌بینم.` (`man dust-e shomā rā mibinam`) means “I see your friend.” The marker comes after `shomā`, not between the noun and its possessor.

### Use an object without a visible subject

`این نامه را می‌خوانم.` (`in nāme rā mikhānam`) means “I am reading this letter.” The ending already indicates “I”, so the pronoun is not obligatory. Keeping or omitting it can depend on emphasis and context. The course often includes pronouns for clarity, not because every natural Persian sentence must begin with one.

A noun's English translation may use “the” even where Persian uses no separate article. Do not look for a hidden Persian “the” before every noun. `rā` marks a relationship involving a direct object; it is not a universal definite article and does not belong after every definite noun.

### Common trap

In “My friend is a teacher”, “my friend” is the subject, not a direct object, so this lesson does not license adding `rā` to it. Identify what the verb does to which participant before deciding on object marking. More subtle specificity distinctions require wider exposure and later review.

### Practice

Use rā with the identified direct object.

1. I read this book.
2. She reads that letter.
3. We see your friend. Use respectful your.
4. You write this letter. Use respectful address.

***Answers:***

1. `من این کتاب را می‌خوانم.` - `Man in ketāb rā mikhānam.`
2. `او آن نامه را می‌خواند.` - `U ān nāme rā mikhānad.`
3. `ما دوست شما را می‌بینیم.` - `Mā dust-e shomā rā mibinim.`
4. `شما این نامه را می‌نویسید.` - `Shomā in nāme rā minevisid.`

### Why these answers work

The entire object is completed before `rā`, and the final verb still agrees with the subject. Retrieve the contrast by saying “I read books” and “I read this book”, then explain why only the second model here uses the marker.

## Lesson 9: Place, origin and destination

### Outcome

Say where someone is, where they go and where they come from. The same place can play different roles, and the preposition signals which relationship you mean.

### Choose the relationship first

`در` (`dar`) locates something “in” or “at”; `به` (`be`) commonly introduces a destination; `از` (`az`) introduces a source or origin. `با` (`bā`) means “with”. Their exact uses do not match every English preposition, so learn them as parts of meaningful phrases.

| Persian | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| در خانه | dar khāne | at home |
| به خانه | be khāne | homeward, to the house |
| از خانه | az khāne | from home |
| با دوست من | bā dust-e man | with my friend |
| دانشگاه | dāneshgāh | university |
| بازار | bāzār | market |
| تهران | Tehrān | Tehran |
| هند | Hend | India |

> **Think first:** “I am at home” and “I am going home” contain the same place. Do they describe the same relationship between the person and that place?

No. Location uses `من در خانه هستم.` (`man dar khāne hastam`). Destination uses `من به خانه می‌روم.` (`man be khāne miravam`). The first has a copula; the second has a movement verb. Selecting a preposition and selecting a verb are connected meaning decisions, not separate vocabulary exercises.

### Expand a sentence one phrase at a time

`ما به دانشگاه می‌رویم.` (`mā be dāneshgāh miravim`) means “We are going to the university.” Add a companion with `bā`: `من با دوستم به بازار می‌روم.` (`man bā dustam be bāzār miravam`), “I am going to the market with my friend.” `دوستم` (`dustam`) is a compact possessive form; for now recognise it as “my friend”. Possessive suffixes are studied more fully later.

`من از هند هستم.` (`man az Hend hastam`) gives your origin. It does not describe a current journey. `او در تهران است.` (`u dar Tehrān ast`) gives someone's location. Ask about that location with `او کجاست؟` (`u kojāst?`), retrieving the question pattern from Lesson 4.

### Common trap

An English speaker may insert `rā` after a destination because the place follows the English verb “go”. But a destination introduced by `be` is not the direct object marked in Lesson 8. Persian grammatical roles are not determined by the word's position in an English translation.

`خانه` and `دانشگاه` often occur without an article in these phrases. Adding `یک` changes the message toward “one/a house” or “one/a university”; it is not a required filler whenever English uses “the”.

### Practice

Choose the relationship and the appropriate verb.

1. I am at home.
2. We are going to the market.
3. She is in Tehran.
4. I am from India.

***Answers:***

1. `من در خانه هستم.` - `Man dar khāne hastam.`
2. `ما به بازار می‌رویم.` - `Mā be bāzār miravim.`
3. `او در تهران است.` - `U dar Tehrān ast.`
4. `من از هند هستم.` - `Man az Hend hastam.`

### Why these answers work

Location, destination and origin each receive a different preposition. The verb tells us whether the sentence states a position or an action. For retrieval, give your origin, current location and next destination as three short sentences. Replace actual place names with `home`, `market` and `university` if you do not yet know their Persian forms.

## Lesson 10: Count objects without adding a plural everywhere

### Outcome

Count a small number of things and recognise a common plural ending. Persian does not need the same plural marking after numerals as English.

### Numbers supply information

In `دو کتاب` (`do ketāb`), “two books”, the numeral already specifies the number. The noun remains in its unmarked form. This is a useful contrast with English “two book-s”. It does not mean Persian lacks plural nouns; it means the language distributes information differently across the phrase.

| Persian | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| یک | yek | one |
| دو | do | two |
| سه | se | three |
| چهار | chahār | four |
| پنج | panj | five |
| شش | shesh | six |
| هفت | haft | seven |
| هشت | hasht | eight |
| نه | noh | nine |
| ده | dah | ten |

> **Think first:** You can say `من کتاب دارم`. If you now want to specify three books, where can you place that number without changing the person of the verb?

Say `من سه کتاب دارم.` (`man se ketāb dāram`). `Dāram` still agrees with “I”, not with the number of books. This sentence tests two separate ideas: the numeral belongs to the object phrase, and the verb ending belongs to the subject.

### Recognise plural nouns

The suffix `ها` (`hā`) is a productive plural marker: `کتاب‌ها` (`ketābhā`), “books”; `اتاق‌ها` (`otāqhā`), “rooms”. When the plural noun is followed by ezafe, you hear `-ye`: `کتاب‌های من` (`ketābhā-ye man`), “my books”. The extra linking sound connects the expanded plural noun to its owner.

Persian also has other plural patterns, particularly with human nouns and borrowed vocabulary. Do not assume every plural encountered in reading will end in `hā`. For this foundation, use the regular pattern where it is appropriate and learn common exceptions as they arise.

With inanimate plural subjects, singular verb agreement can be natural in Persian. That is a separate topic from counting objects, so avoid inventing a universal “plural noun requires plural verb” rule from English. In this lesson's exercises the subject is a person and the counted things are objects, keeping the agreement decision clear.

### Common trap

`نه` pronounced `noh` means “nine”, while `نه` pronounced `na` means “no”. Short vowels are not normally written, so context and pronunciation distinguish them. This is a concrete reason to learn words with audio or a reading guide rather than guessing from letters alone.

### Practice

Count the object while keeping the correct possession verb.

1. I have two books.
2. We have three rooms.
3. She has one pen.
4. You have five books. Use respectful address.

***Answers:***

1. `من دو کتاب دارم.` - `Man do ketāb dāram.`
2. `ما سه اتاق داریم.` - `Mā se otāq dārim.`
3. `او یک قلم دارد.` - `U yek qalam dārad.`
4. `شما پنج کتاب دارید.` - `Shomā panj ketāb dārid.`

### Why these answers work

The counted noun remains unmarked after the numeral in these basic phrases. The possession verb changes with the possessor. Retrieve the pattern by making a small inventory of a desk, then saying which objects belong to you using an ezafe phrase. Keep counting and possession distinct even when you combine them.

## Lesson 11: Put routines on a timeline

### Outcome

Describe a repeated activity and distinguish “today” from “every day”. A present verb becomes much more useful when you can place its action in time.

### The time phrase does not replace the verb

`من هر روز کتاب می‌خوانم.` (`man har ruz ketāb mikhānam`) means “I read books every day.” The phrase `هر روز` supplies repetition; `mikhānam` still supplies the action and the speaker's person. A time phrase often appears toward the beginning of a neutral sentence, although Persian word order can vary with emphasis.

| Persian | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| امروز | emruz | today |
| فردا | fardā | tomorrow |
| دیروز | diruz | yesterday |
| هر روز | har ruz | every day |
| صبح | sobh | morning |
| شب | shab | night |
| الآن | al'ān | now |
| معمولاً | ma'mulan | usually |

> **Think first:** Does adding `هر روز` to “I read” change the person who reads? Should it therefore change `-am` to a different person ending?

No. It changes when and how regularly the action occurs, not the person. `ما هر روز می‌نویسیم.` (`mā har ruz minevisim`) means “We write every day”; the `-im` is chosen because the subject is “we”. Keep separate the decisions about the participant, action and time.

### Ask about a routine

`شما صبح چه می‌خوانید؟` (`shomā sobh che mikhānid?`) asks “What do you read in the morning?” `چه` occupies the information role that the answer will fill. `من صبح کتاب می‌خوانم.` (`man sobh ketāb mikhānam`) supplies one possible answer. The question does not require English “do” or a reversed Persian verb order.

`او شب به خانه می‌رود.` (`u shab be khāne miravad`) describes someone going home at night. If you change `shab` to `emruz`, the destination and the person stay the same, but the time reference changes. Read the whole sentence after replacing a phrase, so you do not accidentally leave behind a conflicting English time assumption.

Persian present forms can refer to an arranged or contextually clear future: `فردا به دانشگاه می‌روم.` (`fardā be dāneshgāh miravam`), “I am going to the university tomorrow.” This does not mean all time distinctions disappear. You will learn dedicated past forms next, and later compare ways of expressing future meaning.

### Common trap

“Usually” does not mean “always”, and “today” does not mean “every day”. Avoid silently upgrading a person's statement about one occasion into a general habit. When reading a dialogue, underline the time phrase before deciding which English tense sounds natural.

### Practice

Combine the action with the supplied time.

1. I read books every day.
2. We go to the university in the morning.
3. She writes a letter today.
4. I am going home tomorrow.

***Answers:***

1. `من هر روز کتاب می‌خوانم.` - `Man har ruz ketāb mikhānam.`
2. `ما صبح به دانشگاه می‌رویم.` - `Mā sobh be dāneshgāh miravim.`
3. `او امروز نامه می‌نویسد.` - `U emruz nāme minevisad.`
4. `من فردا به خانه می‌روم.` - `Man fardā be khāne miravam.`

### Why these answers work

Time phrases extend previously learnt frames without changing their basic agreement. The last answer uses a present form with explicit future context. For retrieval, describe one habit and one plan, then explain which word tells your listener that they should not interpret the two sentences in the same way.

## Lesson 12: Use a compound verb to work and talk

### Outcome

Recognise an everyday Persian compound verb and change only its verbal part when the subject changes. This opens many useful meanings without treating every expression as a completely unrelated conjugation.

### One meaning, more than one written word

`کار کردن` (`kār kardan`) means “to work”. The nonverbal element `کار` supplies the activity, and the verb `کردن` supplies the conjugation. In the present, `kardan` uses the stem `kon`: `کار می‌کنم` (`kār mikonam`), “I work”.

| Persian | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| کار کردن | kār kardan | to work |
| صحبت کردن | sohbat kardan | to talk |
| کمک کردن | komak kardan | to help |
| زندگی کردن | zendegi kardan | to live |
| می‌کنم | mikonam | I do |
| می‌کنیم | mikonim | we do |
| می‌کنید | mikonid | you do, respectful/plural |
| شرکت | sherkat | company |

> **Think first:** If `کار می‌کنم` means “I work”, what would change to describe “we work”? Does the noun-like element `kār` need a new person ending too?

Say `کار می‌کنیم` (`kār mikonim`). The verbal part changes; the nonverbal element stays. `ما در شرکت کار می‌کنیم.` (`mā dar sherkat kār mikonim`) means “We work at the company.” You have combined location, a subject and a compound action.

### Choose the companion or recipient

`من با دوستم صحبت می‌کنم.` (`man bā dustam sohbat mikonam`) means “I talk with my friend.” Here `bā` introduces the person you talk with. `من به شما کمک می‌کنم.` (`man be shomā komak mikonam`) means “I help you.” The useful frame for this help expression includes `be`; do not automatically attach `rā` just because English “help you” has a direct object.

`او در تهران زندگی می‌کند.` (`u dar Tehrān zendegi mikonad`) says “She/he lives in Tehran.” The expression does not require a second form of “to be”. Its ending already states the third-person action. Negation attaches to the verbal portion: `من امروز کار نمی‌کنم.` (`man emruz kār nemikonam`), “I am not working today.”

### Common trap

The existence of productive compound verbs does not mean you can combine any Persian noun with `kardan` and expect a natural verb. Learn an attested expression together with its argument pattern: who talks with whom, who helps whom, who lives where. This gives you a reusable structure without licensing invented vocabulary.

Another trap is moving `mi-` in front of the nonverbal part. In the written forms here, it stays with the conjugated verb. The compound meaning crosses a space; the spelling does not make the prefix free to move anywhere.

### Practice

Use the compound expressions taught above.

1. I work at home.
2. We live in Tehran.
3. She helps you. Use respectful you.
4. I am not working today.

***Answers:***

1. `من در خانه کار می‌کنم.` - `Man dar khāne kār mikonam.`
2. `ما در تهران زندگی می‌کنیم.` - `Mā dar Tehrān zendegi mikonim.`
3. `او به شما کمک می‌کند.` - `U be shomā komak mikonad.`
4. `من امروز کار نمی‌کنم.` - `Man emruz kār nemikonam.`

### Why these answers work

The first element selects a conventional compound meaning. The final conjugated part agrees with the subject and carries the negative when needed. Transfer by saying where you work and where a fictional friend lives, then ask that friend a respectful question about their work.

## Lesson 13: Say what you want, then name the action

### Outcome

Express a desire for a thing or for an action, and use a small, useful set of subjunctive forms after “want”. The grammatical label is less important than seeing why the second verb changes shape.

### Wanting a thing

`خواستن` (`khāstan`), “to want”, has present stem `خواه` (`khāh`). `من چای می‌خواهم.` (`man chāy mikhāham`) means “I want tea.” Standard written `mikhāham` is often shortened in colloquial Iranian speech; the written model here keeps the full form so its structure is visible.

| Persian | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| چای | chāy | tea |
| آب | āb | water |
| می‌خواهم | mikhāham | I want |
| می‌خواهید | mikhāhid | you want, respectful/plural |
| بروم | beravam | that I go; to go after “I want” |
| بخوانم | bekhānam | that I read |
| بنویسم | benevisam | that I write |
| لطفاً | lotfan | please |

> **Think first:** In “I want to go”, is going being asserted as an action already happening, or is it the action that is wanted?

It is wanted. Use `من می‌خواهم بروم.` (`man mikhāham beravam`). The second verb takes a subjunctive form in this construction. For these verbs, the positive subjunctive uses a `be-/bo-` prefix, a present stem and a personal ending. Do not take that brief pattern as a claim that every Persian subjunctive is entirely regular.

### Keep the two subjects clear

`ما می‌خواهیم برویم.` (`mā mikhāhim beravim`) means “We want to go.” Both verbs refer to the same group, so both carry the first-person plural ending. `من می‌خواهم این کتاب را بخوانم.` (`man mikhāham in ketāb rā bekhānam`) means “I want to read this book.” The object phrase belongs with reading, and `rā` still follows the complete identified object.

When the desired action has a different subject, Persian can make that person explicit. This requires attention to both verb endings, so do not replace one with an English infinitive and forget who will perform the action. The present lesson's exercises keep the same subject in both clauses to establish the pattern first.

### Common trap

`می‌خواهم` is a direct expression of desire. Adding `lotfan` can make a practical request more courteous, but it does not automatically suit every social situation. Tone and context matter. You will meet more courteous request frames later.

Do not use `می‌روم` after “I want” as if Persian were merely joining two independent present statements. Here `beravam` marks the action as desired, not asserted. Say the contrast: `miravam`, I go; `mikhāham beravam`, I want to go.

### Practice

Express a desire using the standard written forms.

1. I want water.
2. We want to go home.
3. I want to read this letter.
4. You want to write. Use respectful address.

***Answers:***

1. `من آب می‌خواهم.` - `Man āb mikhāham.`
2. `ما می‌خواهیم به خانه برویم.` - `Mā mikhāhim be khāne beravim.`
3. `من می‌خواهم این نامه را بخوانم.` - `Man mikhāham in nāme rā bekhānam.`
4. `شما می‌خواهید بنویسید.` - `Shomā mikhāhid benevisid.`

### Why these answers work

Wanting a noun uses a single conjugated verb. Wanting an action introduces a second verb with the subjunctive shape taught here. Retrieve the pattern in a new setting: imagine leaving a library. Say what you want to read before going home, using two short statements if one long sentence feels difficult.

## Lesson 14: Ability and necessity

### Outcome

Distinguish wanting, being able and needing to act. Similar-looking sentence patterns can express very different commitments.

### Ability uses its own verb

`توانستن` (`tavānestan`) means “to be able”. `می‌توانم` (`mitavānam`) says “I can”. As with wanting an action, the following verb takes a subjunctive form: `من می‌توانم بخوانم.` (`man mitavānam bekhānam`), “I can read.” This is a statement of ability, not a promise that you will read now.

| Persian | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| می‌توانم | mitavānam | I can |
| می‌توانی | mitavāni | you can, familiar |
| می‌تواند | mitavānad | he/she can |
| می‌توانیم | mitavānim | we can |
| نمی‌توانم | nemitavānam | I cannot |
| باید | bāyad | must, should; necessity |
| نباید | nabāyad | must not, should not |
| بخوانید | bekhānid | that you read, respectful/plural |

> **Think first:** If you cannot go, which part should be negated: the verb that states ability or the verb that names going?

Negate ability: `من نمی‌توانم بروم.` (`man nemitavānam beravam`). The action remains the complement. This is different from a sentence meaning that you are able not to go. Small changes in the placement of negative meaning can produce different practical messages.

### Necessity is not conjugated like ability

`باید` does not take a different person ending in this frame. The following verb shows the person: `من باید بروم.` (`man bāyad beravam`), “I must go”; `ما باید برویم.` (`mā bāyad beravim`), “We must go”. You may also hear a softer “should” interpretation depending on context. Do not assume the English strength of the instruction from the dictionary gloss alone.

`شما می‌توانید این کتاب را بخوانید.` (`shomā mitavānid in ketāb rā bekhānid`) means “You can read this book.” The respectful form appears on both finite verbs. `او باید به خانه برود.` (`u bāyad be khāne beravad`) identifies the person who needs to go home through the final ending.

### Common trap

“Must not” is not “do not have to”. `نباید` expresses a negative obligation or recommendation. It should not be used when you merely want to say that an action is optional. For absence of necessity, one useful later expression is `لازم نیست` (`lāzem nist`), “it is not necessary”. Keep the meanings distinct before expanding the grammar.

Likewise, a respectful question using `mitavānid` can serve as a request in context, but the literal ability meaning remains available. Listen for whether someone is asking about capability or asking you to perform the action.

### Practice

Choose ability, inability or necessity.

1. I can write.
2. We cannot go.
3. I must read this letter.
4. She must go home.

***Answers:***

1. `من می‌توانم بنویسم.` - `Man mitavānam benevisam.`
2. `ما نمی‌توانیم برویم.` - `Mā nemitavānim beravim.`
3. `من باید این نامه را بخوانم.` - `Man bāyad in nāme rā bekhānam.`
4. `او باید به خانه برود.` - `U bāyad be khāne beravad.`

### Why these answers work

Ability is conjugated and can be negated; `bāyad` stays fixed while its following verb identifies the person. Retrieve all three meanings for one action: I want to write, I can write, I must write. Explain why a listener should not treat those statements as interchangeable.

## Lesson 15: Report a completed action in the past

### Outcome

Say what happened yesterday using a simple past stem. Learn the new stem relationship while reusing personal endings that are already familiar.

### The past stem is a useful partner

For many infinitives, removing final `ن` (`-an` in the infinitive pronunciation) gives the past stem: `رفتن` (`raftan`) → `رفت` (`raft`), “go”; `خواندن` (`khāndan`) → `خواند` (`khānd`), “read”. Add past personal endings: `رفتم` (`raftam`), “I went”; `رفتیم` (`raftim`), “we went”.

| Infinitive | Past stem | First-person past | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| رفتن | رفت | رفتم / raftam | I went |
| خواندن | خواند | خواندم / khāndam | I read, past |
| نوشتن | نوشت | نوشتم / neveshtam | I wrote |
| دیدن | دید | دیدم / didam | I saw |
| کردن | کرد | کردم / kardam | I did |
| داشتن | داشت | داشتم / dāshtam | I had |

> **Think first:** You know `می‌نویسم` means “I write”. Does the spelling of its present stem guarantee that the past will be built from `nevis`?

No. The past stem is `nevesht`, so “I wrote” is `نوشتم` (`neveshtam`). Present and past stems are two pieces of lexical information. Seeing a relationship in one verb should not lead you to manufacture the wrong stem for another.

### The third person has no added ending here

`او رفت.` (`u raft`) means “She/he went.” Compare `من رفتم` and `ما رفتیم`. For the basic third-person singular past, the stem itself is the verb form. Do not add the present ending `-ad` to make an invented past form.

`من دیروز به بازار رفتم.` (`man diruz be bāzār raftam`) reports a completed visit yesterday. `او این نامه را خواند.` (`u in nāme rā khānd`) says someone read this identified letter. The object marker does not disappear because the tense changes. `ما در خانه کار کردیم.` (`mā dar khāne kār kardim`) places the compound verb “work” in the past by changing its verbal element.

### Common trap

English spells present “read” and past “read” alike, although pronunciation changes. Persian distinguishes `mikhānam` and `khāndam` visibly. Do not let an ambiguous English prompt erase a tense distinction you can identify from context.

For the negative simple past, add `na-`: `نرفتم` (`naraftam`), “I did not go”. You do not need a Persian word corresponding to English “did” plus a separate unconjugated verb. The negative and past information are contained in the verb form itself.

### Practice

Report a completed past event.

1. I went home yesterday.
2. We wrote this letter.
3. She saw your friend. Use respectful your.
4. I did not go to the market.

***Answers:***

1. `من دیروز به خانه رفتم.` - `Man diruz be khāne raftam.`
2. `ما این نامه را نوشتیم.` - `Mā in nāme rā neveshtim.`
3. `او دوست شما را دید.` - `U dust-e shomā rā did.`
4. `من به بازار نرفتم.` - `Man be bāzār naraftam.`

### Why these answers work

Each verb uses a past stem. The plural subject in answer two adds `-im`, while the third-person singular in answer three uses bare `did`. End by contrasting a daily routine with one completed event: “I read every day” and “I read this letter yesterday.” Identify which part of each sentence carries the tense.

## Lesson 16: Describe what used to happen

### Outcome

Describe a repeated or ongoing past activity and contrast it with one completed event. The choice is about how you present an event, not simply whether it happened before today.

### Add a viewpoint to the past

`رفتم` (`raftam`) reports “I went”. `می‌رفتم` (`miraftam`) can describe “I used to go” or “I was going”, depending on the surrounding sentence. The imperfective prefix `mi-` combines with the past stem and past ending. You have met this prefix in present forms, but the stem makes the time relationship different.

| Persian | Romanisation | Meaning in a suitable context |
| --- | --- | --- |
| می‌رفتم | miraftam | I used to go; I was going |
| می‌خواندم | mikhāndam | I used to read; I was reading |
| می‌نوشت | minevesht | he/she was writing |
| کار می‌کردیم | kār mikardim | we used to work |
| قبلاً | qablan | previously, before |
| وقتی | vaqti | when |
| آمد | āmad | he/she came |
| همیشه | hamishe | always |

> **Think first:** If you want to describe your routine at an earlier stage of life, does one bare “I went” communicate the same viewpoint as “I used to go every day”?

Use the repeated-past pattern: `قبلاً هر روز به دانشگاه می‌رفتم.` (`qablan har ruz be dāneshgāh miraftam`), “Previously, I used to go to the university every day.” The time phrase and repeated-activity form reinforce one another. Neither requires inventing a second past verb corresponding to English “used”.

### Background and event

`وقتی او آمد، من کتاب می‌خواندم.` (`vaqti u āmad, man ketāb mikhāndam`) means “When she/he came, I was reading a book.” The arrival is presented as an event; the reading supplies the background. The two verbs describe different viewpoints even though both situations are in the past.

`ما قبلاً در خانه کار می‌کردیم.` (`mā qablan dar khāne kār mikardim`) describes an earlier work pattern. It does not by itself specify exactly when that pattern ended. English “used to” often suggests a contrast with now; context decides how strongly Persian makes that contrast.

For a negative repeated past, use `نمی‌`: `من شب کار نمی‌کردم.` (`man shab kār nemikardam`), “I did not used to work at night” or “I was not working at night”, as context permits. Compare the completed-event negative `کار نکردم` (`kār nakardam`), “I did not work” on a particular occasion.

### Common trap

Do not translate every English past continuous form by matching words individually. Persian has additional ways of making an ongoing activity explicit, and the form introduced here has both habitual and ongoing uses. A time phrase and the surrounding event usually make the intended reading much clearer than an isolated verb chart.

### Practice

Use the repeated or ongoing past pattern.

1. Previously, I used to read every day.
2. We used to work at home.
3. She was writing a letter.
4. I did not used to work at night.

***Answers:***

1. `قبلاً هر روز می‌خواندم.` - `Qablan har ruz mikhāndam.`
2. `ما در خانه کار می‌کردیم.` - `Mā dar khāne kār mikardim.`
3. `او نامه می‌نوشت.` - `U nāme minevesht.`
4. `من شب کار نمی‌کردم.` - `Man shab kār nemikardam.`

### Why these answers work

The past stem establishes past time, while `mi-` or `nemi-` presents an ongoing or repeated activity. Retrieve the contrast by describing your former morning routine and one specific event yesterday. Choose the viewpoint before choosing the verb form.

## Lesson 17: Connect a past action to the present

### Outcome

Recognise and form a basic present perfect, such as “I have read this book”. Do not confuse the possession verb “have” with the English auxiliary used in this tense.

### Build a participle

A common past participle is formed from the past stem plus `ه` pronounced `-e`: `خوانده` (`khānde`), “read”; `رفته` (`rafte`), “gone”. The present perfect combines this participle with a copular ending. `خوانده‌ام` (`khānde-am`) means “I have read”. The written zero-width non-joiner keeps the parts legible without turning the expression into an English-style two-word “have read”.

| Persian | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| خوانده‌ام | khānde-am | I have read |
| خوانده‌ای | khānde-i | you have read, familiar |
| خوانده است | khānde ast | he/she has read |
| خوانده‌ایم | khānde-im | we have read |
| خوانده‌اید | khānde-id | you have read, respectful/plural |
| خوانده‌اند | khānde-and | they have read |
| نوشته | neveshte | written |
| دیده | dide | seen |

> **Think first:** The English phrase “I have read” contains “have”. Should you therefore use the Persian possession form `dāram` before the past stem?

No. That would mix two different grammatical systems. Use the participle plus the appropriate copular ending: `من این کتاب را خوانده‌ام.` (`man in ketāb rā khānde-am`), “I have read this book.” The possessive `dāram` is not the auxiliary of this Persian construction.

### Choose a useful context

Imagine someone offering a book. Saying that you have read it connects a previous reading to the present conversation. `ما این فیلم را دیده‌ایم.` (`mā in film rā dide-im`) similarly says “We have seen this film.” `فیلم` (`film`) is a familiar loanword, but its Persian grammar still follows the local pattern.

`او به خانه رفته است.` (`u be khāne rafte ast`) means “She/he has gone home.” Depending on context, that can explain a present absence. Do not overstate it as a guaranteed claim about their current location; language and real-world inference are different things.

Negation attaches to the participial verb: `نخوانده‌ام` (`nakhānde-am`), “I have not read”. The form `خوانده نیستم` is not the present-perfect pattern being taught here. Keep the tense assembly intact instead of replacing the final element with whichever negative expression you last memorised.

### Common trap

English and Persian do not divide all past situations identically. These models establish a common use, not a one-to-one translation algorithm. In conversation you may hear shortened endings; first recognise the standard written structure, then compare an actual spoken example rather than guessing contractions.

### Practice

Use the standard present perfect.

1. I have read this letter.
2. We have seen this film.
3. She has gone home.
4. I have not written this letter.

***Answers:***

1. `من این نامه را خوانده‌ام.` - `Man in nāme rā khānde-am.`
2. `ما این فیلم را دیده‌ایم.` - `Mā in film rā dide-im.`
3. `او به خانه رفته است.` - `U be khāne rafte ast.`
4. `من این نامه را ننوشته‌ام.` - `Man in nāme rā naneveshte-am.`

### Why these answers work

Each uses a past participle and a present copular ending. Answer four negates the participle rather than using the possession verb. Retrieve by contrasting “I have a book”, “I read a book yesterday” and “I have read this book”. Explain why the three English occurrences of have/read do not lead to the same Persian construction.

## Lesson 18: Make plans without confusing want and will

### Outcome

Express a future plan using a present form with a time phrase, and recognise the more formal future construction. Choose the register deliberately.

### A familiar present can describe a plan

`فردا به بازار می‌روم.` (`fardā be bāzār miravam`) naturally expresses “I am going to the market tomorrow.” The time phrase makes the future reference explicit. You do not need a dedicated future auxiliary for every plan.

| Persian | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| فردا | fardā | tomorrow |
| هفتهٔ آینده | hafte-ye āyande | next week |
| امشب | emshab | tonight |
| بعداً | ba'dan | later |
| خواهم رفت | khāham raft | I will go, formal future |
| خواهیم رفت | khāhim raft | we will go, formal future |
| خواهد نوشت | khāhad nevesht | he/she will write |
| خواهد بود | khāhad bud | he/she/it will be |

> **Think first:** You learnt `می‌خواهم بروم`, “I want to go”. Compare it with `خواهم رفت`, “I will go”. Which two visible differences stop these expressions from being identical?

The desire form has `mi-` on “want” and a following subjunctive `beravam`. The formal future has a future auxiliary `khāham` followed by the short infinitive, here identical to the past stem `raft`. The second part does not receive a new first-person ending. `خواهم رفتم` is therefore not the pattern.

### Read both registers

`ما فردا می‌رویم.` (`mā fardā miravim`) is a straightforward plan. `ما فردا خواهیم رفت.` (`mā fardā khāhim raft`) states the future in a more formal style. Neither sentence proves that the plan is certain to occur; grammar expresses the speaker's presentation, not a guarantee about events.

`او هفتهٔ آینده این نامه را خواهد نوشت.` (`u hafte-ye āyande in nāme rā khāhad nevesht`) means “She/he will write this letter next week.” The identified direct object remains before its marker, and the auxiliary carries the person. A larger sentence does not erase the relationships established in earlier lessons.

For everyday production, use the present-plus-time pattern confidently when it fits. Learn the formal future so that notices, announcements and written plans are comprehensible. Learning two registers is useful; mixing their forms indiscriminately is not.

### Common trap

Do not mistake a sentence about wanting for a firm statement about a future action. “I want to go tomorrow” leaves open whether the trip is arranged or possible. In a real exchange, ask a follow-up if the practical difference matters. Meaning is not settled by seeing the letters `خواه` somewhere in a verb.

### Practice

Use the future expression requested.

1. I am going home tomorrow. Use present plus time.
2. We will go tomorrow. Use the formal future.
3. She will write this letter. Use the formal future.
4. I want to go tomorrow. Express desire.

***Answers:***

1. `من فردا به خانه می‌روم.` - `Man fardā be khāne miravam.`
2. `ما فردا خواهیم رفت.` - `Mā fardā khāhim raft.`
3. `او این نامه را خواهد نوشت.` - `U in nāme rā khāhad nevesht.`
4. `من می‌خواهم فردا بروم.` - `Man mikhāham fardā beravam.`

### Why these answers work

The requested meaning and register determine the construction. The formal future marks person on its auxiliary; desire uses the subjunctive complement. Retrieve by making one realistic plan, one wish and one statement in the style of a formal announcement. Keep the difference audible.

## Lesson 19: Ask someone to act courteously

### Outcome

Make a simple respectful request and recognise why a grammatical imperative can sound different depending on address and situation.

### A request can name an action directly

For many verbs, a positive imperative uses `be-/bo-` plus the present stem. The respectful or plural form commonly adds `-id`: `بخوانید` (`bekhānid`), “read”; `بنویسید` (`benevisid`), “write”. These resemble subjunctive forms you have already used, but their function in a direct request is different.

| Persian | Romanisation | Meaning in a request |
| --- | --- | --- |
| لطفاً | lotfan | please |
| بخوانید | bekhānid | read, respectful/plural |
| بنویسید | benevisid | write, respectful/plural |
| بیایید | biyāyid | come, respectful/plural |
| بروید | beravid | go, respectful/plural |
| صبر کنید | sabr konid | wait, respectful/plural |
| تکرار کنید | tekrār konid | repeat, respectful/plural |
| آهسته | āheste | slowly |

> **Think first:** If you are asking an unfamiliar person to repeat something, which is more appropriate for this course's default address: a familiar singular command or the `-id` form?

Use `لطفاً تکرار کنید.` (`lotfan tekrār konid`), “Please repeat.” In this compound expression, the imperative of the verbal part is `konid`, without an added `be-`. This is why you should learn common request expressions as supported patterns instead of assuming every command is formed by one completely exceptionless prefix rule.

### Give the request an object

`لطفاً این نامه را بخوانید.` (`lotfan in nāme rā bekhānid`) means “Please read this letter.” The request's implied subject is the listener; the identified letter is still a direct object. `لطفاً آهسته صحبت کنید.` (`lotfan āheste sohbat konid`) asks “Please speak slowly.” This is a practical repair tool when comprehension is difficult.

You can also frame a request as an ability question: `می‌توانید تکرار کنید؟` (`mitavānid tekrār konid?`), “Can you repeat?” The literal question concerns ability, but in context it often asks for the action. Adding `lotfan` is possible. No single form guarantees politeness: urgency, tone and the relationship between speakers all matter.

### Common trap

A direct request to an elder or stranger may sound abrupt if you use the familiar singular ending by accident. Also, `بروید` means “go”, not “come”. When asking someone to move, choose the viewpoint before choosing the verb. A courteous wrong-direction instruction still sends the listener to the wrong place.

Negative commands use their own negative forms; do not attach a free-standing English “not” to the positive imperative. For example, `نروید` (`naravid`) means “do not go” in respectful/plural address. Practise this as a whole contrast with `beravid`.

### Practice

Make a respectful request.

1. Please read this book.
2. Please repeat.
3. Please speak slowly.
4. Do not go. Use respectful address.

***Answers:***

1. `لطفاً این کتاب را بخوانید.` - `Lotfan in ketāb rā bekhānid.`
2. `لطفاً تکرار کنید.` - `Lotfan tekrār konid.`
3. `لطفاً آهسته صحبت کنید.` - `Lotfan āheste sohbat konid.`
4. `نروید.` - `Naravid.`

### Why these answers work

The request addresses the listener, and the respectful forms preserve the chosen relationship. The compound verb's nonverbal part remains stable. Retrieve the patterns by imagining a library interaction: ask someone to wait, read a short note and repeat an unfamiliar word. Keep each request separate and manageable.

## Lesson 20: Compare two things without changing the adjective for gender

### Outcome

Make a basic comparison and express a preference between two options. Persian adjectives do not acquire masculine and feminine endings in these constructions.

### Build a comparison

The comparative suffix `تر` (`-tar`) turns many adjectives into a “more …” form. `بزرگ` (`bozorg`) becomes `بزرگ‌تر` (`bozorgtar`), “bigger”. Use `از` (`az`) to introduce the comparison standard: `این اتاق از آن اتاق بزرگ‌تر است.` (`in otāq az ān otāq bozorgtar ast`), “This room is bigger than that room.”

| Persian | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| بزرگ‌تر | bozorgtar | bigger |
| کوچک‌تر | kuchektar | smaller |
| ارزان | arzān | inexpensive |
| ارزان‌تر | arzāntar | cheaper |
| گران | gerān | expensive |
| گران‌تر | gerāntar | more expensive |
| بهتر | behtar | better |
| بهترین | behtarin | best |

> **Think first:** “This book is cheaper than that book” compares price. Which element tells the listener what quality is compared, and which element introduces the other book?

`arzāntar` supplies the comparative quality; `az ān ketāb` introduces the other book. The full sentence is `این کتاب از آن کتاب ارزان‌تر است.` (`in ketāb az ān ketāb arzāntar ast`). You do not need a gender ending because `ketāb` is not assigned masculine/feminine adjective agreement in Persian.

### Learn an irregular form as a useful whole

The ordinary comparative of “good” is `بهتر` (`behtar`), not a mechanically invented form based only on `khub`. `این کتاب بهتر است.` (`in ketāb behtar ast`) means “This book is better.” A listener may infer the comparison from context even when you do not repeat “than that book”.

The superlative suffix `-tarin` appears before the noun in a phrase such as `بزرگ‌ترین اتاق` (`bozorgtarin otāq`), “the biggest room”. That order differs from a normal noun-plus-adjective ezafe phrase. Compare `اتاق بزرگ` (`otāq-e bozorg`), “the big room”, with the superlative expression. Do not apply one adjective order indiscriminately to both.

### Common trap

“Cheaper” and “better” do not mean the same thing. If you ask for a cheaper option, use the price adjective rather than a broad quality judgement. In practical conversation, an explicit comparison dimension prevents misunderstanding more reliably than a vague “good” or “bad”.

Also notice that `از` previously expressed origin. Here its role is comparative. Prepositions have multiple constructions, so their meaning is determined by the phrase around them, not by one English translation printed beside the word.

### Practice

Compare the specified quality.

1. This room is bigger than that room.
2. This book is cheaper.
3. That book is better.
4. The biggest room. Build a noun phrase.

***Answers:***

1. `این اتاق از آن اتاق بزرگ‌تر است.` - `In otāq az ān otāq bozorgtar ast.`
2. `این کتاب ارزان‌تر است.` - `In ketāb arzāntar ast.`
3. `آن کتاب بهتر است.` - `Ān ketāb behtar ast.`
4. `بزرگ‌ترین اتاق` - `Bozorgtarin otāq`

### Why these answers work

The first three are statements ending in a copula; the fourth names a room using a superlative phrase. Retrieve by comparing two imaginary rooms on size and price. Say which one you want using Lesson 13, and explain whether your preference is based on size, price or another quality.

## Lesson 21: My book and your address

### What you will be able to do

Attach a short possessive ending to an ordinary consonant-final noun, and recognise how that differs from a verb ending. You already know the longer expression `کتاب من` (`ketāb-e man`), “my book.” Persian also allows `کتابم` (`ketābam`). Both identify the possessor, although emphasis and the surrounding phrase may make one preferable.

Pause before reading the table. In `کتابم` and `می‌خوانم`, the ending points to the speaker. Does that make the first expression a complete sentence? No. A possessive noun phrase still needs a predicate if you want to make a statement about it. Recognising an ending is only half the task; identify what it attaches to.

### Build a possessive phrase

| Phrase | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `کتابم` | `ketābam` | my book |
| `کتابت` | `ketābat` | your book, singular familiar |
| `کتابش` | `ketābash` | his or her book |
| `کتابمان` | `ketābemān` | our book |
| `کتابتان` | `ketābetān` | your book, plural or respectful |
| `کتابشان` | `ketābeshān` | their book |
| `آدرسم` | `ādresam` | my address |
| `آدرستان` | `ādresetān` | your address, respectful |

The endings do not distinguish a male from a female possessor. `کتابش` can refer to the book belonging to either person. The plural-person endings have a connecting vowel in the readings shown. Spellings and linking sounds after vowel-final nouns need separate attention, so practise with the consonant-final nouns in this lesson before extending the pattern.

### Work it through

Begin with “Your book is here,” addressing one person respectfully. The noun phrase is `کتابتان`; “here” is `اینجا` (`injā`); the copula is `است`. The result is `کتابتان اینجا است.` (`Ketābetān injā ast.`). Respectful possession changes the possessive ending; it does not make the book plural.

Now identify the same book as an object: “I read your book.” Say `کتابتان را می‌خوانم.` (`Ketābetān rā mikhānam.`). The possessive phrase is already specific in this context, so `را` follows it. It does not go between the noun and its ending. Compare `کتاب شما را می‌خوانم.` (`Ketāb-e shomā rā mikhānam.`), the longer version.

### A useful distinction

`کتابم` by itself is normally “my book.” It is not a safe way to say “I am a book.” Short copulas and possessive endings can look alike, and context resolves many real examples. At this stage use the full copulas from Lesson 2 when constructing an identity sentence. That keeps your intended structure visible while you learn.

A common trap is attaching both a possessive ending and a personal pronoun without knowing the construction. For an ordinary neutral “my book,” choose either `کتابم` or `کتاب من`. Do not add pieces merely because English uses a separate possessive word.

### Practice

Use an attached possessive ending; address the listener respectfully where needed.

1. My book is here.
2. Your book is here.
3. Our book is here.
4. I read your book.

***Answers:***

1. `کتابم اینجا است.` - `Ketābam injā ast.`
2. `کتابتان اینجا است.` - `Ketābetān injā ast.`
3. `کتابمان اینجا است.` - `Ketābemān injā ast.`
4. `کتابتان را می‌خوانم.` - `Ketābetān rā mikhānam.`

### Why these answers work

The possessive endings change the owner; the first three predicates remain identical. In the fourth, the same noun phrase becomes a marked object. Retrieve by replacing each attached form with an ezafe phrase and a pronoun. Then imagine returning a borrowed book: identify its owner and say where the book is now.

## Lesson 22: Join ideas without losing the verb

### What you will be able to do

Connect short clauses with “and,” “but,” “because” and a simple real-world “if.” Longer communication often comes from combining familiar sentences, not memorising a longer mysterious form. Keep a complete thought on each side of the connector while you first practise.

Think about “I want to read, but I do not have time.” Which half requires a negative verb? Only the second. A connector does not spread negation automatically into the other clause. Work out each proposition before joining them.

### A small set of connectors

| Persian | Reading | Use |
|---|---|---|
| `و` | `va` | and, careful written reading |
| `اما` | `ammā` | but |
| `چون` | `chon` | because, in these examples |
| `اگر` | `agar` | if |
| `وقت` | `vaqt` | time |
| `خسته` | `khaste` | tired |
| `خانه` | `khāne` | house, home |
| `می‌مانم` | `mimānam` | I stay |

You will also hear the conjunction `و` pronounced more like `o` in connected speech. Learn to recognise both without assuming every occurrence has the same careful pronunciation. The examples here retain `va` to make the written construction easy to follow.

### Work it through

Make the first clause: `می‌خواهم کتاب بخوانم.` (`Mikhāham ketāb bekhānam.`), “I want to read a book.” Make the second: `وقت ندارم.` (`Vaqt nadāram.`), “I do not have time.” Now combine them: `می‌خواهم کتاب بخوانم، اما وقت ندارم.` (`Mikhāham ketāb bekhānam, ammā vaqt nadāram.`). The desire construction keeps its subjunctive even inside a longer sentence.

To explain a decision, say `در خانه می‌مانم، چون خسته هستم.` (`Dar khāne mimānam, chon khaste hastam.`), “I am staying at home because I am tired.” Both predicates keep their own structure: staying uses an action verb; being tired uses a copula. There is no need to insert an English-style auxiliary into the first clause.

### A bounded conditional pattern

`اگر وقت دارم، کتاب می‌خوانم.` (`Agar vaqt dāram, ketāb mikhānam.`) describes a general situation: “If I have time, I read a book.” This lesson uses a habitual, open condition. Persian has other conditional patterns for possibilities, imagined situations and past counterfactuals. Do not assume that copying these present forms will express every English sentence containing “if.”

The common trap is losing the person ending once a sentence grows longer. In the conditional above, both verbs are first person. If the subject changes, state it clearly and use the appropriate ending in that clause. A second trap is treating `چون` as a universal replacement for every sense of English “as.” Here it supplies a reason; other meanings require other constructions.

### Practice

Join the ideas using the specified relationship.

1. I have time and I read a book.
2. I want to read a book, but I do not have time.
3. I am staying at home because I am tired.
4. If I have time, I read a book. Describe a habit.

***Answers:***

1. `وقت دارم و کتاب می‌خوانم.` - `Vaqt dāram va ketāb mikhānam.`
2. `می‌خواهم کتاب بخوانم، اما وقت ندارم.` - `Mikhāham ketāb bekhānam, ammā vaqt nadāram.`
3. `در خانه می‌مانم، چون خسته هستم.` - `Dar khāne mimānam, chon khaste hastam.`
4. `اگر وقت دارم، کتاب می‌خوانم.` - `Agar vaqt dāram, ketāb mikhānam.`

### Why these answers work

Each connector expresses a different relationship while leaving the clauses internally recognisable. Retrieve by covering everything after the connector and predicting a sensible continuation. Then write two linked sentences about a real routine, one giving a reason and one contrasting your wish with an obstacle.

## Lesson 23: The book that I read

### What you will be able to do

Identify a person or thing with a short relative clause using `که` (`ke`). This is useful when you do not know a name or when an adjective is insufficient. A relative clause adds information to a noun; the whole expanded phrase can then serve as a subject or object.

Consider “The book is good” and “I read the book.” How could you identify the book in the first sentence using the second fact? Persian can build “The book that I read is good.” You need to keep track of which verb belongs inside the description and which verb completes the main sentence.

### Pieces for a relative clause

| Persian | Reading | Meaning or role |
|---|---|---|
| `که` | `ke` | that, relative connector here |
| `کتابی که` | `ketābi ke` | the/a book that, depending on context |
| `دوستی که` | `dusti ke` | a/the friend who |
| `اینجا` | `injā` | here |
| `آنجا` | `ānjā` | there |
| `دیدم` | `didam` | I saw |
| `خریدم` | `kharidam` | I bought |
| `جالب` | `jāleb` | interesting |

The ending written `ی` on `کتابی` is a familiar feature of the restrictive relative pattern practised here. Do not read `کتابی که` as though every occurrence must refer to a completely unknown book. Determining whether English needs “a” or “the” requires the whole context. Persian does not reproduce the English article system word for word.

### Work it through

Start with `کتاب خوب است.` (`Ketāb khub ast.`), “The book is good.” Insert the description `که خواندم` (`ke khāndam`), “that I read,” after the noun in the relative pattern: `کتابی که خواندم خوب است.` (`Ketābi ke khāndam khub ast.`). The past verb `خواندم` identifies the book; `خوب است` tells us the main new information.

Now try a person as the subject inside the relative clause: `دوستی که اینجا است فارسی می‌خواند.` (`Dusti ke injā ast fārsi mikhānad.`), “The friend who is here studies Persian.” The friend is third person, so the main verb has `-ad`. You do not copy the first-person ending from the previous example simply because both sentences contain `که`.

### Keep the scope small

Persian relative clauses can use pronouns inside the clause in ways that differ from English, especially when the relationship involves a preposition or possession. This lesson practises direct subject and direct object relationships only. “The friend whose book…” and “the person with whom…” need further patterns; do not improvise them by deleting an English word.

A common trap is ending the sentence after the relative clause when you intended a complete statement. `کتابی که خواندم` names “the book that I read.” Add a main predicate such as `جالب است` to say something about it. Another trap is treating every `که` as relative. It also connects other kinds of clauses; examine the preceding word and the sentence's purpose.

### Practice

Use the relative patterns from this lesson.

1. The book that I read is good.
2. The book that I bought is interesting.
3. The friend who is here studies Persian.
4. The book that I read is here.

***Answers:***

1. `کتابی که خواندم خوب است.` - `Ketābi ke khāndam khub ast.`
2. `کتابی که خریدم جالب است.` - `Ketābi ke kharidam jāleb ast.`
3. `دوستی که اینجا است فارسی می‌خواند.` - `Dusti ke injā ast fārsi mikhānad.`
4. `کتابی که خواندم اینجا است.` - `Ketābi ke khāndam injā ast.`

### Why these answers work

The words between `که` and the main predicate identify the noun. In the third answer the noun is also the subject of the relative clause. Retrieve by reducing every answer to its main statement first. Then add back the identifying clause and explain which extra question it answers for the listener.

## Lesson 24: Recognise everyday Iranian speech

### What you will be able to do

Recognise several high-frequency differences between the careful written standard used in this course and everyday Iranian conversation. This is a listening bridge, not a rule that every Persian variety must follow. Speakers in Iran vary, and Dari and Tajik have their own standards and conversational forms.

Before looking at the pairs, ask what would happen if a listener expected every written vowel and ending to appear unchanged in rapid speech. They might know the grammar but fail to recognise it. Your task is to connect familiar meanings with a few common spoken shapes while keeping a dependable written form for your own production.

### Common recognition pairs

| Written form | Reading | Common Iranian spoken form and reading |
|---|---|---|
| `خانه` | `khāne` | `خونه` / `khune`, house |
| `نان` | `nān` | `نون` / `nun`, bread |
| `آن` | `ān` | `اون` / `un`, that |
| `می‌روم` | `miravam` | `می‌رم` / `miram`, I go |
| `می‌خواهم` | `mikhāham` | `می‌خوام` / `mikhām`, I want |
| `است` | `ast` | attached `ـه` / `-e` in many contexts |
| `را` | `rā` | `رو` / `ro`, often reduced further |
| `نمی‌دانم` | `nemidānam` | `نمی‌دونم` / `nemidunam`, I do not know |

These are selected correspondences, not instructions for globally replacing letters. The change from `ā` to `u` occurs in particular environments and words; it is not a universal sound conversion. Spellings of informal speech also vary because ordinary edited prose generally uses the written standard.

### Work it through

You learned `به خانه می‌روم.` (`Be khāne miravam.`), “I am going home.” In a casual Iranian conversation you may hear `می‌رم خونه.` (`Miram khune.`). Recognise the same destination and action even though pronunciation and phrasing differ. Do not conclude that all prepositions may now be deleted in every sentence.

Likewise `خوب است.` (`Khub ast.`), “It is good,” commonly corresponds to `خوبه.` (`Khube.`). The predicate has not disappeared; the copula has a shorter form. When you return to a careful written exercise, restore `است` rather than mixing an informal ending into an otherwise formal paradigm by accident.

### Choose a register deliberately

In an informal chat between friends, everyday forms may be appropriate. In a formal application or an elementary written exercise, use the taught written standard unless instructed otherwise. Respect and informality are separate dimensions: a speaker can address someone respectfully while still using conversational pronunciation.

The trap is calling one register “wrong Persian,” or treating a colloquial form as universal across all speakers. Another trap is judging an unfamiliar sound solely by the Latin reading. Listen to recordings from speakers of the variety you are studying and match a short phrase to its written counterpart. The table is a recognition aid, not a substitute for hearing speech.

### Practice

Give the careful written-standard equivalent, not the colloquial spelling.

1. I am going home. You heard “Miram khune.”
2. I want a book. Use the full written verb.
3. It is good. Use the separate copula.
4. I do not know. Use the full written form.

***Answers:***

1. `به خانه می‌روم.` - `Be khāne miravam.`
2. `کتاب می‌خواهم.` - `Ketāb mikhāham.`
3. `خوب است.` - `Khub ast.`
4. `نمی‌دانم.` - `Nemidānam.`

### Why these answers work

Each answer restores the course's written standard without changing the basic meaning. The second uses a noun object after “want,” so it does not require a second subjunctive verb. Retrieve by matching the spoken forms to meanings with the written column hidden, then produce a careful sentence and label its register.

## Lesson 25: Build a conversation from decisions

### What you will be able to do

Combine identity, location, time, possession and requests into a short purposeful exchange. The aim is to explain how you built a sentence and repair it when the listener needs something different. Completing these lessons is a foundation milestone, not proof of fluency or a certified proficiency level.

Imagine visiting a small bookshop. You want a Persian book, you need to know where it is, and you may need the seller to repeat something. Before translating whole English sentences, decide what each turn is doing: identifying, asking, requesting or reporting an earlier action. Those decisions select the constructions.

### Your reusable resources

| Function | Persian | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| greet | `سلام` | `salām` |
| polite attention | `ببخشید` | `bebakhshid` |
| request politely | `لطفاً` | `lotfan` |
| thank someone | `ممنون` | `mamnun` |
| identify a book | `این کتاب` | `in ketāb` |
| ask a location | `کجا است؟` | `kojā ast?` |
| ask a price | `چند است؟` | `chand ast?` |
| request repetition | `دوباره بگویید` | `dobāre beguyid` |

`ببخشید` is a useful polite form for attracting attention or apologising. Its role depends on context. `بگویید` is the respectful imperative of “say.” Learn it as the form appropriate to this request, alongside the imperative patterns already introduced; do not derive it from the past stem `گفت`.

### Work through a short exchange

Open with `سلام. یک کتاب فارسی می‌خواهم.` (`Salām. Yek ketāb-e fārsi mikhāham.`), “Hello. I would like a Persian book.” Here the noun phrase is nonspecific. If you point at a particular book, `این کتاب را می‌خواهم.` (`In ketāb rā mikhāham.`) identifies a specific object and includes `را`.

If you do not understand the answer, say `لطفاً دوباره بگویید.` (`Lotfan dobāre beguyid.`). If the explanation remains difficult, `لطفاً آرام‌تر صحبت کنید.` (`Lotfan ārāmtar sohbat konid.`) asks the speaker to speak more slowly. A repair request is a successful conversational action: it tells the other person exactly what would help.

### Check the structure, then the purpose

When revising your own sentence, first locate the finite verb. Is it a copula, possession verb, ordinary action or compound verb? Next check person, time and negation. Then examine the noun phrase: does it need ezafe, an attached possessor or the object marker? Finally check whether the register fits the person you are addressing.

The common trap at this stage is creating a long sentence just to display every rule. Start with a short sentence that accomplishes a real goal. Add a reason or relative clause only when it helps identify your meaning. Your listener needs a clear request more than a demonstration of the largest possible number of suffixes.

### Practice

Use the careful written standard and respectful requests.

1. Hello. I want a Persian book.
2. I want this book.
3. Please say it again.
4. Yesterday I bought a book, but I have not read it. Use “that book” explicitly.

***Answers:***

1. `سلام. یک کتاب فارسی می‌خواهم.` - `Salām. Yek ketāb-e fārsi mikhāham.`
2. `این کتاب را می‌خواهم.` - `In ketāb rā mikhāham.`
3. `لطفاً دوباره بگویید.` - `Lotfan dobāre beguyid.`
4. `دیروز یک کتاب خریدم، اما آن کتاب را نخوانده‌ام.` - `Diruz yek ketāb kharidam, ammā ān ketāb rā nakhānde-am.`

### Why these answers work

The first introduces an unspecified book; the second selects an identified one. The third uses a respectful imperative. The fourth contrasts a completed purchase with the present-relevant fact that reading has not happened. For retrieval, retell the visit without notes, then change the item, owner and time. Ask a fluent speaker to review the result and record the construction behind each correction, not just the corrected sentence.
