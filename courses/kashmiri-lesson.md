# Kashmiri: A pattern-first foundation

Editorial status: original AI-assisted draft; fluent-speaker review pending.

This is a romanised spoken foundation in the broadly standard Kashmir Valley variety represented in the teaching and grammatical sources below. It is not a native-script literacy course. Kashmiri is commonly written in Perso-Arabic and also has established Devanagari and historical Sharada traditions. Native-script lessons remain pending; the Latin transcription here is a pronunciation and grammar aid, not an equivalent replacement orthography. Regional speech and community registers differ. The course keeps its vocabulary small and does not present one speaker's forms as the only legitimate Kashmiri.

The transcription uses a colon for vowel length, ɨ for a high central unrounded vowel, ə for a mid central vowel, and ɔ for an open rounded back vowel. These are distinct sounds, not optional spelling decorations. An apostrophe after a consonant marks palatalisation; it is not an English possessive mark. The letters c and ch represent the unaspirated and aspirated palatal affricates; ts and tsh represent the dental affricates. Thus c is approximately English ch, while ch adds aspiration. Shorthand has been converted to readable Unicode after checking rendered source pages. Ordinary a, i, u, e and o retain their own values. A tilde indicates nasalisation. No text-only course can teach the full sound system without listening.

### References and review notes

Consulted resources include Koul and Wali's [Modern Kashmiri Grammar](https://www.kashmirculturaltrust.in/publications/ModernKashmiriGrammar.pdf), Bhat's [A Course in Kashmiri Language](https://www.kashmirculturaltrust.in/publications/A-Course-In-Kashmiri-Language.pdf), and the CIIL handbook's [demonstratives lesson](https://koshur.org/ciil/prog18.html) and [personal pronouns lesson](https://koshur.org/ciil/prog19.html). The [CIIL primer](https://www.kashmirculturaltrust.in/publications/primer.pdf) was inspected for the distinct native-script teaching tradition. Grammar-page images were used where text extraction corrupted vowel symbols. The sequence and explanations below are original, with deliberately limited productive paradigms. Review should prioritise pronunciation, palatalisation, register and naturalness of recombined sentences.

## Lesson 1: Identify a person with a linking verb

### Outcome and thinking point

Say that a person is a teacher or shopkeeper. Start with a practical question about structure: if you already know who the sentence is about, where should the little word meaning is go? Do not assume that neighbouring languages provide the answer. Kashmiri commonly places its finite verb in the second constituent position in an ordinary main clause.

`Aslam chu ma:sṭar.` means “Aslam is a teacher.” `Aslam chu duka:nda:r.` means “Aslam is a shopkeeper.” The person's name occupies the first position, chu links it to an identity, and the identity follows. There is no separate indefinite article in these neutral identifications.

### Useful words

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Aslam | Aslam | a person's name |
| su | he, that male person | singular pronoun |
| chu | is, masculine singular here | linking verb |
| ma:sṭar | teacher | occupation |
| duka:nda:r | shopkeeper | occupation |
| da:ktar | doctor | occupation |
| vaki:l | lawyer | occupation |

### Rebuild rather than memorise

`Su chu vaki:l.` means “He is a lawyer.” You have changed the first and last pieces while preserving the relationship. The pronoun refers to a male person understood from the situation. Do not replace it with the Urdu pronoun merely because lawyer is a recognisable loanword. Shared vocabulary does not make the grammatical systems interchangeable.

A colon means the preceding vowel is long. The a in ma:sṭar is therefore held longer than a short a. The retroflex ṭ is pronounced with the tongue farther back than a dental t. You do not need to master every sound before building a sentence, but noticing a contrast is better than declaring two distinct spellings equivalent.

The word chu is not an empty decoration. If you omit it from the simple positive clause, you have removed the finite link that makes the identity claim. In more advanced connected speech, a repeated copula can sometimes be omitted under particular conditions. That is a later discourse pattern, not a reason to omit it from every first sentence.

### Common trap

Avoid a final-copula order borrowed from Hindi or Urdu. The teaching model here is person, finite link, identity. It is a starting pattern for an ordinary main clause, not a statement that every Kashmiri clause has exactly three words.

### Practice

Produce one short identification.

1. Aslam is a shopkeeper.
2. He is a lawyer.
3. Aslam is a doctor.
4. He is a teacher.

***Answers:***

1. `Aslam chu duka:nda:r.`
2. `Su chu vaki:l.`
3. `Aslam chu da:ktar.`
4. `Su chu ma:sṭar.`

### Why these answers work

The person comes first and the finite linking verb follows. Each occupation completes the identification. All subjects in these exercises are ordinary masculine singular references, so chu is appropriate throughout.

### Retrieve and recombine

Imagine introducing two men at a community class. Give one an occupation and identify the other by a pronoun. Then exchange their occupations. Preserve the finite link while changing only the information that has actually changed.

## Lesson 2: Say that something is ready or clean

### Outcome and thinking point

Describe a condition instead of an occupation, and notice feminine agreement. If the subject changes from a masculine item to a feminine one, can the linking verb change even when the describing word stays unchanged? Compare tea with a pen before answering.

`Ca:y cha taya:r.` means “The tea is ready.” Tea is feminine in the examples used here, and its copula is cha. `Kalam chu sa:ph.` means “The pen is clean.” Pen is masculine and selects chu. The adjective or state word follows the finite link in both sentences.

### A small descriptive set

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Useful information |
|---|---|---|
| ca:y | tea | feminine |
| kalam | pen | masculine |
| kursi: | chair | feminine |
| kamrɨ | room | masculine |
| taya:r | ready | state word |
| sa:ph | clean | unchanged adjective |
| cha | is with feminine subjects here | finite link |
| ja:n | good | unchanged adjective |

### Keep the grammatical jobs separate

`Kursi: cha sa:ph.` means “The chair is clean.” `Kamrɨ chu sa:ph.` means “The room is clean.” The word sa:ph does not receive a different ending in these examples. The copula nevertheless records the subject's grammatical gender. Agreement can therefore be visible in one part of the sentence even when another part is unchanged.

`Ca:y cha ja:n.` means “The tea is good.” A description of quality differs from a readiness statement: good tea might not yet be ready to serve, and ready tea is not necessarily good. Changing one state word changes the claim without requiring a new sentence frame.

Grammatical gender is a property of the noun, not an assertion that tea is biologically female. Nor does the speaker's sex determine the tea sentence. A man and a woman describing the same tea use the same agreement. In the next lesson, a description of the speaker will behave differently because the speaker becomes the subject.

### Common trap

Do not make every adjective imitate an English gender-neutral form or a Hindi adjective ending. Kashmiri has both changing and unchanged adjective classes. This lesson deliberately uses unchanged descriptions while teaching the copula contrast; it is not evidence that all adjectives are invariable.

### Practice

Describe the named item.

1. The chair is clean.
2. The room is clean.
3. The tea is good.
4. The tea is ready.

***Answers:***

1. `Kursi: cha sa:ph.`
2. `Kamrɨ chu sa:ph.`
3. `Ca:y cha ja:n.`
4. `Ca:y cha taya:r.`

### Why these answers work

Chair and tea take feminine cha; room takes masculine chu. The descriptions sa:ph, ja:n and taya:r remain in their demonstrated form. The change of copula follows the described noun rather than the person producing the sentence.

### Retrieve and recombine

At a café, describe the chair as clean and the tea as ready. At a classroom, describe a room as clean. Explain which noun controls each copula, then imagine a different speaker making the same three claims.

## Lesson 3: Describe yourself and a familiar listener

### Outcome and thinking point

Say that you are ready and ask a familiar person to consider the same condition. You have used third-person chu and cha. Would replacing the subject with I leave the finite form completely unchanged? Look for the small person endings as well as the gender distinction.

`Bɨ chus taya:r.` means “I am ready”, for a male speaker. `Bɨ chas taya:r.` is the female-speaker form. The first-person pronoun contains ɨ, a central vowel. It is not simply English be or the front vowel of see. The final s in the copula is part of the first-person pattern.

### Person contrasts

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Context |
|---|---|---|
| bɨ | I | either speaker gender |
| tsɨ | you | familiar singular |
| chus | am | masculine speaker |
| chas | am | feminine speaker |
| chukh | are | masculine familiar listener |
| chakh | are | feminine familiar listener |
| taya:r | ready | unchanged state word |

### Two features at once

`Tsɨ chukh taya:r.` says “You are ready” to a familiar male listener. `Tsɨ chakh taya:r.` says the same to a familiar female listener. The pronoun tsɨ remains the same; gender appears on the copula. First person and second person also differ through s versus kh endings in these forms.

This gives a useful comparison with English. English distinguishes am and are but does not encode the same masculine-feminine contrast there. Kashmiri asks you to keep track of both person and gender in this small paradigm. Do not treat that as extra information about the word ready; it is grammatical information about the person being described.

The familiar singular is not the safest form for every stranger. Learn it for understanding family or peer conversations, then use the respectful pattern introduced next when the relationship calls for it. Real households differ in how familiarity and respect are expressed, so a pronoun is not a universal measure of affection.

### Common trap

Do not say chus merely because the speaker producing the sentence is male if the subject is actually the listener. Agreement follows the subject being described. A male speaker addressing a woman uses the female-listener form chakh in this familiar pattern.

### Practice

Match person and grammatical gender.

1. I am ready. (male speaker)
2. I am ready. (female speaker)
3. You are ready. (familiar male listener)
4. You are ready. (familiar female listener)

***Answers:***

1. `Bɨ chus taya:r.`
2. `Bɨ chas taya:r.`
3. `Tsɨ chukh taya:r.`
4. `Tsɨ chakh taya:r.`

### Why these answers work

The pronoun identifies first or second person, while the copula supplies matching person and gender information. Taya:r does not change. The last two answers describe the listener rather than the speaker, so the listener's grammatical gender is relevant.

### Retrieve and recombine

Give your own readiness statement, then describe a familiar man and woman as ready. Imagine that a different person speaks the last two sentences. Explain why the listener-directed forms remain unchanged when only the speaker changes.

## Lesson 4: Use respectful address and plural reference

### Outcome and thinking point

Address someone respectfully and identify several people. A language can use plural-style agreement for one respected person. Before translating every plural form as a crowd, ask whether the conversation is marking social respect instead.

`Toh' chiv taya:r.` is a useful respectful “You are ready” in the masculine honorific pattern used here. The pronoun toh' has palatalisation indicated by the apostrophe. Do not pronounce that mark as an extra English syllable or treat it as punctuation that can always be ignored.

### Respect and number

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Context |
|---|---|---|
| toh' | you | plural or respectful |
| chiv | are | masculine plural/honorific pattern |
| chav | are | feminine plural pattern |
| tim | they | masculine plural |
| chi | are | masculine plural third person |
| taya:r | ready | state |
| ma:sṭar | teacher | occupation |

### A respectful model with stated limits

`Tim chi ma:sṭar.` means “They are teachers”, referring to men or the masculine agreement group. `Tim chi taya:r.` means “They are ready.” Third-person plural forms can also be used honorifically in suitable contexts. The course uses explicit plural prompts when it needs an unambiguous numerical reading.

For groups of women, feminine agreement is relevant, and the fuller pronoun paradigm has feminine plural forms. Rather than infer those from Hindi or Urdu, learn them with their own examples in the demonstrative lesson. The current productive respectful model uses chiv, which the consulted grammar identifies as available for honorific reference.

Titles such as jina:b occur in respectful interaction. Other address particles can reflect community and situational preferences. This course does not assign a person's religion from the particle they use, nor does it recommend mechanically attaching a title to every sentence. The pronoun and agreement provide the primary pattern being practised.

### Common trap

Do not mix familiar tsɨ with the respectful agreement merely because English translates both as you. A change of address level affects the construction. At the same time, do not insist that every honorific plural describes more than one human being.

### Practice

Use the specified plural or respectful reference.

1. You are ready. (respectful chiv pattern)
2. They are teachers. (masculine plural)
3. They are ready. (masculine plural)
4. Give the respectful/plural pronoun used in this lesson.

***Answers:***

1. `Toh' chiv taya:r.`
2. `Tim chi ma:sṭar.`
3. `Tim chi taya:r.`
4. `Toh'`

### Why these answers work

The respectful second-person pattern uses toh' and chiv together. The explicit masculine plural third-person examples use tim and chi. The final answer is only the pronoun because the prompt requests a word rather than an entire statement.

### Retrieve and recombine

Address a teacher respectfully, then describe two teachers to somebody else. Explain why both sentences involve plural-style grammar but only the second necessarily describes several people. Keep the familiar singular pattern available for recognition without making it your default with strangers.

## Lesson 5: Ask what something is

### Outcome and thinking point

Turn an identification into an information question. You have learned a finite verb in second position for ordinary statements. Before applying that mechanically to questions, notice that an information word can precede the finite verb after a topic.

`Yi k'a: chu?` means “What is this?” `Yi chu kalam.` means “This is a pen.” The question places the unknown identity before the copula. The answer returns to the ordinary demonstrative, copula, identity pattern. Different clause types need their own observed order.

### Identifying objects

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Information |
|---|---|---|
| yi | this | nearby singular |
| k'a: | what | information question |
| kalam | pen | masculine |
| me:z | table | masculine in these examples |
| ka:pi: | notebook | feminine |
| ca:y | tea | feminine |
| chu | is | masculine |
| cha | is | feminine |

### Use known gender when it is available

`Yi chu me:z.` identifies a table: “This is a table.” `Yi cha ka:pi:.` identifies a notebook: “This is a notebook.” The feminine item selects cha even though the near demonstrative yi remains the same in the singular examples. A neutral unknown-item question commonly uses the masculine form shown above; a context where the feminine class is already known can support feminine questioning.

`Yi cha ca:y.` means “This is tea.” If somebody asks what is in a cup, you can supply the identity without adding an English article. The absence of a separate article does not prevent context from distinguishing a particular cup of tea from tea as a substance.

The palatalisation mark in k'a: belongs to the consonant. It should not be read as an English contraction. Kashmiri palatalisation can distinguish words and grammatical forms, so replacing every marked consonant with an unmarked one would lose information rather than merely simplify typography.

### Common trap

Do not move the copula to the end of every answer just because it occurs after the question word in the information question. Learn the question-answer relationship, including the change of word order, rather than copying one clause mechanically into the other.

### Practice

Ask an identity question or supply the identification.

1. What is this? (neutral object question)
2. This is a table.
3. This is a notebook.
4. This is tea.

***Answers:***

1. `Yi k'a: chu?`
2. `Yi chu me:z.`
3. `Yi cha ka:pi:.`
4. `Yi cha ca:y.`

### Why these answers work

The information question uses k'a: before the copula. The statements restore the finite link after the demonstrative. Table takes masculine chu, while notebook and tea take feminine cha. None needs a separate indefinite article.

### Retrieve and recombine

Point to a desk object, ask what it is and identify it. Repeat with a notebook and a drink. Describe what happens to the unknown-identity position when a question becomes an answer, and keep palatalisation audible in k'a:.

## Lesson 6: Ask who a person is

### Outcome and thinking point

Distinguish a question about a person from one about an object or role. If the person is male, which question word appears in the basic identity question? Try replacing what with the person question word while preserving the observed interrogative order.

`Yi kus chu?` means “Who is this?”, referring to a male person. `Yi chu Aslam.` means “This is Aslam.” A name answers the identity question. By contrast, “He is a shopkeeper” gives a role and may answer a different question about the person.

### Person identification

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Context |
|---|---|---|
| kus | who | masculine singular |
| kɔsɨ | who | feminine singular |
| kam | who | masculine plural |
| yi | this | nearby reference |
| su | he | masculine singular |
| sɔ | she | feminine singular |
| Aslam | Aslam | example male name |
| Razia | Razia | example female name |

### Compare identity and description

`Sɔ cha Razia.` means “She is Razia.” `Su chu Aslam.` means “He is Aslam.” Kashmiri distinguishes gender in these third-person forms; do not transfer Urdu's gender-neutral voh into this pattern. The open rounded vowel ɔ in sɔ is distinct from u in su.

`Yi kɔsɨ cha?` asks “Who is this?”, in a feminine reference context. The question word and copula both reflect that grammatical setting. `Tim kam chi?` asks “Who are they?”, for a masculine plural group. The question word is not invariant across the whole person paradigm.

A useful dialogue can therefore separate name and occupation. Ask who the man is, identify him as Aslam, then state that he is a teacher. You have built two claims about the same referent without treating a person's name and occupation as interchangeable kinds of answer.

### Common trap

Do not infer that the English single word who must correspond to one unchanged Kashmiri form. Also do not use the male pronoun su merely because the person is distant; distance and grammatical gender are separate features in this system.

### Practice

Ask about or identify the specified person.

1. Who is this? (male reference)
2. This is Aslam.
3. She is Razia.
4. Who are they? (masculine plural)

***Answers:***

1. `Yi kus chu?`
2. `Yi chu Aslam.`
3. `Sɔ cha Razia.`
4. `Tim kam chi?`

### Why these answers work

The singular masculine question uses kus and chu. Razia is referred to with feminine sɔ and cha. The plural question uses kam with chi. A name answers identity, while an occupation would add another kind of information about the same person.

### Retrieve and recombine

Introduce Aslam and Razia by name, then ask another learner to identify a male group. Explain how gender and number influence the question words, pronouns and copulas rather than assuming one English word predicts one unchanging Kashmiri word.

## Lesson 7: Negate a description

### Outcome and thinking point

Correct a mistaken identity while keeping the copula. English places not near its finite verb, but neighbouring languages may put their negatives elsewhere. Find the negative's position in the Kashmiri examples before attempting a translation.

`Aslam chu nɨ vaki:l.` means “Aslam is not a lawyer.” The negative nɨ follows the finite copula. `Bɨ chus nɨ taya:r.` means “I am not ready”, for a male speaker. Person and gender agreement remain visible before the negative.

### Positive and negative pieces

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Use |
|---|---|---|
| nɨ | not | follows finite verb here |
| na | no | independent reply |
| a: | yes | independent reply |
| chus | am | masculine first person |
| chas | am | feminine first person |
| taya:r | ready | condition |
| vaki:l | lawyer | identity |
| da:ktar | doctor | identity |

### Build a correction in two steps

`Bɨ chas nɨ taya:r.` means “I am not ready”, for a female speaker. Only the copula changes when you switch speaker gender; the negative does not become a feminine form. `Ca:y cha nɨ taya:r.` means “The tea is not ready.” The same state word can now describe a nonhuman feminine subject.

If somebody mistakes Aslam's occupation, first negate the mistaken role and then give the correct one: `Aslam chu da:ktar.` means “Aslam is a doctor.” These are two distinct claims. A correction can be clear even when you do not yet know a special word for actually or instead.

An independent no is not the same grammatical item as the negative inside this clause. Likewise, an independent yes is not a copula. You should recognise both kinds of expression but avoid replacing one with the other merely because English uses no and not in related situations.

### Common trap

Do not omit chu or cha from a simple negative identity clause. In this pattern the negative follows the copula; it does not take over its job. This is a point where copying a shortened negative from another South Asian language can produce an unsupported Kashmiri sentence.

### Practice

Negate the stated claim.

1. Aslam is not a lawyer.
2. I am not ready. (male speaker)
3. I am not ready. (female speaker)
4. The tea is not ready.

***Answers:***

1. `Aslam chu nɨ vaki:l.`
2. `Bɨ chus nɨ taya:r.`
3. `Bɨ chas nɨ taya:r.`
4. `Ca:y cha nɨ taya:r.`

### Why these answers work

The negative follows the finite link in every answer. Copula agreement continues to distinguish masculine and feminine subjects and first versus third person. The state or occupation remains after the negative and is the claim being denied.

### Retrieve and recombine

Deny a mistaken occupation, give the correct one and say that a drink is not ready. Change the speaker in your own readiness sentence from male to female. Explain why the negative itself stays the same throughout.

## Lesson 8: Distinguish this, that and those

### Outcome and thinking point

Identify a nearby object, a visible distant object and several objects. English this and that provide a useful starting contrast, but Kashmiri distinguishes more than simple distance. Track visibility, gender and number rather than translating every distant referent with one word.

`Hu chu kalam.` means “That is a pen”, with the visible distant masculine demonstrative used in the teaching source. `Hum chi kalam.` means “Those are pens.” The noun kalam does not visibly change here, but the demonstrative and copula establish plurality.

### Demonstrative contrasts

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| yi | this | near singular |
| yim | these | near masculine plural |
| hu | that | visible distant masculine singular |
| hum | those | visible distant masculine plural |
| su | that male person | out-of-sight/reference use |
| tim | those, they | masculine plural reference |
| kalam | pen, pens | number comes from context here |
| me:z | table, tables | demonstrated masculine noun |

### Let agreement reveal number

`Yim chi me:z.` means “These are tables.” `Yi chu me:z.` means “This is a table.” A learner who looks only for a noun suffix could miss this contrast. Number may be distributed over the demonstrative, copula and context rather than announced by every noun.

Feminine demonstratives have their own forms, including yimɨ for these in the source's feminine plural set. The full system includes additional remote forms. This lesson keeps production with the transparent masculine contrasts in the table, while retaining feminine singular yi from the notebook lesson. A narrow productive set is more useful than a half-remembered complete chart.

Deixis is the grammatical organisation of pointing and reference. You do not need that technical label to use the pattern: ask whether your listener can identify the referent and whether it is near, visible at a distance or established elsewhere in the conversation. Real speakers use context flexibly, so these labels guide interpretation rather than acting as fixed physical measurements.

### Common trap

Do not decide that kalam must be singular because it lacks an English-like plural suffix. Equally, do not treat every demonstrative form as a free synonym. The choice contributes information about the speaker's perspective on the referent.

### Practice

Use the requested demonstrative pattern.

1. That is a pen. (visible distant masculine)
2. Those are pens. (visible distant masculine plural)
3. These are tables. (masculine plural)
4. This is a table.

***Answers:***

1. `Hu chu kalam.`
2. `Hum chi kalam.`
3. `Yim chi me:z.`
4. `Yi chu me:z.`

### Why these answers work

The demonstrative and copula encode the relevant distance and number contrasts. The nouns retain their demonstrated form. The singular table sentence uses chu; plural tables and pens use chi, even though the noun itself does not supply a visible plural suffix.

### Retrieve and recombine

Imagine objects on your desk and across the room. Identify one pen, several pens, one table and several tables. Explain which words carry number and which help the listener locate the referent from your point of view.

## Lesson 9: Describe reading in progress

### Outcome and thinking point

Move from describing a person to describing an activity. Kashmiri can put the finite auxiliary early while leaving an action participle later. If the speaker is female, should the reading participle change, or should the auxiliary carry the agreement?

`Bɨ chus kita:b para:n.` means “I am reading a book”, for a male speaker. `Bɨ chas kita:b para:n.` is the female-speaker form. The finite auxiliary changes, while para:n remains the same. The object lies between the auxiliary and the participle in this useful neutral pattern.

### Actions and objects

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Function |
|---|---|---|
| par | read | action stem |
| para:n | reading | present participle |
| le:kh | write | action stem |
| le:kha:n | writing | present participle |
| kita:b | book | object |
| akhba:r | newspaper | object |
| chus | am | masculine first person |
| chas | am | feminine first person |

### Separate finite and nonfinite work

`Su chu akhba:r para:n.` means “He is reading a newspaper.” `Sɔ cha kita:b para:n.` means “She is reading a book.” The pronoun and auxiliary distinguish masculine and feminine reference. The present participle itself does not take a new ending for person, gender or number in this pattern.

English uses am reading and is reading as a neighbouring sequence. Kashmiri lets the finite element and action participle be separated by the object. Thinking of the verb phrase as two pieces helps you resist moving every verb-related word together to one edge of the sentence.

The ending -a:n is a productive present-participle pattern for the demonstrated consonant stems. That does not mean the dictionary infinitive can simply be used unchanged as a present verb. Par names the stem, para:n presents the activity, and a finite auxiliary anchors the clause.

### Common trap

Do not add a feminine ending to para:n merely because a woman is reading. The auxiliary already carries the relevant agreement. Also do not move chu to the very end by analogy with Urdu or Punjabi. The early finite auxiliary is a defining part of the pattern you are learning.

### Practice

Describe the current reading activity.

1. I am reading a newspaper. (male speaker)
2. I am reading a book. (female speaker)
3. He is reading a book.
4. She is reading a newspaper.

***Answers:***

1. `Bɨ chus akhba:r para:n.`
2. `Bɨ chas kita:b para:n.`
3. `Su chu kita:b para:n.`
4. `Sɔ cha akhba:r para:n.`

### Why these answers work

The auxiliary agrees with the reader. Book and newspaper remain objects, and the participle para:n does not change with the subject. Each answer follows the same grammatical relationship without requiring all the verb material to be adjacent.

### Retrieve and recombine

Imagine a man reading a book and a woman reading a newspaper. Describe them, then exchange their reading material. Explain why the auxiliaries stay with the readers while only the objects change. Finally make yourself one of the readers and choose the appropriate first-person auxiliary.

## Lesson 10: Drink, eat and notice the linking v

### Outcome and thinking point

Describe drinking and eating while recognising a useful stem difference. The previous lesson added -a:n after a consonant. What could happen when a stem already ends in a vowel? Listen for an inserted v that keeps the pieces together.

`Su chu ca:y ceva:n.` means “He is drinking tea.” `Sɔ cha batɨ kheva:n.` means “She is eating food.” The stems ce and khe combine with a v before the participial ending. The object and the auxiliary retain their separate grammatical roles.

### Vowel-final stems

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Relationship |
|---|---|---|
| ce | drink | stem |
| ceva:n | drinking | present participle |
| khe | eat | stem |
| kheva:n | eating | present participle |
| di | give | stem |
| diva:n | giving | present participle |
| batɨ | food, cooked rice in context | object |
| dɔd | milk | masculine noun |

### Keep the action form stable

`Bɨ chus dɔd ceva:n.` means “I am drinking milk”, for a male speaker. `Bɨ chas dɔd ceva:n.` is the female-speaker sentence. Milk does not change the auxiliary from chas to chu; the subject is still the female speaker. This contrasts with the earlier identity statement in which a drink itself was the subject.

`Tim chi batɨ kheva:n.` means “They are eating food”, for a masculine plural group. The participle stays kheva:n. Number appears on the pronoun and auxiliary. This gives you a three-way test: change the object, change the subject's gender, then change the subject's number. Different parts of the sentence respond to each change.

Batɨ can refer to cooked rice and, in an eating context, food or a meal more generally. The English gloss should not erase that cultural and lexical connection. Likewise, local bread and tea vocabulary may distinguish items that English groups under one broad name. Learn food words from the actual item and situation where possible.

### Common trap

Do not write a mechanically concatenated cea:n or khea:n for these demonstrated present participles. The v is part of their ordinary formation. Conversely, do not insert v into every consonant-final stem just because it appeared with drink and eat.

### Practice

Describe the activity with the indicated subject.

1. He is drinking milk.
2. She is eating food.
3. I am drinking tea. (female speaker)
4. They are eating food. (masculine plural)

***Answers:***

1. `Su chu dɔd ceva:n.`
2. `Sɔ cha batɨ kheva:n.`
3. `Bɨ chas ca:y ceva:n.`
4. `Tim chi batɨ kheva:n.`

### Why these answers work

Drink and eat use the v-containing participles. The finite auxiliary agrees with the person doing the action. Milk, tea and food are objects here, so their noun gender does not replace the subject agreement of this present construction.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe a café scene with a man drinking milk, a woman drinking tea and several people eating. Then move yourself into one role. Say which part marks the activity and which part identifies the participant's person, gender or number.

## Lesson 11: A present form can describe a habit

### Outcome and thinking point

Use the same present construction for a repeated activity when context supports it. English distinguishes “is going” and “goes” morphologically. Must Kashmiri build two different present participles for those two English translations? Examine the role of a frequency word instead.

The auxiliary plus -a:n construction can describe ongoing activities and habits. `Aslam chu dɔhay daphtar gatsha:n.` means “Aslam goes to the office daily.” The frequency word makes a routine reading natural. Without that context, a similar clause can describe a current journey.

### Routines and destinations

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Use |
|---|---|---|
| dɔhay | daily | frequency |
| hame:shɨ | always | frequency |
| daphtar | office | destination |
| soku:l | school | destination |
| ba:zar | market | destination |
| gatsh | go | stem |
| gatsha:n | going, goes in context | present participle |
| ca:y | tea | drink object |

### Translate the meaning in context

`Su chu dɔhay soku:l gatsha:n.` means “He goes to school daily.” `Sɔ cha ba:zar gatsha:n.` can mean “She is going to the market” in a current scene. Do not insist on a habit reading just because you have previously translated -a:n as an English simple present.

`Bɨ chas dɔhay akhba:r para:n.` means “I read a newspaper daily”, for a female speaker. Reading keeps the same participle as in the current-activity lesson. The frequency expression changes the expected time profile of the event, while the auxiliary still agrees with the speaker.

A reliable translation asks what the speaker is presenting: an activity now, a repeated routine or a general fact. Grammatical forms divide that meaning differently across languages. English tense labels can help organise a comparison, but they are not universal boxes that every language fills with identical structures.

### Common trap

Do not import an Urdu habitual -tā form to create a second Kashmiri present tense. The construction demonstrated here already covers habitual readings in suitable contexts. Also avoid claiming every -a:n clause is habitual; the earlier ongoing examples remain valid.

### Practice

Use frequency to make the routine explicit.

1. He goes to school daily.
2. Aslam goes to the office daily.
3. I read a newspaper daily. (female speaker)
4. She is going to the market. (current scene)

***Answers:***

1. `Su chu dɔhay soku:l gatsha:n.`
2. `Aslam chu dɔhay daphtar gatsha:n.`
3. `Bɨ chas dɔhay akhba:r para:n.`
4. `Sɔ cha ba:zar gatsha:n.`

### Why these answers work

The first three include a daily frequency word, supporting habitual English translations. The last is interpreted as a current journey from the stated context. The auxiliary and participle retain their Kashmiri forms rather than copying separate English present-tense constructions.

### Retrieve and recombine

Give a daily school routine and a current market journey for the same person. Then describe a daily reading habit. Explain why the English translations use different present forms even though the Kashmiri activity construction remains recognisably the same.

## Lesson 12: Locate someone here, there or elsewhere

### Outcome and thinking point

Answer a location question without first learning a large set of noun-case endings. Complete location words let you build useful sentences immediately. Predict whether here should appear before or after the finite copula in an ordinary person-first statement.

`Su chu yeti.` means “He is here.” `Sɔ cha tati.` means “She is there.” The finite link follows the person and the location completes the clause. Yeti and tati are whole location expressions, so they do not need an added English in or at.

### Place words

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Contrast |
|---|---|---|
| yeti | here | near speaker/context centre |
| hoti | there | remote visible reference |
| tati | there | established remote reference |
| kati | where | location question |
| andar | inside | location |
| nebar | outside | location |
| garɨ | home | common home expression |

### Ask where, then answer

`Su kati chu?` asks “Where is he?” `Su chu andar.` answers “He is inside.” The location question follows the topic-plus-question-word order already encountered in identity questions. The answer returns to the simple subject, copula, location pattern.

`Bɨ chas yeti.` means “I am here”, for a female speaker. `Toh' chiv tati.` respectfully states “You are there” in the chiv pattern. The location word does not control the copula; the person being located does. This remains true even when the location is an enclosed room or a distant building.

Pointing words depend on shared context. Here can mean beside the speaker, within the present building or at a place established as the centre of the conversation. It is not a universal number of metres. When precision matters, a named place and a fuller location phrase may be needed.

### Common trap

Do not treat a location word as an object of the copula that determines gender. He remains masculine and she feminine regardless of being inside or outside. Also keep the question order separate from the answer order instead of moving the copula to one fixed position in every clause type.

### Practice

Locate the stated person.

1. He is outside.
2. She is here.
3. Where is he?
4. I am inside. (female speaker)

***Answers:***

1. `Su chu nebar.`
2. `Sɔ cha yeti.`
3. `Su kati chu?`
4. `Bɨ chas andar.`

### Why these answers work

The statements place the finite link after the person and the location after that. The question inserts kati before the copula after its topic. Copula agreement follows the person, not the place. Inside and outside are already complete location expressions.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe a man outside and a woman inside. Ask where the man is and answer with there. Then announce your own location. Explain which information is supplied by the place word and which is supplied by the copula.

## Lesson 13: Put something in a garden or on a table

### Outcome and thinking point

Build a location phrase with a noun and a postposition. Unlike the complete word here, a phrase such as in the garden combines a place with a relationship. Predict which comes first in Kashmiri: the garden noun or the word meaning in.

`Ba:gas manz` means “in the garden”. The noun bāg appears here as ba:gas, a case-marked form, before manz. The relationship word follows its noun. Case marking is not an optional decoration added only when English happens to have a preposition.

### Location phrases to use as units

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| ba:g | garden | citation form |
| ba:gas manz | in the garden | noun case plus postposition |
| me:zas peth | on the table | noun case plus postposition |
| manz | in | postposition |
| peth | on | postposition |
| kalam | pen | located item |
| kursi: | chair | located item |
| yeti | here | complete place word |

### Keep the located item separate

`Kalam chu ba:gas manz.` means “The pen is in the garden.” `Kalam chu me:zas peth.` means “The pen is on the table.” The pen controls masculine chu, while the garden and table belong inside location phrases. Their grammatical forms are determined by the relationship being expressed.

`Kursi: cha ba:gas manz.` means “The chair is in the garden.” Changing the located item to a feminine chair changes the copula but does not change the garden phrase. This is a good test of whether you have correctly identified the subject rather than choosing agreement from the nearest noun.

The small table supplies complete phrases intentionally. Kashmiri case formation interacts with noun class, sound changes and postposition choice. Learn these verified combinations before generalising a universal -s rule. New nouns should be checked in a dictionary or grammar for the relevant case form rather than forced into the garden pattern.

### Common trap

Do not replace the case-marked ba:gas with a bare ba:g simply because English leaves garden unchanged. Equally, do not infer that every postposition takes exactly the same case or creates the same ending. The relationship and noun class both matter.

### Practice

Use the demonstrated location phrases.

1. The pen is on the table.
2. The chair is in the garden.
3. The pen is in the garden.
4. Give the phrase “in the garden”.

***Answers:***

1. `Kalam chu me:zas peth.`
2. `Kursi: cha ba:gas manz.`
3. `Kalam chu ba:gas manz.`
4. `Ba:gas manz`

### Why these answers work

The item being located selects the copula. Garden and table occur in their supplied case-marked phrases before the postpositions. The last answer is a phrase because the prompt requests only the location expression, not a full statement.

### Retrieve and recombine

Move a pen from a table to a garden in your imagined scene. Then locate a chair in the same garden. Explain why the place phrase stays constant when the located item changes, while the copula may change.

## Lesson 14: Mark a person as a recipient

### Outcome and thinking point

Say who receives a book and recognise a different pronoun form for to me. You have used bɨ as the subject I. Should it remain unchanged when the speaker becomes the recipient of somebody else's giving? Compare the roles before choosing a pronoun.

`Su chu me kita:b diva:n.` means “He is giving me a book.” Me marks the recipient; su is the giver. The finite auxiliary agrees with the giver in this present construction, while the book is the transferred object and diva:n expresses the giving activity.

### Participant forms

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Role |
|---|---|---|
| bɨ | I | ordinary subject |
| me | to me, me in case-marked constructions | dative/ergative form by context |
| asi | to us | dative form in appropriate constructions |
| təmis | to him or her | third-person dative |
| kita:b | book | transferred thing |
| di | give | stem |
| diva:n | giving | present participle |
| ra:jas | to Raj | case-marked name |

### Build the event from roles

`Bɨ chus təmis kita:b diva:n.` means “I am giving him or her a book”, for a male speaker. `Bɨ chas təmis kita:b diva:n.` is the female-speaker form. The recipient form təmis does not itself force the auxiliary into masculine or feminine third person, because the recipient is not the subject of the giving clause.

`Sɔ cha me kita:b diva:n.` means “She is giving me a book.” You can change the giver from he to she while preserving the recipient and object. This makes a useful alternative to memorising an undifferentiated phrase meaning give me a book: a full statement identifies who is actually performing the transfer.

Second-person case forms interact with additional agreement and pronominal suffixes in Kashmiri. They should not be inferred by simply adding an English-like to to tsɨ. This lesson limits production to first- and third-person recipients, where the demonstrated forms are clear. Later study can add the more complex second-person system with proper evidence.

### Common trap

Do not use bɨ in every position where English uses I or me. Case distinguishes grammatical roles. Nor should you choose chus just because the recipient is the speaker; the giver remains the agreement controller in the present giving examples.

### Practice

Keep giver, recipient and object distinct.

1. He is giving me a book.
2. She is giving me a book.
3. I am giving that person a book. (male speaker)
4. I am giving that person a book. (female speaker)

***Answers:***

1. `Su chu me kita:b diva:n.`
2. `Sɔ cha me kita:b diva:n.`
3. `Bɨ chus təmis kita:b diva:n.`
4. `Bɨ chas təmis kita:b diva:n.`

### Why these answers work

The subject identifies the giver and controls the present auxiliary. Me and təmis identify recipients, while the book remains the thing given. The giving participle stays diva:n across the subject changes, just as reading and eating participles remained stable earlier.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe a book passing from a man to you and then from you to another person. Name the three roles before each sentence. Explain why changing the recipient does not automatically change the auxiliary, and why the speaker's pronoun differs as giver and recipient.

## Lesson 15: Say what you like

### Outcome and thinking point

Express a preference using a case-marked experiencer. English I like begins with an ordinary subject, but Kashmiri can frame a thing as pleasing to a person. If the speaker appears as me, which noun might then control the copula?

`Me chu yi rang pasand.` means “I like this colour.” Me identifies the experiencer; the masculine colour noun rang supplies the theme. The copula is chu, not first-person chus. Changing the speaker's sex does not change the sentence, because the speaker is not the ordinary nominative subject here.

### Preference vocabulary

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Role |
|---|---|---|
| me | to me | experiencer |
| təmis | to him or her | experiencer |
| rang | colour | masculine theme |
| yi rang | this colour | theme phrase |
| pasand | pleasing, liked | preference expression |
| ca:y | tea | feminine theme |
| chu | is | masculine agreement |
| cha | is | feminine agreement |

### Change the liked thing

`Me cha ca:y pasand.` means “I like tea.” The feminine theme selects cha. `Təmis chu yi rang pasand.` means “He or she likes this colour.” The experiencer can change while the theme and its agreement remain the same. This is the reverse of the subject-agreeing present-action pattern practised earlier.

`Me cha nɨ ca:y pasand.` means “I do not like tea.” The negative follows the finite copula, preserving the familiar negation position. You do not need to create a new first-person negative form of like. Instead, build the theme-agreeing preference construction and then negate it.

A preference is not automatically an immediate request. Saying that you like tea may explain your general taste; asking someone to give tea is a different action. Keep the communicative purpose clear, especially in a service exchange where the listener needs to know whether you are ordering something now.

### Common trap

Do not turn me back into bɨ and keep the rest of the sentence unchanged. That would confuse the experiencer construction with an ordinary action-subject construction. Also do not force chus into every sentence translated with English I.

### Practice

Express the preference with the supplied theme.

1. I like this colour.
2. I like tea.
3. That person likes this colour.
4. I do not like tea.

***Answers:***

1. `Me chu yi rang pasand.`
2. `Me cha ca:y pasand.`
3. `Təmis chu yi rang pasand.`
4. `Me cha nɨ ca:y pasand.`

### Why these answers work

Me and təmis are case-marked experiencers. Colour and tea are the themes that determine masculine or feminine agreement. The negative follows the finite copula. The speaker's gender is irrelevant to the choice between chu and cha in these examples.

### Retrieve and recombine

Express your liking for a colour and for tea, then give the same colour preference for another person. Negate one preference. Explain why changing the experiencer and changing the theme have different effects on the copula.

## Lesson 16: Express having work and holding an object

### Outcome and thinking point

Distinguish an available task or resource from possession of a movable object. English have covers many relationships. Should Kashmiri necessarily express all of them with one ordinary verb? Begin with a case-marked possessor and inspect the item possessed.

`Me cha ka:m.` means “I have work.” The word ka:m is feminine in this construction, so the copula is cha. `Təmis cha ka:m.` means “He or she has work.” As in the preference construction, the person is case-marked and does not force first-person or third-person subject agreement onto the copula.

### Possession pieces

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Function |
|---|---|---|
| me | to me | possessor in this pattern |
| təmis | to him or her | possessor |
| ka:m | work | feminine possessed theme |
| va:riya:h | much, many | quantity expression |
| nishi | near, with | possession-related postposition |
| athi | with, in hand | possession-related expression |
| kalam | pen | movable object |
| kita:b | book | movable object |

### Do not flatten all kinds of possession

`Me cha va:riya:h ka:m.` means “I have a lot of work.” `Təmis cha nɨ ka:m.` means “He or she does not have work.” The negative still follows the finite copula. The amount of work can change without turning the experiencer into an ordinary subject.

For temporary possession of objects, Kashmiri uses additional relational expressions, including nishi and athi with appropriate case forms. Learn the actual combinations rather than translating English have by adding one word after every pronoun. The current answer key practises the transparent work construction; object-possession phrases require their own person-and-case patterns.

This distinction is useful in real life. Being assigned work, owning a pen, having a book temporarily in your hand and having a family relationship are not the same relationship even if English uses have for all of them. Kashmiri makes some of those distinctions through its case and postpositional structure.

### Common trap

Do not assume the phrase me cha ka:m proves that every object can be substituted without any further grammatical choice. A narrowly learned construction can be useful while still having limits. Check the possession type before extending it to a new noun or person.

### Practice

Use the work-possession construction.

1. I have work.
2. That person has work.
3. I have a lot of work.
4. That person does not have work.

***Answers:***

1. `Me cha ka:m.`
2. `Təmis cha ka:m.`
3. `Me cha va:riya:h ka:m.`
4. `Təmis cha nɨ ka:m.`

### Why these answers work

The possessor is case-marked and the feminine work theme selects cha. Adding a quantity word changes the amount rather than the person agreement. Negation follows the finite copula. The keys avoid pretending that every English-have meaning shares this exact structure.

### Retrieve and recombine

Say whether you and another person have work, and give one amount contrast. Then explain in English why borrowing a pen is a different possession problem. Identify what additional phrase you would need to verify before extending the pattern to that object.

## Lesson 17: Identify my book and my pen

### Outcome and thinking point

Distinguish ownership inside a noun phrase from a sentence saying someone has work. In English my stays unchanged before every possession. Could Kashmiri make a possessive agree with the thing owned? Compare a pen and a book while keeping the owner constant.

`M'o:n kalam` means “my pen”. `Me:n' kita:b` means “my book”. The possessive forms differ with the grammatical properties of the possessed noun. The owner's sex is not what selects the ending. Palatalisation also belongs to these forms and should remain visible in the transcription.

### Ownership phrases

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Information |
|---|---|---|
| m'o:n | my | masculine singular possessed noun |
| me:n' | my | feminine singular or masculine plural form |
| m'a:ni | my | feminine plural form |
| kalam | pen | masculine |
| kita:b | book | feminine |
| do:s | friend | masculine in this lesson |
| yi | this | identifying subject |
| chu | is | masculine agreement |

### Build the identification around the phrase

`Yi chu m'o:n kalam.` means “This is my pen.” `Yi cha me:n' kita:b.` means “This is my book.” You can inspect the sentence at two levels: the possessive agrees within the noun phrase, and the copula agrees in the larger identification clause. Both levels respond to the pen-versus-book contrast.

`Su chu m'o:n do:s.` means “He is my friend”, with a masculine friend. The possessive does not become a feminine form merely because a woman is speaking. The relationship word friend is the possessed or relational noun within the phrase. A possessive can mark a social relationship without implying that people are property.

`Yi chu nɨ m'o:n kalam.` means “This is not my pen.” The negative follows the finite copula and rejects the ownership identification. It does not necessarily claim that the object is not a pen at all. Scope comes from the description being negated and the conversational context.

### Common trap

Do not use me from the dative experiencer lessons as an unchanging equivalent of my. Case-marked personal forms and possessives have different jobs. Also do not discard apostrophes in m'o:n and me:n' as if they were English quotation marks; they record palatalisation.

### Practice

Build the ownership identification.

1. This is my pen.
2. This is my book.
3. He is my friend. (masculine friend)
4. This is not my pen.

***Answers:***

1. `Yi chu m'o:n kalam.`
2. `Yi cha me:n' kita:b.`
3. `Su chu m'o:n do:s.`
4. `Yi chu nɨ m'o:n kalam.`

### Why these answers work

The possessive follows the possessed noun's gender and number pattern. The copula matches the identified item in the larger clause. The negative retains the same ownership phrase while rejecting the claim. The owner's gender does not determine m'o:n versus me:n'.

### Retrieve and recombine

Identify your pen and book, then identify a male friend. Say that a second pen is not yours. Explain why my changes before book but remains the same when a different-gender speaker refers to a masculine pen.

## Lesson 18: Count a small set of objects

### Outcome and thinking point

State a quantity without expecting every noun to reveal plurality through a suffix. You already know plural demonstratives and copulas. Add small numerals and predict what remains unchanged in a statement about three pens.

`Yim chi tre kalam.` means “These are three pens.” The numeral specifies the quantity, while yim and chi establish the plural identification. Kalam retains the demonstrated direct plural form. The sentence does not need a separate English-style plural ending on every word.

### Small numbers

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Learning cue |
|---|---|---|
| akh | one | singular quantity |
| zɨ | two | central vowel |
| tre | three | short e |
| tsor | four | dental affricate |
| pã:tsh | five | long nasalised vowel |
| she | six | short e |
| kalam | pen, pens | demonstrated count noun |
| yim | these | masculine plural |

### Count and identify separately

`Yi chu akh kalam.` means “This is one pen.” `Yim chi zɨ kalam.` means “These are two pens.” The first contrasts one item with more than one; the second makes the quantity explicit. In a neutral identification, an article-like one may be unnecessary, but it is useful when counting matters.

`Hum chi tre me:z.` means “Those are three tables”, using the visible distant masculine plural form. Here the demonstrative tells the listener which group to count. The numeral alone does not identify the group: three tables could refer to many different sets in a room.

Numerals participate in case patterns when the noun phrase enters other grammatical relationships. This lesson uses direct identification phrases, where the forms in the table are appropriate. Do not carry the same numeral forms unchanged into every “with two”, “after three” or other case-marked phrase without checking the construction.

### Common trap

Do not pronounce ts as two unrelated syllables or replace it automatically with English ch. Kashmiri distinguishes dental and palatal affricates. In the number five, the nasalisation and vowel length are also meaningful sound information, not typography added for decoration.

### Practice

Identify the counted objects.

1. This is one pen.
2. These are two pens.
3. These are three pens.
4. Those are three tables. (visible distant masculine plural)

***Answers:***

1. `Yi chu akh kalam.`
2. `Yim chi zɨ kalam.`
3. `Yim chi tre kalam.`
4. `Hum chi tre me:z.`

### Why these answers work

One combines with the singular identification pattern, while two and three combine with plural demonstratives and chi. The count nouns retain their demonstrated direct forms. The distant demonstrative in the final answer locates the set being counted.

### Retrieve and recombine

Count one pen near you, two pens beside it and three tables across the room. Explain which word supplies exact quantity, which identifies the group and which supplies finite agreement. Then count the same objects without a sentence to practise the numeral sounds separately.

## Lesson 19: Give a short instruction

### Outcome and thinking point

Tell a familiar person to read, drink or eat. A statement describing what someone is doing is not automatically an instruction. Predict whether a direct imperative requires the present auxiliary or can use the action stem itself.

`Kita:b par.` means “Read the book”, as a direct familiar instruction. `Ca:y ce.` means “Drink the tea.” The action stem functions as an imperative in these examples. There is no present auxiliary because the clause is directing an action rather than describing an ongoing one.

### Direct action forms

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Function |
|---|---|---|
| par | read | direct imperative/stem |
| ce | drink | direct imperative/stem |
| khe | eat | direct imperative/stem |
| an | bring | direct imperative/stem |
| vuch | look, see | direct imperative/stem |
| kita:b | book | object |
| batɨ | food | object |
| ca:y | tea | object |

### Objects precede the instruction

`Batɨ khe.` means “Eat the food.” `Kalam an.` means “Bring the pen.” These short clauses place the object before the imperative. They do not use the finite-second statement pattern by inserting chu after the object. Imperatives have their own structure, just as information questions did.

`Kita:b vuch.` means “Look at the book.” Reading and looking are different actions even when they take the same object. Before choosing a verb, identify what you actually want the listener to do. This practical distinction matters more than producing a longer sentence with vague meaning.

A direct imperative can be natural among familiar people, in instruction or in an urgent situation. It can also sound abrupt with an unfamiliar adult. The next lesson introduces a polite reading form, but social context remains important. No suffix alone turns every demand into a considerate request.

### Common trap

Do not say a present participle such as para:n when you intend the direct instruction par. The participle describes reading as an activity and normally combines with a finite auxiliary in the present construction. The imperative addresses the listener directly and has no such auxiliary here.

### Practice

Give a direct familiar instruction.

1. Read the book.
2. Drink the tea.
3. Eat the food.
4. Bring the pen.

***Answers:***

1. `Kita:b par.`
2. `Ca:y ce.`
3. `Batɨ khe.`
4. `Kalam an.`

### Why these answers work

Each answer places an object before a direct imperative stem. No present auxiliary or participial ending is required. The short form is appropriate to the explicitly familiar instruction context; it is not presented as a universal polite request for every listener.

### Retrieve and recombine

Imagine helping a familiar learner organise a desk and take a tea break. Ask them to bring a pen, read a book and drink tea. Then describe one of those actions as happening now, and explain why the imperative stem and present participle differ.

## Lesson 20: Make a polite request and a prohibition

### Outcome and thinking point

Ask for reading politely and tell a familiar person not to perform an action. If ordinary statements put the negative after the finite verb, should a negative imperative necessarily do the same? Compare the location of the imperative negative before choosing your answer.

`Kita:b par'tav.` means “Please read the book”, using the polite plural form shown here. `Kita:b mɨ par.` means “Do not read the book”, as a familiar prohibition. The negative imperative particle mɨ precedes the action, unlike nɨ after the finite copula in a statement.

### Request contrasts

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Context |
|---|---|---|
| par | read | direct familiar instruction |
| par'tav | please read | polite plural request |
| mɨ | do not | familiar imperative negative |
| matɨ | do not | polite imperative negative |
| akhba:r | newspaper | object |
| kita:b | book | object |
| ca:y | tea | object |
| batɨ | food | object |

### Preserve the purpose of the turn

`Akhba:r par'tav.` asks “Please read the newspaper.” A teacher might request reading aloud, or a colleague might ask someone to inspect a report. The short sentence does not specify every detail of how to read, but the context can make that purpose clear.

`Ca:y mɨ ce.` means “Do not drink the tea”, in a familiar context. `Batɨ mɨ khe.` means “Do not eat the food.” These may be practical warnings. The prohibition does not describe the person's regular habits; it directs them not to act now or in the relevant situation.

The polite imperative system includes more forms than the one reading example. Stem changes and suffixes should be learned with evidence, especially when an object pronoun is attached to the verb. This course uses a verified polite reading pattern and familiar negative stems rather than inventing a complete polite paradigm from one ending.

### Common trap

Do not use the ordinary declarative negative nɨ after a bare imperative by copying the statement order. Likewise, do not treat the polite reading suffix as a universal appendage to any stem without checking the resulting form. Clause type and verb morphology both matter.

### Practice

Use the specified request or prohibition.

1. Please read the book. (polite plural form)
2. Please read the newspaper. (polite plural form)
3. Do not drink the tea. (familiar)
4. Do not eat the food. (familiar)

***Answers:***

1. `Kita:b par'tav.`
2. `Akhba:r par'tav.`
3. `Ca:y mɨ ce.`
4. `Batɨ mɨ khe.`

### Why these answers work

The first two preserve the polite reading form while changing the object. The prohibitions use mɨ before the direct imperative. They do not need a copula or a present participle because the clauses direct actions rather than report ongoing events.

### Retrieve and recombine

Ask someone politely to read a book, then warn a familiar person not to drink a particular tea. Describe in English how their purposes differ. Finally give a negative statement about tea being ready and compare its negative position with the prohibition.

## Lesson 21: Add today, yesterday and tomorrow

### Outcome and thinking point

Place an event in time while respecting the finite-verb pattern. English often begins a sentence with today. If a time expression becomes the first constituent in a Kashmiri statement, where should the finite verb go? Predict the order before checking the worked example.

`Az chu Aslam ba:zar gatsha:n.` means “Today Aslam is going to the market.” Az is the first constituent, and chu follows it. The subject then appears after the finite verb. This shows why verb-second means second constituent, not “always after the subject”.

### Time and sequence words

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Use |
|---|---|---|
| az | today | present-day frame |
| ra:th | yesterday | past-day frame |
| paga:h | tomorrow | next-day frame |
| dɔhay | daily | frequency |
| subɨhan | in the morning | time expression |
| sha:man | in the evening | time expression |
| hame:shɨ | always | frequency |
| gatsha:n | going | activity participle |

### Keep time and aspect distinct

`Aslam chu az ba:zar gatsha:n.` presents the same basic information with Aslam as the first constituent. The difference concerns what is placed first as the conversational starting point. Both retain the finite verb in the demonstrated second position. A beginner can use the subject-first version while learning to recognise the time-first version.

`Az cha sɔ kita:b para:n.` means “Today she is reading a book.” The time word does not force masculine agreement; the feminine subject still selects cha even though it follows the copula. This is a useful check against choosing agreement from whichever word happens to stand first.

Yesterday and tomorrow are distinct lexical forms here. They do not share one ambiguous day word as in some neighbouring languages. The verb must still express the intended temporal or aspectual relationship. The next lessons provide small past and future constructions rather than asking a day word to do all the grammatical work.

### Common trap

Do not force the subject before the auxiliary after you have already put today first in the verb-second pattern. Also do not equate a time adverb with tense: an explicit future form and a word meaning tomorrow contribute different information to the clause.

### Practice

Use the requested starting point.

1. Today Aslam is going to the market. (start with today)
2. Aslam is going to the market today. (start with Aslam)
3. Today she is reading a book. (start with today)
4. Give the Kashmiri word for tomorrow.

***Answers:***

1. `Az chu Aslam ba:zar gatsha:n.`
2. `Aslam chu az ba:zar gatsha:n.`
3. `Az cha sɔ kita:b para:n.`
4. `Paga:h`

### Why these answers work

The finite auxiliary follows the first constituent in each statement. It still agrees with the subject, even when the subject comes after it. The last answer is a time word only because the prompt requests lexical retrieval rather than a complete future sentence.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe today's reading activity first with the person as your starting point and then with today first. Explain what moved and what stayed in the second position. Contrast today with the separate words for yesterday and tomorrow before using them in later tense patterns.

## Lesson 22: Describe a past location or condition

### Outcome and thinking point

Say where a man was and whether he was ready. This lesson introduces a deliberately small past-state set before the more complex completed-action system. If a present description uses chu, what changes when the same state is located in the past?

`Su o:s taya:r.` means “He was ready.” `Su o:s garɨ.` means “He was at home.” The past copula o:s replaces present chu in these third-person masculine singular states. It does not by itself narrate a journey or the act of becoming ready.

### A limited past-state set

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Context |
|---|---|---|
| o:s | was | third-person masculine singular |
| o:sus | was | first-person masculine singular |
| chu | is | present comparison |
| chus | am | present comparison |
| ra:th | yesterday | time |
| garɨ | home | location |
| taya:r | ready | state |
| yeti | here | location |

### Build time and state separately

`Bɨ o:sus yeti.` means “I was here”, for a male speaker. `Bɨ o:sus nɨ taya:r.` means “I was not ready”, again for a male speaker. The person ending remains relevant in the past. Negation follows the finite copula just as it did in present statements.

`Aslam o:s ra:th garɨ.` means “Aslam was at home yesterday.” The time word identifies the day; the past copula identifies the temporal state construction. You could change home to here without changing the past tense, or change yesterday to another established past time without changing the location phrase.

Kashmiri past morphology distinguishes several temporal and aspectual relationships and interacts with person, gender and case. The female and plural paradigms should not be guessed from this masculine pair. This lesson gives a usable narrow set with explicit subject restrictions. It does not present o:s as an all-purpose past word for every subject.

### Common trap

Do not use o:sus simply because you are the speaker if you are describing Aslam. First-person marking belongs only when I is the grammatical subject. Likewise, do not interpret being at home as proof that the sentence has narrated going home.

### Practice

Use the limited past-state forms taught here.

1. He was ready.
2. He was at home.
3. I was here. (male speaker)
4. I was not ready. (male speaker)

***Answers:***

1. `Su o:s taya:r.`
2. `Su o:s garɨ.`
3. `Bɨ o:sus yeti.`
4. `Bɨ o:sus nɨ taya:r.`

### Why these answers work

Third-person masculine singular uses o:s, while first-person masculine singular uses o:sus. The negative follows the finite copula. The remaining material describes a state or location, so these are not completed transitive clauses with an object agreement pattern.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe Aslam's location yesterday and your own readiness then, using a male speaker for the first-person form. Turn the two statements back into the present. Explain what expresses time, what expresses person and what merely supplies the location or condition.

## Lesson 23: State a simple future plan

### Outcome and thinking point

Say that you or another person will go somewhere. The present copula distinguishes masculine and feminine subjects, but must a future verb preserve the same gender distinction? Compare male and female third-person plans with the same verb form.

`Su gatshi garɨ.` means “He will go home.” `Sɔ gatshi garɨ.` means “She will go home.” The basic future form gatshi is the same for these two third-person singular subjects. Future agreement is not marked for gender in the way the present copula is.

### A small future set

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Context |
|---|---|---|
| gatshɨ | will go | first-person singular |
| gatshi | will go | third-person singular |
| parɨ | will read | first-person singular |
| pari | will read | third-person singular |
| garɨ | home | destination |
| ba:zar | market | destination |
| paga:h | tomorrow | time |
| kita:b | book | reading object |

### Build the plan without a present auxiliary

`Bɨ gatshɨ ba:zar.` means “I will go to the market.” The same first-person future form is used without a male-female contrast in this basic construction. `Bɨ parɨ yi kita:b.` means “I will read this book.” The main verb is finite; there is no need to insert chus or chas as though the sentence were a present activity.

`Sɔ gatshi paga:h ba:zar.` means “She will go to the market tomorrow.” The person-first pattern places the finite future immediately after the subject. Tomorrow supplies the day, while the future form supplies the verbal relationship. You can understand the plan even without a separate English-like will word.

Kashmiri also has future forms with more complex subject-object suffixation. Those are not taught as a guessable extension of this small table. The course uses the basic future pattern with clear subjects and ordinary destinations or inanimate objects, keeping the scope of the rule explicit.

### Common trap

Do not add a feminine gī ending borrowed from Hindi, Urdu or Punjabi. The Kashmiri future pattern here does not express gender that way. Also distinguish the first-person central vowel ɨ from the third-person i; they identify different person forms.

### Practice

State the simple future plan.

1. I will go to the market.
2. He will go home.
3. She will go home.
4. I will read this book.

***Answers:***

1. `Bɨ gatshɨ ba:zar.`
2. `Su gatshi garɨ.`
3. `Sɔ gatshi garɨ.`
4. `Bɨ parɨ yi kita:b.`

### Why these answers work

The basic future verb is finite and stands after the subject. First-person singular uses the demonstrated central-vowel form; third-person singular uses i. Male and female third-person subjects share the same future verb, unlike their different present copulas.

### Retrieve and recombine

Give a market plan for yourself and a homeward plan for a man and a woman. Add tomorrow to one plan. Explain why changing gender changes the pronoun but does not add a new gender ending to the basic future verb.

## Lesson 24: Join two claims in a practical exchange

### Outcome and thinking point

Connect a positive claim with a contrast and keep each clause's grammar intact. If tea is ready but someone is not ready, does joining the claims allow you to omit all finite links? Build each small clause first, then choose a connector.

`Ca:y cha taya:r magar bɨ chus nɨ taya:r.` means “The tea is ready, but I am not ready”, for a male speaker. The two clauses have different subjects and different agreement. Magar presents the contrast without replacing either clause's grammar.

### Connecting and responding

| Kashmiri | Meaning | Use |
|---|---|---|
| tɨ | and | coordination |
| magar | but | contrast |
| ti | also | addition |
| a: | yes | short response |
| na | no | short response |
| taya:r | ready | shared condition |
| kita:b | book | discussion object |
| par'tav | please read | polite request |

### Give each turn a purpose

`Su chu ma:sṭar tɨ bɨ chus duka:nda:r.` means “He is a teacher and I am a shopkeeper”, for a male speaker. Each clause retains its appropriate copula. The connector tɨ has a central vowel; ti, meaning also, has a different vowel and function. Do not collapse them merely because their Latin spellings are short.

A small class exchange can begin with `Yi k'a: chu?`, “What is this?” The reply `Yi cha me:n' kita:b.`, “This is my book”, identifies both object and ownership. `Kita:b par'tav.`, “Please read the book”, then changes the purpose from identification to a requested action.

A short yes or no is useful only when the previous question is clear. When correcting an identity or a negative statement, a complete clause can make the intended meaning easier to understand. Use your negation pattern rather than assuming that one affirmative particle will resolve every conversational ambiguity.

### Common trap

Do not use ti as a general substitute for and, or tɨ as an interchangeable spelling of also. Small function words carry distinct meanings. Also avoid dropping a finite verb across clauses with different subjects unless you have learned the relevant ellipsis construction.

### Practice

Connect or request the stated action.

1. The tea is ready, but I am not ready. (male speaker)
2. He is a teacher and I am a shopkeeper. (male speaker)
3. Please read the book.
4. Give the Kashmiri word for “but”.

***Answers:***

1. `Ca:y cha taya:r magar bɨ chus nɨ taya:r.`
2. `Su chu ma:sṭar tɨ bɨ chus duka:nda:r.`
3. `Kita:b par'tav.`
4. `Magar`

### Why these answers work

The contrast and addition sentences preserve the finite grammar inside each clause. The polite request is a different clause type and uses its own imperative form. The final answer is the connector alone because that is the lexical item requested.

### Retrieve and recombine

Create a short classroom exchange: identify a book, say whose it is and request reading. Add a contrast about somebody being ready. Explain the purpose of each turn and distinguish the connector and from the particle also.

## Lesson 25: Build a small account and explain your choices

### Outcome and thinking point

Combine identity, location, current activity, preference and a future plan. Before producing each sentence, ask which construction it needs. A person can appear as a subject in one clause and as a case-marked experiencer in another, so English I does not guarantee a single Kashmiri form.

Use an imagined male learner for first-person examples in this review, then make the supported changes for a female learner where the course has taught them. The goal is a small, explainable account with verified patterns. A limited foundation becomes useful through accurate recombination, not through unsupported claims of comprehensive mastery.

### Review map

| Kashmiri | Meaning | What to notice |
|---|---|---|
| bɨ chus | I am, masculine | person and gender |
| bɨ chas | I am, feminine | different copula |
| su chu | he is | third person |
| sɔ cha | she is | feminine reference |
| me chu yi rang pasand | I like this colour | case-marked experiencer |
| bɨ o:sus | I was, masculine | past state |
| bɨ gatshɨ | I will go | basic future |
| kita:b par'tav | please read the book | polite imperative |

### Follow the changing grammatical roles

`Bɨ chus yeti.` says “I am here”, for a male speaker. `Bɨ chus kita:b para:n.` says “I am reading a book.” The same person controls the auxiliary in both, but the first is a location and the second an activity with a participle.

`Me cha ca:y pasand.` means “I like tea.” The speaker now appears as me, and tea controls feminine cha. `Bɨ gatshɨ paga:h ba:zar.` means “I will go to the market tomorrow.” The future form does not retain the present copula's gender distinction. These differences follow the construction, not arbitrary exceptions invented for individual sentences.

Now introduce another person: `Sɔ cha kita:b para:n.` says “She is reading a book.” The present participle remains unchanged while the auxiliary follows the female subject. Compare a future plan for her with one for him: gatshi serves both in the basic future pattern. This contrast is a useful test of whether you are reasoning from Kashmiri rather than importing another language's agreement rules.

### Common trap

Do not call a sentence correct merely because its English translation looks familiar. Identify the finite verb, the subject or theme controlling agreement, the role of each case-marked person and the clause type. When a new construction falls outside the taught set, check it rather than extending a partial pattern by guesswork.

### Practice

Recombine the course's central constructions.

1. She is reading a book.
2. I like tea.
3. I will go to the market tomorrow.
4. I was not ready. (male speaker)

***Answers:***

1. `Sɔ cha kita:b para:n.`
2. `Me cha ca:y pasand.`
3. `Bɨ gatshɨ paga:h ba:zar.`
4. `Bɨ o:sus nɨ taya:r.`

### Why these answers work

The first sentence has a female subject controlling the auxiliary. The second has a dative experiencer and feminine tea theme. The third uses a finite basic future with no gender distinction, and the last uses the explicitly taught masculine first-person past-state form followed by negation.

### Retrieve and recombine

Tell a five-sentence account with identity, location, reading activity, preference and tomorrow's plan. Ask one identity question and one location question about it, then make a polite reading request. Seek fluent-speaker feedback on pronunciation and natural phrasing, and use a dedicated native-script resource when beginning literacy rather than treating this romanisation as native spelling.
