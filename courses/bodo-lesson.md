# Bodo: A pattern-first foundation

This is an original foundation in the modern Bodo described by Aleendra Brahma. It uses a Roman phonemic reading notation, not Devanagari lessons. Devanagari is the main modern written script; native-script instruction and verified native-script answer keys are pending. This choice avoids reconstructing damaged native-script OCR. It does not imply that Roman transcription is the community's sole or preferred orthography.

Editorial status: original AI-assisted draft; fluent-speaker review pending.

The reading notation preserves the source's six vowel distinctions: i, e, a, ɔ, u, ɯ. The symbol ɯ represents a high unrounded vowel; it is not ordinary English u. ŋ is the final sound of English “sing.” We write ph, th, kh for aspirated stops, r for the source's tap symbol, and g for its phonetic g. The letter z is not an instruction to substitute Hindi j. Hyphens show grammatical boundaries in explanations; complete practice answers join suffixes to their words.

Bodo is tonal. In the introductory sound comparison, acute and grave accents retain the source's rising and falling labels: zá “eat” contrasts with zà “be/happen,” and hɯ̀ “give” contrasts with hɯ́ “drive away.” The grammar's running morphological examples are largely untoned, and our running examples likewise do not claim to provide complete tone or connected-speech transcriptions. Unmarked words are not claimed to lack tone. Use fluent-speaker audio before treating this written foundation as a pronunciation course.

## Lesson 1: Commit to a simple action

### Outcome and your first pattern

You will say that you will come or go. Bodo places the main action at the end of a neutral sentence. `aŋ phɯigɯn` means “I will come.” The pieces are `aŋ`, the speaker; `phɯi`, come; and `-gɯn`, the future suffix. This is already a useful commitment in a conversation about a visit. English uses a separate “will,” while Bodo attaches the future information to the verb.

### Think before reading on

If `thaŋ` means “go,” can you replace “come” without changing the person? Try it. `aŋ thaŋgɯn` means “I will go.” The person remains the speaker, and the same future suffix follows a different action. Do not introduce an extra linking verb equivalent to “am”; the future verb already completes the clause.

### A small action kit

| Bodo | Meaning | Notice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| aŋ | I | final ŋ is one nasal consonant |
| phɯi | come | ph is aspirated |
| thaŋ | go | th is an aspirated stop |
| za | eat | lexical tone matters in speech |
| lɯŋ | drink | ɯ is unrounded |
| -gɯn | future | attached to an action |
| gabɯn | tomorrow | gives an explicit future time |
| dinɯi | today | a day word, not a verb ending |

### Build from meaning

`aŋ gabɯn phɯigɯn` means “I will come tomorrow.” `aŋ dinɯi thaŋgɯn` means “I will go today.” A time phrase can be inserted before the final verb. The time word adds precision, while the suffix makes the future claim. “Today” can include an event later today, so it is compatible with a future verb.

The reading form `thaŋ` does not encode an English dental fricative as in “think.” The notation th means an aspirated stop, with a burst of breath after the closure. Likewise, ph is not English f. These contrasts help you interpret the notation, but the untoned sentence examples are not complete pronunciation instructions.

### Common trap

Do not replace `aŋ` with Hindi “main” or add a Hindi agreement ending. Bodo finite verbs do not change for masculine versus feminine speakers in these examples. The main decisions here are person reference, lexical action, and future meaning.

### Practice

Build one Bodo statement for each meaning.

1. I will come.
2. I will go.
3. I will come tomorrow.
4. I will go today.

***Answers:***

1. `aŋ phɯigɯn`
2. `aŋ thaŋgɯn`
3. `aŋ gabɯn phɯigɯn`
4. `aŋ dinɯi thaŋgɯn`

### Why these answers work

Every answer places the action last and attaches the future suffix directly to its stem. The final two add a time phrase without changing the subject. No additional word corresponding to English “will” or “am” is required.

### Retrieve and recombine

Imagine arranging two visits: one today and one tomorrow. State that you will come for one and go for the other. Explain which word identifies the day and which part makes the future commitment.

## Lesson 2: Change the person without inventing agreement

### Outcome and participant choice

You will use first, second, and third person with a known future action. `nɯŋ phɯigɯn` means “You will come,” using ordinary nonhonorific address. `bi phɯigɯn` means “He or she will come.” The verb keeps the same future form. This is a useful contrast with languages where changing the subject requires a new person ending.

### Think before reading on

If Bodo does not change this verb for each person, does that make the person irrelevant? No. The pronoun supplies the participant information. Compare `aŋ`, `nɯŋ`, and `bi` before the same predicate. You can change who is coming without changing the action or its future timing.

### People and respectful reference

| Bodo | Meaning | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| aŋ | I | individual speaker |
| zɯŋ | we | group including the speaker |
| nɯŋ | you | nonhonorific address |
| nɯŋthaŋ | you | honorific address |
| bi | he/she | third-person human reference |
| bithaŋ | he/she | honorific third-person reference |
| phɯigɯn | will come | unchanged across these subjects |
| thaŋgɯn | will go | another future action |

### Respect belongs to the pronoun choice

`nɯŋthaŋ phɯigɯn` means “You will come,” respectfully. `bithaŋ thaŋgɯn` means “He or she will go,” respectfully. Honorific reference is present even though the verb does not acquire a Hindi-style honorific agreement form. Do not infer that a language lacks politeness merely because it encodes it differently.

`zɯŋ gabɯn phɯigɯn` means “We will come tomorrow.” This group includes the speaker. `bi` and `bithaŋ` do not specify gender as English “he” and “she” do; context identifies the person. If the English prompt uses “she,” you need not invent a distinct feminine pronoun in the Bodo answer.

Bodo can mark a definite or discourse-established subject with additional morphology. The short personal-pronoun sentences here use a common unmarked frame. Later lessons introduce subject marking where it makes the structure clearer. The absence of that marker in these examples is not a claim that it never appears.

### Common trap

Do not equate honorific pronouns with plural pronouns. A single respected visitor can be `nɯŋthaŋ` or `bithaŋ`. Also avoid using ordinary `nɯŋ` as an unexamined default for every adult relationship; choose the honorific form when appropriate to the social setting.

### Practice

Use the specified person and relationship.

1. We will come tomorrow.
2. You will come. (honorific)
3. She will go. (ordinary reference)
4. She will go. (honorific reference)

***Answers:***

1. `zɯŋ gabɯn phɯigɯn`
2. `nɯŋthaŋ phɯigɯn`
3. `bi thaŋgɯn`
4. `bithaŋ thaŋgɯn`

### Why these answers work

The pronoun changes the participant or relationship while the future action remains stable. The first explicitly includes the speaker in a group. The last two differ in respect, not in gender or tense.

### Retrieve and recombine

State tomorrow's travel plan first for yourself, then for a group including you, and finally for a respected visitor. Keep the action constant and identify exactly what changes in the subject phrase.

## Lesson 3: Ask whether a plan will happen

### Outcome and yes-or-no questions

You will turn a future statement into a question using `-na`. `nɯŋ phɯigɯnna?` means “Will you come?” The action stem comes first, followed by the future suffix, followed by the question suffix. This makes the order of grammatical information visible inside one word.

### Think before reading on

In English, “You will come” becomes “Will you come?” through a change in word order. Must Bodo move the future material to the beginning? No. It can keep the normal sentence order and add a question marker after the verb's future suffix. Build the question from the statement rather than rearranging its words into English order.

### Question building blocks

| Bodo | Meaning | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| -na | yes-or-no question | follows the finite predicate here |
| -da | another question form | recognised, not the practice default |
| phɯi | come | action stem |
| -gɯn | future | precedes the question marker |
| nɯŋthaŋ | you, honorific | respectful addressee |
| gabɯn | tomorrow | time expression |
| thaŋ | go | alternative action |
| zɯŋ | we | group including the speaker |

### Separate the question from its possible answer

`nɯŋthaŋ gabɯn phɯigɯnna?` asks respectfully whether the listener will come tomorrow. A positive full reply is `aŋ gabɯn phɯigɯn`, “I will come tomorrow.” The reply changes from addressee to speaker perspective and drops the question suffix. Those are two separate changes, even though the action and day stay the same.

`bi thaŋgɯnna?` asks “Will he or she go?” You are asking about a third person rather than inviting the addressee to travel. `zɯŋ thaŋgɯnna?` asks “Will we go?” Depending on context, that may seek confirmation of a group plan. It is not identical to the explicit “Let's go” construction introduced later.

Questioning intonation can also signal a question, and the grammar describes alternative particles. The course selects one visible form so you can inspect the structure. That teaching choice does not make naturally occurring alternatives incorrect.

### Common trap

Do not insert the question marker between the stem and future suffix. The selected order is come, future, question. Also do not treat a question as a promise: it asks whether the event will occur rather than asserting that it will.

### Practice

Ask about the future event using -na.

1. Will you come? (ordinary address)
2. Will you come tomorrow? (honorific)
3. Will she go?
4. Will we go?

***Answers:***

1. `nɯŋ phɯigɯnna?`
2. `nɯŋthaŋ gabɯn phɯigɯnna?`
3. `bi thaŋgɯnna?`
4. `zɯŋ thaŋgɯnna?`

### Why these answers work

The question suffix follows an already future-marked verb. Pronouns determine whether the question addresses a person, refers to someone else, or concerns the speaker's group. The honorific remains part of the subject phrase rather than becoming a special question ending.

### Retrieve and recombine

Ask a respected visitor whether they will go today. Then answer as that visitor with a complete first-person statement. Check both the pronoun change and removal of the question marker.

## Lesson 4: Identify and describe without a compulsory copula

### Outcome and an equational sentence

You will identify a role and describe a person. Bodo can form an affirmative identity or descriptive sentence without an overt linking verb. It also has the copula `nɯŋgɯo`. Thus a statement can include the linking word for a visible beginner pattern without implying that every natural affirmative sentence must contain it.

### Think before reading on

If English requires “is” in “She is beautiful,” must Bodo insert a separate verb in exactly that place? No. The language can predicate a quality directly. In the explicit model `bi sɔmaina nɯŋgɯo`, “He or she is beautiful,” the linking verb remains final, not between the subject and quality.

### Identity and quality words

| Bodo | Meaning | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| phɯrɯŋgiri | teacher | identity noun |
| mansi | person | ordinary human noun |
| sɔmaina | beautiful | descriptive quality |
| mɯzaŋ | good | descriptive quality |
| zɯbɯr | very | degree word |
| nɯŋgɯo | be | optional affirmative copula |
| aŋ | I | speaker |
| bi | he/she | third person |

### Build a statement with a visible linking word

`aŋ phɯrɯŋgiri nɯŋgɯo` means “I am a teacher.” Use it only if that role is true or you are explicitly acting a role in practice. `bi zɯbɯr sɔmaina nɯŋgɯo` means “He or she is very beautiful.” The degree word modifies the quality; it does not change the identity of the subject.

The shorter `bi sɔmaina` can also serve as an affirmative description in context. This is not a fragment accidentally missing an English verb. A language can have a complete predicate without an overt copula. The explicit forms in the keys make the relation easy to inspect while you are learning.

Do not confuse this identity/descriptive copula with `dɔŋ`, the existence verb introduced later. Saying what a person is and saying that an item is available are related English “be” meanings but different useful Bodo constructions. The distinction helps prevent a single memorised “is” word from being forced into every sentence.

### Common trap

Do not add Hindi gender endings to `mɯzaŋ` or `sɔmaina` in these descriptions. Bodo does not divide ordinary inanimate nouns into a Hindi-style masculine/feminine system. Some lexical gender distinctions exist, but they do not justify importing an entire agreement system.

### Practice

Use the explicit copula in each answer.

1. I am a teacher.
2. She is beautiful.
3. She is very beautiful.
4. You are a teacher. (honorific)

***Answers:***

1. `aŋ phɯrɯŋgiri nɯŋgɯo`
2. `bi sɔmaina nɯŋgɯo`
3. `bi zɯbɯr sɔmaina nɯŋgɯo`
4. `nɯŋthaŋ phɯrɯŋgiri nɯŋgɯo`

### Why these answers work

The subject and its identity or quality precede the optional linking verb. The third answer adds degree information before the adjective. The final answer changes respectful address without changing the copula for person agreement.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe a person with and without the explicit affirmative copula. Then identify a respected person as a teacher. Explain why omitting the linking word can be grammatical in an affirmative Bodo sentence.

## Lesson 5: Negate an identity or description

### Outcome and a negative copula

You will say that an identity or quality does not apply. The negative of the copula in the selected pattern is `nɯŋa`, built from `nɯŋ` and the negative suffix `-a`. An affirmative description can omit its copula, but a negative description needs the negative-bearing form. Negation makes previously optional material grammatically useful.

### Think before reading on

If an affirmative sentence has no overt verb, where could a verbal negative suffix attach? It cannot float freely after an English-style “not.” Bodo supplies the negative copular form: `aŋ phɯrɯŋgiri nɯŋa`, “I am not a teacher.” The negative is part of the final predicate.

### Contrastive toolkit

| Bodo | Meaning | Contrast |
| --- | --- | --- |
| nɯŋgɯo | be | explicit affirmative |
| nɯŋa | not be | negative copula |
| phɯrɯŋgiri | teacher | possible role |
| mɯzaŋ | good | quality |
| sɔmaina | beautiful | quality |
| zɯbɯr | very | degree |
| aŋ | I | speaker |
| bithaŋ | he/she, honorific | respectful reference |

### Keep the scope of the denial clear

`bi sɔmaina nɯŋa` means “He or she is not beautiful.” `bi zɯbɯr sɔmaina nɯŋa` means “He or she is not very beautiful.” The second denies a degree, not necessarily every degree of the quality. Sentence meaning depends on the words inside the scope of negation, not just on identifying a negative suffix.

`nɯŋthaŋ phɯrɯŋgiri nɯŋa` says “You are not a teacher,” respectfully. The sentence may correct a mistaken role identification. Respectful reference remains in the pronoun, even when the proposition is negative. A negative statement does not license dropping the social relationship encoded in the subject.

For practical correction, two short clauses are often enough. First reject a wrong identity, then state the correct one using a known noun. You do not need to learn a long contrastive sentence before you can repair a misunderstanding. The key is choosing a role or description that is actually true of the person.

### Common trap

Do not form the negative by appending `-a` blindly to the entire visible affirmative `nɯŋgɯo`. The attested negative uses the stem `nɯŋ`. Nor should you omit the copula and leave a free negative suffix after an adjective. The affirmative and negative constructions differ in more than English “not.”

### Practice

Negate the given identity or description.

1. I am not a teacher.
2. She is not beautiful.
3. She is not very beautiful.
4. You are not a teacher. (honorific)

***Answers:***

1. `aŋ phɯrɯŋgiri nɯŋa`
2. `bi sɔmaina nɯŋa`
3. `bi zɯbɯr sɔmaina nɯŋa`
4. `nɯŋthaŋ phɯrɯŋgiri nɯŋa`

### Why these answers work

Each uses the negative copular stem rather than an invented negative of the whole affirmative word. The subject remains before the predicate. The degree word in the third answer changes what is denied, while the honorific in the fourth changes the relationship to the addressee.

### Retrieve and recombine

Produce an affirmative and negative role statement about yourself. Then contrast “beautiful” with “not very beautiful” in a fictional description. Explain why the negative needs an overt copular form even when an affirmative can omit it.

## Lesson 6: Build a possessive noun phrase

### Outcome and belonging

You will say “my book” and “her friend.” Bodo attaches `-ni` to the possessor in an ordinary possessive noun phrase. `aŋni bizab` means “my book.” The owner comes first, followed by the owned or associated item. English changes “I” into “my”; Bodo makes the relationship visible through the suffix.

### Think before reading on

If `bi` means “he or she,” which piece should receive the possession marker in “her book”? The owner receives it: `bini bizab`. Putting `-ni` on the book instead would establish a different relationship. Identify the possessor before attaching the suffix.

### Possessive phrase kit

| Bodo | Meaning | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| -ni | possessive/genitive | follows the possessor |
| bizab | book | possessed item |
| lɯgɯ | friend | associated person |
| nɔ | home; house | associated place |
| aŋni | my | speaker as possessor |
| bini | his/her | third-person possessor |
| nɯŋthaŋni | your, honorific | respectful possessor |
| zɯŋni | our | group as possessor |

### A phrase is not yet an availability statement

`zɯŋni nɔ` means “our house.” `nɯŋthaŋni bizab` means “your book,” respectfully. `bini lɯgɯ` means “his or her friend.” The same grammatical relationship can connect a person with a building, object, or social associate. The phrase does not have to mean legal ownership; genitive relations are broader than that.

An identity sentence can contain a possessive phrase: `be aŋni bizab nɯŋgɯo`, “This is my book.” The demonstrative `be` identifies a nearby thing, the possessive phrase identifies whose book it is, and the optional copula links the two. You have combined a new noun phrase with Lesson 4 rather than learned an unrelated sentence.

Saying “my book” does not tell someone whether the book is currently with you. Bodo has a different possession construction with `-ha` and an existence verb. That distinction is important enough to receive its own lesson. Do not make `-ni` and `-ha` interchangeable merely because English can translate both through ideas of possession.

### Common trap

Do not choose a different possessive ending according to whether the owner is male or female. `bi` can refer to either. Also do not turn a possessive phrase into a complete “I have” sentence by adding an arbitrary English-style verb. Its internal relation and its role in the larger clause are separate questions.

### Practice

Produce the phrase or identification requested.

1. My book.
2. Your book. (honorific)
3. Our house.
4. This is my book. (use the explicit copula)

***Answers:***

1. `aŋni bizab`
2. `nɯŋthaŋni bizab`
3. `zɯŋni nɔ`
4. `be aŋni bizab nɯŋgɯo`

### Why these answers work

The suffix belongs to the possessor. The first three are deliberately noun phrases, while the last embeds one in an identity sentence. The honorific is retained inside the possessor expression; it does not create a new agreement ending on “book.”

### Retrieve and recombine

Build “her book,” “our friend,” and “your house” with respectful address for the last. Then identify a nearby book as belonging to the listener. Distinguish the owner from the item every time.

## Lesson 7: Say that you have something

### Outcome and possessive existence

You will say that you have a book or a friend. In the useful Bodo construction `aŋha bizab dɔŋ`, the meaning is “I have a book.” The possessor takes `-ha`, and the clause ends with the existence verb `dɔŋ`. This differs from the noun phrase `aŋni bizab`, “my book.” The two suffixes do not perform the same job.

### Think before reading on

If “my book” is a phrase identifying an owner, what additional claim does “I have a book” make? It asserts a possession or availability relation. Use `aŋha`, not merely `aŋni`, in the selected existence frame. The ending of the possessor tells your listener how the clause is organised.

### Existence vocabulary

| Bodo | Meaning | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| -ha | possessive marker in this clause | marks the possessor |
| dɔŋ | exists; is present | final predicate |
| aŋha | I have; with me | possessor phrase |
| biha | he/she has | third-person possessor |
| bizab | book | available item |
| lɯgɯ | friend | associated person |
| raŋ | money; rupee | available resource |
| be | this | demonstrative |

### Change the possessor or item

`biha lɯgɯ dɔŋ` means “He or she has a friend.” `aŋha raŋ dɔŋ` means “I have money.” The final existence verb is the same. Changing the possessor does not add a person ending to `dɔŋ`; the possessor phrase itself carries the relevant reference.

`nɯŋthaŋha bizab dɔŋ` means “You have a book,” respectfully. The honorific pronoun is preserved before the possessive suffix. This is a useful sentence to recognise when checking whether someone has an item, although the questions practised earlier were specifically future questions. Learn a new question construction from its actual pattern instead of mechanically assuming every predicate takes every suffix identically.

An English “have” sentence can sometimes concern ownership, sometimes an available item, and sometimes a relationship. This Bodo frame covers the selected possession examples. It should not be transferred automatically to English expressions such as “have lunch,” which describe consuming food rather than owning lunch.

### Common trap

Do not alternate `-ni` and `-ha` for stylistic variety. The first builds a possessive noun phrase; the second participates in the possessive existence statement taught here. A borrowed book illustrates why ownership and having available may differ in practical conversation, even when the grammar of both relates a possessor and an item.

### Practice

Use the possessive existence construction.

1. I have a book.
2. I have money.
3. She has a friend.
4. You have a book. (honorific)

***Answers:***

1. `aŋha bizab dɔŋ`
2. `aŋha raŋ dɔŋ`
3. `biha lɯgɯ dɔŋ`
4. `nɯŋthaŋha bizab dɔŋ`

### Why these answers work

Each possessor takes `-ha`, while the item or associated person comes before `dɔŋ`. The third-person pronoun does not specify gender. The final answer preserves respectful address without introducing person agreement on the existence verb.

### Retrieve and recombine

Say “my book,” then “I have a book,” and explain the suffix difference. Next, change the statement to a respected listener who has money. Keep the existence verb and change only the relevant noun phrases.

## Lesson 8: Say what is absent

### Outcome and negative existence

You will say that you do not have an item. Bodo's ordinary negative existence form in these examples is `gɯija`. It is not created by adding a negative suffix directly to `dɔŋ`. `aŋha bizab gɯija` means “I do not have a book.” The possessor frame remains, but the existence predicate changes.

### Think before reading on

Does every language form the negative of “exists” from exactly the same visible stem as the positive? English itself uses several different constructions around “be” and “have.” Bodo has a distinct negative existence stem in the selected pattern. Learn the positive and negative as a meaningful contrast, not as a rule that all stems behave identically.

### Presence and absence

| Bodo | Meaning | Contrast |
| --- | --- | --- |
| dɔŋ | is present; exists | affirmative existence |
| gɯija | is absent; does not exist | negative existence |
| bizab | book | countable item |
| raŋ | money | resource |
| lɯgɯ | friend | social associate |
| aŋha | with me; I have | possessor |
| biha | with him/her | possessor |
| zɯŋha | with us | group possessor |

### Deny availability, not identity

`aŋha raŋ gɯija` means “I do not have money.” `biha bizab gɯija` means “He or she does not have a book.” These sentences do not deny that books or money exist anywhere. They deny the relevant availability relation for the specified person. Context gives the statement its practical scope.

Contrast `aŋ phɯrɯŋgiri nɯŋa`, “I am not a teacher,” with `aŋha bizab gɯija`, “I do not have a book.” One rejects an identity; the other rejects possessive existence. English may use a negative in both, but the Bodo predicate families differ.

`zɯŋha bizab dɔŋ` and `zɯŋha raŋ gɯija` can form a useful two-sentence account: “We have a book. We do not have money.” The group possessor remains constant while the available resource and polarity change. This is a small but genuine recombination task, rather than memorising one isolated refusal.

### Common trap

Do not produce an invented `dɔŋa` as the general negative existence verb. Also avoid using the negative copula `nɯŋa` in place of `gɯija` in this possession construction. Similar English translations are not proof that the Bodo words are interchangeable.

### Practice

Deny the stated availability.

1. I do not have a book.
2. I do not have money.
3. She does not have a book.
4. We do not have money.

***Answers:***

1. `aŋha bizab gɯija`
2. `aŋha raŋ gɯija`
3. `biha bizab gɯija`
4. `zɯŋha raŋ gɯija`

### Why these answers work

The possession phrase remains marked with `-ha`, and the negative existence verb replaces the affirmative predicate. Number is conveyed through `zɯŋ` in the final answer rather than through a new plural form of the verb.

### Retrieve and recombine

State one available item and one absent item for yourself. Then distinguish a wrong identity from a missing resource by producing one sentence with `nɯŋa` and one with `gɯija`.

## Lesson 9: Locate a person or object

### Outcome and a place marker

You will say that someone is at home or that something is at school. Bodo uses locative suffixes whose shapes depend partly on the end of the noun. The useful complete forms here are `nɔwao`, “at home,” and `phɔraisalijao`, “at school.” They end in related location markers but do not attach one identical visible string after every noun.

### Think before reading on

If English puts “at” before “home,” where will the corresponding relation appear in a head-final Bodo phrase? It follows the place. `aŋ nɔwao dɔŋ` means “I am at home.” The place phrase precedes the final existence verb. The sentence locates you rather than identifies your occupation.

### Place vocabulary

| Bodo | Meaning | Shape to notice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| nɔ | home; house | back-vowel noun |
| nɔwao | at home | locative form |
| phɔraisali | school | ends in a front vowel |
| phɔraisalijao | at school | locative form |
| ukhum | roof | consonant-final noun |
| ukhumao | on the roof | locative form |
| dɔŋ | exists; is present | location predicate |
| gɯija | is absent | negative location predicate |

### Keep location and ownership separate

`bi phɔraisalijao dɔŋ` means “He or she is at school.” `bizab nɔwao dɔŋ` means “The book is at home.” The same location phrase can locate a person or an object. In the second sentence, the location does not tell us who owns the book. Ownership would require a possessive phrase such as `aŋni bizab`.

`aŋ nɔwao gɯija` means “I am not at home.” This uses negative existence rather than the negative identity copula. You are not denying that you are yourself; you are denying your presence at a place. The predicate choice follows the intended relation.

The source also describes special location marking involving animate reference points and genitive-plus-locative combinations. Those are not obtained by putting the ordinary inanimate place suffix on every personal pronoun. This lesson keeps the examples to clear place nouns so you can use the location pattern before learning its larger case system.

### Common trap

Do not put a Hindi postposition after the Bodo place and call it Bodo grammar. Also do not assume the visible w or j before the location ending is optional decoration. Learn the supplied locative word forms as actual combinations, then compare their sound environments.

### Practice

Locate the person or book.

1. I am at home.
2. She is at school.
3. The book is at home.
4. I am not at home.

***Answers:***

1. `aŋ nɔwao dɔŋ`
2. `bi phɔraisalijao dɔŋ`
3. `bizab nɔwao dɔŋ`
4. `aŋ nɔwao gɯija`

### Why these answers work

The locative suffix follows the place noun, and the place phrase precedes the existence predicate. The final sentence changes polarity without changing the place. None is an identity statement, so the copular forms from Lessons 4 and 5 are not the selected predicates.

### Retrieve and recombine

Locate yourself at school and a book at home. Then deny your presence at home. Finally, add a possessor to the book so the listener knows both whose book it is and where it is.

## Lesson 10: Trace a route from a starting point

### Outcome and source of movement

You will say where a journey begins and where it ends. Bodo uses `-niphrai` for a source, as in `nɔniphrai`, “from home.” A useful endpoint marker is `-sim`, as in `nɔsim`, “to home.” These relational suffixes follow the place, allowing a route to be built before the final motion verb.

### Think before reading on

If a sentence contains both a market and a house, which one is the starting point? The marker tells you. `hathainiphrai` means “from the market,” while `nɔsim` supplies the homeward endpoint. Do not decide the route solely from the order in an English translation.

### Route vocabulary

| Bodo | Meaning | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| hathai | market | place noun |
| -niphrai | from | source suffix |
| -sim | to; up to | endpoint suffix |
| nɔsim | to home | destination phrase |
| phɯi | come | motion toward a reference point |
| thaŋ | go | motion away or onward |
| gabɯn | tomorrow | future time |
| dinɯi | today | day expression |

### Build the route before its action

`aŋ hathainiphrai nɔsim phɯigɯn` means “I will come home from the market.” `aŋ nɔniphrai hathaisim thaŋgɯn` means “I will go from home to the market.” The same two places reverse roles through their markers, while the verb reflects the chosen motion viewpoint.

`aŋ nɔniphrai phɯigɯn` means “I will come from home.” The destination can remain understood from the conversation. Conversely, `aŋ nɔsim thaŋgɯn` means “I will go home.” You do not have to state both endpoints in every motion sentence if one is already known.

The endpoint marker can also delimit time in other constructions. That wider relation helps explain why it is not simply an English preposition copied onto a noun. For now, the route examples give you a practical starting point: identify source, identify destination, then choose come or go.

### Common trap

Do not confuse being `nɔwao`, at home, with moving `nɔsim`, to home. Location and endpoint are different relations. Also avoid assuming “come” and “go” are interchangeable: they present the movement from a conversational viewpoint, which should remain coherent in a dialogue.

### Practice

Build the indicated future route.

1. I will come from home.
2. I will go home.
3. I will come home from the market.
4. I will go from home to the market.

***Answers:***

1. `aŋ nɔniphrai phɯigɯn`
2. `aŋ nɔsim thaŋgɯn`
3. `aŋ hathainiphrai nɔsim phɯigɯn`
4. `aŋ nɔniphrai hathaisim thaŋgɯn`

### Why these answers work

Source and endpoint follow their own place nouns. The longer answers place both marked phrases before the motion verb. The future suffix remains unchanged because adding a route does not change the verb's temporal commitment.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe a tomorrow journey from school to home. Then describe where you are today using a location sentence. Explain why the place suffix and the final predicate differ between being somewhere and traveling there.

## Lesson 11: Add an object before the action

### Outcome and object marking

You will say what you will see, eat, or drink. A neutral Bodo action sentence commonly places the object before the final verb. `aŋ dɯi lɯŋgɯn` means “I will drink water.” The action is not moved before the object to match English. The future suffix remains attached to the action stem.

### Think before reading on

When you say “I will see the friend,” which participant is the viewer and which is the person seen? Bodo can mark the direct object with `-khɯo`: `aŋ lɯgɯkhɯo nugɯn`. The marker makes the friend's role explicit. You do not add it to the speaker merely because the sentence includes two people.

### Objects and actions

| Bodo | Meaning | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| dɯi | water | ordinary consumed substance |
| ɯŋkham | cooked rice | ordinary food object |
| bizab | book | object noun |
| lɯgɯ | friend | human object in an example |
| nu | see | action stem |
| za | eat | action stem |
| lɯŋ | drink | action stem |
| -khɯo | direct-object marker | follows the object |

### Distinguish a role from a position

`aŋ bizabkhɯo nugɯn` means “I will see the book.” `aŋ ɯŋkham zagɯn` means “I will eat rice.” The definite object in the seeing example is explicitly marked, while the ordinary food object is bare in the selected eating example. This introduces a useful contrast without claiming that every Bodo object must have exactly the same marking.

`bi aŋkhɯo nugɯn` means “He or she will see me.” The suffix now attaches to the speaker pronoun because the speaker is the person seen. The viewer is third person. If you only memorise the English subject-object order, you may miss how the marker identifies the affected participant.

The object marker can follow other noun morphology, such as a plural suffix, which a later lesson introduces. This is part of Bodo's suffix-rich structure: a word can carry several distinct pieces of grammatical information in an order. Each piece should be understood before you combine several at once.

### Common trap

Do not attach `-khɯo` to every noun in the sentence. A location, possessor, recipient, and direct object have different roles. Also do not replace it with Hindi `को` merely because both can occur with people. The Bodo marker belongs to the Bodo construction being taught.

### Practice

Build the action with the correct object.

1. I will drink water.
2. I will eat rice.
3. I will see the friend.
4. She will see me.

***Answers:***

1. `aŋ dɯi lɯŋgɯn`
2. `aŋ ɯŋkham zagɯn`
3. `aŋ lɯgɯkhɯo nugɯn`
4. `bi aŋkhɯo nugɯn`

### Why these answers work

Objects precede the final action. The first two use common bare consumed objects. The last two explicitly mark the person seen. In the final sentence, the speaker is the object rather than the subject, so the marker attaches to `aŋ`.

### Retrieve and recombine

State that you will see a book, then that a friend will see you. Finally, plan a meal with rice and water in two short sentences. Track the viewer or consumer separately from the object.

## Lesson 12: Mark the person who receives something

### Outcome and a recipient

You will say that you will give money or a book to someone. Bodo uses `-nɯ` for a recipient in a giving construction. `aŋ binɯ bizab hɯgɯn` means “I will give a book to him or her.” The recipient and the thing given are distinct participants. English sometimes expresses the recipient with “to” and sometimes with word order; Bodo can make the role visible with a suffix.

### Think before reading on

In “I give her money,” is “her” the object transferred, or the person receiving it? The money is transferred; the person receives it. Mark the receiver with `-nɯ`. You should not substitute the direct-object marker merely because both participants can follow an English verb.

### Giving vocabulary

| Bodo | Meaning | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| hɯ | give | action; lexical tone matters |
| -nɯ | to; for a recipient | dative marker here |
| binɯ | to him/her | recipient phrase |
| aŋnɯ | to me | recipient phrase |
| zɯŋnɯ | to us | recipient phrase |
| bizab | book | thing given |
| raŋ | money | thing given |
| nɯŋthaŋ | you, honorific | possible giver or recipient |

### Keep all three participants clear

`aŋ binɯ raŋ hɯgɯn` means “I will give him or her money.” `bi aŋnɯ bizab hɯgɯn` means “He or she will give me a book.” The first sentence has the speaker as giver; the second has the speaker as receiver. The verb remains future in both.

`nɯŋthaŋ aŋnɯ bizab hɯgɯn` means “You will give me a book,” respectfully. Respect is expressed in the giver's pronoun. `aŋ nɯŋthaŋnɯ bizab hɯgɯn` instead says “I will give you a book,” with a respected recipient. Read the suffix boundary carefully so the longer pronoun does not obscure the receiver's role.

Bodo's `-nɯ` also appears in other grammatical environments, including nonfinite verb constructions introduced later. A suffix with the same sound can have different functions depending on what it attaches to. Here it follows a noun or pronoun and marks a recipient, so there is no need to import its later verbal function prematurely.

### Common trap

The giving verb and the negative or future suffixes should not be confused with a Hindi light-verb construction. Build the Bodo action from the verified stem and suffix. In speech, the tone distinction in the giving root matters; the running untoned notation is a morphology aid, not a claim that tone disappears.

### Practice

Identify the receiver before building the sentence.

1. I will give her a book.
2. I will give her money.
3. She will give me a book.
4. I will give you a book. (honorific recipient)

***Answers:***

1. `aŋ binɯ bizab hɯgɯn`
2. `aŋ binɯ raŋ hɯgɯn`
3. `bi aŋnɯ bizab hɯgɯn`
4. `aŋ nɯŋthaŋnɯ bizab hɯgɯn`

### Why these answers work

The dative suffix identifies the receiver, while the item remains the thing transferred. The third answer changes the speaker's role from giver to receiver. The final answer preserves honorific reference inside the recipient phrase.

### Retrieve and recombine

Give money to a friend in one sentence, then describe receiving a book from a third person. Point out which noun phrase is marked as recipient and which word names the transferred item.

## Lesson 13: State a routine with habitual aspect

### Outcome and regular activity

You will distinguish a routine from a future plan. Bodo uses a habitual suffix whose visible form depends on the action stem. After a consonant, the selected form is `-ɯ`; after most vowel-final stems it is `-jɯ`. Thus `lɯŋɯ` means “drinks” as a habitual action, while `zajɯ` means “eats.” These are not person endings.

### Think before reading on

If you change “I will drink water” into “I drink water regularly,” does the future suffix still express what you mean? Replace future marking with the habitual form: `aŋ dɯi lɯŋɯ`. The subject and object remain; the viewpoint toward the action changes.

### Routine vocabulary

| Bodo | Meaning | Stem pattern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| lɯŋɯ | drinks habitually | consonant-final stem |
| zajɯ | eats habitually | vowel-final stem |
| thajɯ | stays; lives | vowel-final stem |
| nujɯ | sees | vowel-final stem |
| gabɯ | weeps | consonant-final stem |
| sanphrɯmbɯ | every day | habitual time expression |
| saha | tea | drink noun |
| simaŋ | dream | used with seeing |

### Show a habit in context

`aŋ saha lɯŋɯ` means “I drink tea.” `aŋ ɯŋkham zajɯ` means “I eat rice.” `aŋ nɔwao thajɯ` means “I stay at home,” or “I live at home” in an appropriate context. The staying verb describes a regular situation rather than merely locating you at this instant.

`aŋ sanphrɯmbɯ simaŋ nujɯ` means “I dream every day.” Literally the expression involves seeing dreams. This gives a language-specific lexical combination rather than assuming every English verb must map to one Bodo root. The time phrase makes the repeated interpretation explicit.

The habitual construction can express a general situation or recurring action. It should not be equated mechanically with every English simple present, which also has other uses. A separate progressive construction will describe an action unfolding now, and a past suffix can later combine with habitual aspect to describe a former routine.

### Common trap

Do not attach `-jɯ` to every stem regardless of its final sound, and do not interpret the habitual ending as “third-person singular.” `aŋ`, `bi`, and `zɯŋ` can all occur with the same habitual verb form. The suffix concerns aspect rather than English-style person agreement.

### Practice

Describe a routine rather than a future event.

1. I drink water.
2. I drink tea.
3. I eat rice.
4. I stay at home.

***Answers:***

1. `aŋ dɯi lɯŋɯ`
2. `aŋ saha lɯŋɯ`
3. `aŋ ɯŋkham zajɯ`
4. `aŋ nɔwao thajɯ`

### Why these answers work

The habitual suffix replaces the future construction. Consonant-final drinking takes `-ɯ`, while eating and staying use `-jɯ`. The object or location remains before the action. None changes according to the speaker's gender.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe your own tea or water routine, then describe the same routine for a group including you. Finally, turn one routine into a tomorrow plan and explain why the verb's ending changes.

## Lesson 14: Negate an action

### Outcome and negative stems

You will say that you do not go and that you will not come tomorrow. Bodo commonly forms an ordinary action negative with a suffix whose main form is `-a`. The visible form varies after vowels. `thaŋa` means “does not go,” while `phɯija` means “does not come.” These negative verbs do not simply retain the positive habitual or future suffixes.

### Think before reading on

If “I will come tomorrow” is `aŋ gabɯn phɯigɯn`, can you negate it by leaving the future suffix untouched and adding an English-like “not”? The selected negative is `aŋ gabɯn phɯija`. The time phrase establishes the future reference, while the negative form follows its own morphology.

### Action-negative contrasts

| Bodo | Meaning | Notice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| thaŋ | go | consonant-final stem |
| thaŋa | does not go | negative suffix |
| phɯi | come | vowel-final stem |
| phɯija | does not come | negative form |
| lɯŋ | drink | consonant-final stem |
| lɯŋa | does not drink | negative form |
| gabɯn | tomorrow | can time a negative event |
| saha | tea | object noun |

### Deny a routine or a plan

`aŋ saha lɯŋa` means “I do not drink tea.” `aŋ thaŋa` means “I do not go,” with the destination or situation understood. `aŋ gabɯn thaŋa` means “I will not go tomorrow.” These examples show why positive and negative tense-aspect patterns should not be assumed to be mechanically identical.

`bi gabɯn phɯija` means “He or she will not come tomorrow.” Changing the subject does not require a new person ending. The negative remains tied to the action stem. A respected addressee can likewise appear with the same negative action form while keeping the honorific pronoun.

The source distinguishes ordinary negative statements from prohibitions. “You do not go” reports a situation; “Do not go” directs someone not to act. Bodo uses a negative prefix in the prohibition pattern introduced later. Learn that distinction now at the level of meaning, even before producing the prefix.

### Common trap

Do not extend the affirmative future suffix into an unattested negative form merely because English uses “will not.” The negative construction can rely on a time expression for future reference. Also avoid treating every negative as a command; an ordinary statement about absence of an action does not automatically tell the listener what to do.

### Practice

Use an ordinary negative statement.

1. I do not drink tea.
2. I do not go.
3. I will not come tomorrow.
4. She will not go tomorrow.

***Answers:***

1. `aŋ saha lɯŋa`
2. `aŋ thaŋa`
3. `aŋ gabɯn phɯija`
4. `bi gabɯn thaŋa`

### Why these answers work

The negative attaches to the lexical action rather than to an already future-marked form. The last two use tomorrow to establish future time. The consonant-final and vowel-final stems show different visible negative combinations.

### Retrieve and recombine

State one drink you consume and one you do not. Then make a positive and negative tomorrow travel plan. Keep the affirmative future morphology separate from the negative construction.

## Lesson 15: Describe an action in progress

### Outcome and ongoing activity

You will describe sleeping, eating, or drinking as an activity currently unfolding. A clear Bodo progressive combines `-gasinɯ` on the action with the auxiliary `dɔŋ`. `aŋ undugasinɯ dɔŋ` means “I am sleeping.” The auxiliary follows the progressive action, so the entire verb group remains at the end.

### Think before reading on

Does a statement that you drink tea habitually prove you are drinking it now? No. To present the current activity, use `aŋ saha lɯŋgasinɯ dɔŋ`, “I am drinking tea.” The object stays in its familiar position while the action receives progressive marking and an auxiliary.

### Progressive toolkit

| Bodo | Meaning | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| undu | sleep | action stem |
| za | eat | action stem |
| lɯŋ | drink | action stem |
| -gasinɯ | progressive | attaches to the action |
| dɔŋ | auxiliary here | completes the progressive |
| ɯŋkham | rice | object |
| saha | tea | object |
| dɯi | water | object |

### Build and compare

`bi undugasinɯ dɔŋ` means “He or she is sleeping.” `aŋ ɯŋkham zagasinɯ dɔŋ` means “I am eating rice.” `zɯŋ dɯi lɯŋgasinɯ dɔŋ` means “We are drinking water.” The progressive suffix and auxiliary stay the same across these subjects. The pronouns identify the participants.

Compare `aŋ saha lɯŋɯ`, “I drink tea,” with the explicit progressive above. The first describes a routine or general habit, while the second presents the activity as unfolding. English uses different verb groups for this contrast; Bodo uses its own aspect suffix and auxiliary combination.

The grammar also describes a progressive use of `-bai` when followed by an auxiliary. That suffix has another important perfect use in other environments. We postpone it until the next lesson so that one familiar-looking suffix does not become a vague label for every English present or past meaning.

### Common trap

Do not omit `dɔŋ` from this selected full progressive construction, or confuse it with the different vowel in the aspect suffix `-dɯŋ`. The two forms look similar in transcription, but ɔ and ɯ represent distinct vowels. Accurate reading depends on preserving that difference.

### Practice

Describe the activity as ongoing.

1. I am sleeping.
2. She is sleeping.
3. I am eating rice.
4. We are drinking water.

***Answers:***

1. `aŋ undugasinɯ dɔŋ`
2. `bi undugasinɯ dɔŋ`
3. `aŋ ɯŋkham zagasinɯ dɔŋ`
4. `zɯŋ dɯi lɯŋgasinɯ dɔŋ`

### Why these answers work

The progressive suffix follows the action, and the existence auxiliary follows the marked action. Objects remain before the verb group. A group subject does not create a new plural auxiliary form in these examples.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe a current tea-drinking scene, then give a habitual statement about tea. Next, change the current scene from yourself to a respected person. Keep the aspect construction stable while changing the participant.

## Lesson 16: Present an action as already accomplished

### Outcome and perfect aspect

You will say that someone has eaten or has come. Bodo can use the suffix `-bai` to present an action as accomplished with relevance to the current situation. `aŋ ɯŋkham zabai` means “I have eaten rice.” This is useful when someone offers a meal and you want to report that the eating is already accomplished.

### Think before reading on

If “I am eating” describes an unfolding activity, does it mean the meal is finished? No. Compare the progressive `aŋ ɯŋkham zagasinɯ dɔŋ` with `aŋ ɯŋkham zabai`. The second presents accomplishment. Do not treat every suffix associated with an English past translation as a simple past-tense marker.

### Accomplishment forms

| Bodo | Meaning | Construction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| zabai | has eaten | eat plus perfect |
| phɯibai | has come | come plus perfect |
| thaŋbai | has gone | go plus perfect |
| lɯŋbai | has drunk | drink plus perfect |
| -bai | perfect here | not a universal past suffix |
| ɯŋkham | rice | food object |
| saha | tea | drink object |
| nɔsim | to home | destination phrase |

### Current relevance comes from context

`bi phɯibai` means “He or she has come.” It can answer a question about whether an expected visitor has arrived. `aŋ saha lɯŋbai` means “I have drunk tea.” `bi nɔsim thaŋbai` means “He or she has gone home.” The destination adds information to a completed departure, without changing the perfect suffix.

A perfect statement does not itself specify an exact clock time. If the conversation requires a dated past narrative, other past constructions are available. The next lesson makes the past relation explicit. For now, ask what the accomplished event tells the listener about the present situation: the visitor has arrived, the meal is eaten, or the person has gone home.

The source also describes a progressive `-bai` when an aspectual auxiliary follows it. That means the surrounding construction matters. This lesson's practice uses the perfect form without a following `dɔŋ`. It would be misleading to label every occurrence of `bai` “already” and ignore what follows.

### Common trap

Do not translate “has” as an independent Bodo possession verb before a past action. English perfect “has eaten” and English possessive “has a book” use similar words, but Bodo expresses them through different constructions. `biha bizab dɔŋ` is possession; `bi ɯŋkham zabai` is an accomplished action.

### Practice

Present the event as accomplished.

1. I have eaten rice.
2. She has come.
3. I have drunk tea.
4. She has gone home.

***Answers:***

1. `aŋ ɯŋkham zabai`
2. `bi phɯibai`
3. `aŋ saha lɯŋbai`
4. `bi nɔsim thaŋbai`

### Why these answers work

The suffix follows the lexical action and presents accomplishment. Objects and destinations still precede the final verb. The English auxiliary “have/has” is not translated through the Bodo possessive existence construction.

### Retrieve and recombine

Contrast having a book with having eaten rice. Then contrast eating now with having eaten. Explain which part of each Bodo construction carries the distinction that English expresses through different uses of “have” and “be.”

## Lesson 17: Place an event or routine in the past

### Outcome and past marking

You will describe a completed past visit and a former routine. Bodo uses `-mɯn` for past time in the selected constructions, normally after aspect marking on an affirmative main verb. `aŋ phɯidɯŋmɯn` means “I came.” The sequence is action, aspect, past. It is not simply the bare action plus a person ending.

### Think before reading on

If “used to drink tea” describes a past routine, should you choose the same aspect as a single completed arrival? No. `aŋ saha lɯŋɯmɯn` means “I used to drink tea.” Habitual aspect remains inside the past construction. The past suffix locates that habitual situation before the present.

### Past forms and time words

| Bodo | Meaning | Structure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| phɯidɯŋmɯn | came | come, aspect, past |
| thaŋdɯŋmɯn | went | go, aspect, past |
| lɯŋɯmɯn | used to drink | drink, habitual, past |
| thajɯmɯn | used to stay/live | stay, habitual, past |
| -mɯn | past | follows relevant aspect material |
| mɯija | yesterday | past day |
| nɔwao | at home | location |
| saha | tea | object |

### Build a small past account

`aŋ mɯija phɯidɯŋmɯn` means “I came yesterday.” `bi nɔsim thaŋdɯŋmɯn` means “He or she went home.” The completed-event reading is useful in a narrative about a visit. `aŋ nɔwao thajɯmɯn` means “I used to stay at home,” presenting a former regular situation instead.

Compare the vowels carefully: the aspect suffix is `-dɯŋ`, while the independent existence auxiliary is `dɔŋ`. The past suffix is `-mɯn`. Their similarity makes a compact word easy to misread, so separate the morphemes mentally before saying the full form. The course's reading notation preserves these vowel contrasts intentionally.

The suffix `-mɯn` also appears in noun and pronoun formations with other meanings, including certain plural expressions. Its position and the kind of word it attaches to distinguish those uses. A sequence of sounds does not carry one invariant English translation across the whole grammar.

### Common trap

Do not strip out aspect information and call every `-mɯn` form the same English simple past. “Came” and “used to come” differ in how the event is viewed. Also avoid adding a Hindi or Nepali agent marker to the speaker just because the event is past.

### Practice

1. I came yesterday.
2. She went home.
3. I used to drink tea.
4. I used to stay at home.

***Answers:***

2. `bi nɔsim thaŋdɯŋmɯn`
3. `aŋ saha lɯŋɯmɯn`
4. `aŋ nɔwao thajɯmɯn`

### Why these answers work

The completed events use the selected aspect-plus-past sequence. The former routines preserve habitual morphology before past marking. The final verb remains independent of the subject's gender and person in these examples.

### Retrieve and recombine

Tell where you went yesterday, then describe an earlier tea-drinking habit. Next, replace the speaker with a respected person. Keep the past/aspect distinction rather than changing only the pronoun.

## Lesson 18: Request an action and suggest acting together

### Outcome and polite imperatives

You will ask someone to come and suggest that a group go together. A Bodo imperative can use the bare action stem, but `-dɯ` provides a polite request form in the selected examples. `phɯidɯ` means “Please come.” This is not the same as predicting that someone will come in the future.

### Think before reading on

Does “You will come” necessarily invite someone to come? It may only predict or state a plan. A request has a different purpose. Remove the future statement structure and use the imperative: `phɯidɯ`. The addressee is normally understood, so an explicit pronoun is not required in every request.

### Requests and shared action

| Bodo | Meaning | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| phɯidɯ | please come | polite imperative |
| thaŋdɯ | please go | polite imperative |
| zadɯ | please eat | polite imperative |
| lɯŋdɯ | please drink | polite imperative |
| -dɯ | polite imperative particle | attaches near the action stem |
| -ni | let us/let me, by context | cohortative or permissive |
| zɯŋ | we | group including speaker |
| thaŋdini | let us go | imperative allomorph plus cohortative |

### Include the speaker when appropriate

`zɯŋ thaŋdini` means “Let us go.” Here the imperative element appears as `-di` before `-ni`. Learn the combined form rather than leaving the vowel unchanged by guesswork. `aŋ zadini` means “Let me eat.” It requests permission for the speaker rather than inviting a group to eat.

`dɯi lɯŋdɯ` means “Please drink water.” `ɯŋkham zadɯ` means “Please eat rice.” Objects remain before the requested action. This makes a meal invitation possible using the same vocabulary that already appeared in routine, future, and progressive sentences.

Politeness is affected by the social situation and delivery as well as the particle. The course teaches a useful grammatical form, not a promise that any imperative will sound equally considerate in every context. Bare stems are not automatically wrong, but an unfamiliar learner benefits from recognising how the polite particle changes the request.

### Common trap

Do not attach the imperative particle after a complete future or habitual form. In the selected requests it follows the action stem directly. Also distinguish “let us” from “let me”: both involve `-ni`, but the participant phrase changes the intended action and permission relation.

### Practice

Make a request or shared suggestion.

1. Please come.
2. Please drink water.
3. Let us go.
4. Let me eat.

***Answers:***

1. `phɯidɯ`
2. `dɯi lɯŋdɯ`
3. `zɯŋ thaŋdini`
4. `aŋ zadini`

### Why these answers work

The first two are polite imperatives. The third includes the speaker's group, and the fourth requests permission for the speaker alone. The vowel in the imperative element changes before `-ni`, so the combined forms are learned as attested morphological combinations.

### Retrieve and recombine

Invite a visitor to come and drink tea. Then suggest that everyone go home. Finally, ask to eat yourself. Explain which requests involve the listener alone and which include the speaker.

## Lesson 19: Tell someone not to act

### Outcome and prohibition

You will distinguish a negative statement from a negative command. Bodo uses the prefix `da-` in the selected prohibitions. `dathaŋ` means “Do not go.” Compare ordinary negative `thaŋa`, “does not go.” The position of the negative material helps distinguish the sentence's purpose.

### Think before reading on

If you say “I do not go,” are you ordering someone else to stay? No. A statement reports a situation; a prohibition directs an addressee. `daphɯi` means “Do not come.” You cannot obtain that meaning just by translating an English “not” word without choosing the proper Bodo construction.

### Negative commands

| Bodo | Meaning | Contrast |
| --- | --- | --- |
| da- | prohibitive negative | before the action |
| dathaŋ | do not go | prohibition |
| daphɯi | do not come | prohibition |
| daza | do not eat | prohibition |
| dalɯŋ | do not drink | prohibition |
| thaŋa | does not go | ordinary statement |
| phɯija | does not come | ordinary statement |
| gabɯn | tomorrow | time for either type |

### Keep the addressee understood

`gabɯn daphɯi` means “Do not come tomorrow.” `be daza` means “Do not eat this.” `be dalɯŋ` means “Do not drink this.” These short commands rely on a clear referent for `be`, “this.” Pointing or an established topic can supply it; a bare demonstrative cannot identify an unknown item without context.

`aŋ gabɯn phɯija` means “I will not come tomorrow.” Comparing it with the prohibition shows two differences: the ordinary negative follows the action, and the statement names the speaker. The prohibition instead directs an understood addressee and uses the prefix.

A prohibition can be urgent or gentle depending on context. The written grammar does not determine the entire social force. Use an ordinary clear request context when practising, such as asking someone not to come on a day when you are absent, rather than assuming all commands carry identical emotional weight.

### Common trap

Do not combine both negative patterns by automatically adding a negative suffix after a `da-` prohibition. The selected form already expresses the intended negative command. Also avoid treating `da-` as the universal negative for identity, existence, and ordinary action statements; each family has been taught separately.

### Practice

Use a prohibition, not an ordinary negative statement.

1. Do not go.
2. Do not come tomorrow.
3. Do not eat this.
4. Do not drink this.

***Answers:***

1. `dathaŋ`
2. `gabɯn daphɯi`
3. `be daza`
4. `be dalɯŋ`

### Why these answers work

The negative prefix precedes the action stem. The time or object phrase remains before the final command. No future suffix or ordinary negative suffix is added to these selected prohibitions.

### Retrieve and recombine

Say “I will not come tomorrow,” then tell someone “Do not come tomorrow.” Next, contrast “Please drink water” with “Do not drink this.” Explain the grammatical and conversational difference in each pair.

## Lesson 20: Make a group explicit

### Outcome and plural forms

You will refer to books, friends, and groups of people. Bodo has several plural suffixes with different distributions. `bizabphɯr` means “books.” Ordinary personal-pronoun plurals include `bisɯr`, “they,” while honorific pronouns can take `-mɯn`, as in `bithaŋmɯn`. These suffixes are not freely interchangeable.

### Think before reading on

Would a language necessarily use the same plural marker for a book, a personal pronoun, and a respectful group? Bodo does not. Learn the category of expression before choosing the marker. This is a language-specific pattern that an English final “s” does not predict.

### Useful plural expressions

| Bodo | Meaning | Category |
| --- | --- | --- |
| bizabphɯr | books | ordinary noun plural |
| lɯgɯphɯr | friends | ordinary noun plural |
| bisɯr | they | ordinary human pronoun |
| nɯŋsɯr | you all | ordinary address |
| bithaŋmɯn | they, honorific | respectful group |
| nɯŋthaŋmɯn | you all, honorific | respectful addressees |
| bephɯr | these; they | nonhuman demonstrative/pronoun |
| zɯŋ | we | distinct first-person plural |

### Put the plural into a familiar clause

`bisɯr gabɯn phɯigɯn` means “They will come tomorrow.” `bithaŋmɯn gabɯn phɯigɯn` conveys the same future event with honorific reference. The verb does not take a new plural ending. The group and relationship are represented in the subject phrase.

`aŋha bizabphɯr dɔŋ` means “I have books.” This combines noun plurality with possessive existence. `aŋ lɯgɯphɯrkhɯo nugɯn` means “I will see the friends.” The plural suffix comes before the direct-object marker. That ordering makes the word's structure interpretable: friend, plural, object.

The suffix `-mɯn` also occurs with names and kinship expressions, where a group can mean a named person and associated people. It is not always a simple numerical multiplication of one identical individual. In a different environment, the same sound sequence marks past time on a verb. Position and word category distinguish the functions.

### Common trap

Do not attach `-phɯr` to every personal pronoun by analogy with books. Nor should you read honorific `-mɯn` on a pronoun as a past-tense instruction. A suffix's grammatical environment is part of its meaning.

### Practice

Use the appropriate plural expression.

1. Books. (a noun phrase)
2. They will come tomorrow. (ordinary reference)
3. They will come tomorrow. (honorific reference)
4. I will see the friends.

***Answers:***

1. `bizabphɯr`
2. `bisɯr gabɯn phɯigɯn`
3. `bithaŋmɯn gabɯn phɯigɯn`
4. `aŋ lɯgɯphɯrkhɯo nugɯn`

### Why these answers work

The plural marker matches the type of noun or pronoun. The final answer demonstrates plural-before-case ordering. The future verb remains unchanged across ordinary and honorific groups because its suffix expresses time rather than plural agreement.

### Retrieve and recombine

Say that you have books, then ask a respected group whether they will come tomorrow. Finally, identify the plural and object suffixes separately in the word for “the friends” as an object.

## Lesson 21: Count an item with its classifier

### Outcome and counting categories

You will count books and houses using appropriate classifiers. Bodo commonly combines a classifier with a numeral. `gaŋse bizab` means “one book,” while `gɔŋse nɔ` means “one house.” The classifier is not identical in the two phrases, because the nouns belong to different counting categories.

### Think before reading on

English can put “one” directly before both “book” and “house.” Does that predict the internal structure of a Bodo count phrase? No. First identify the noun category, then choose the classifier and numeral. A count word is not merely an English numeral written with different letters.

### Counting toolkit

| Bodo | Meaning | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| se | one | numeral in running notation |
| nɯi | two | numeral |
| tham | three | numeral |
| ba | five | numeral |
| gaŋ- | classifier for flat/leaf-like items | books, paper, cloth in relevant uses |
| gɔŋ- | classifier for several objects including houses | category-dependent |
| sa- | human classifier | people |
| bizab | book | uses gaŋ- in these examples |

### Combine classifier and numeral

`gaŋnɯi bizab` means “two books.” `gaŋtham bizab` means “three books.” The classifier remains because the item type stays the same, while the numeral changes the quantity. `gɔŋnɯi nɔ` means “two houses.” Here the numeral stays two, but the classifier changes with the noun category.

`aŋha gaŋse bizab dɔŋ` means “I have one book.” This inserts a counted noun phrase into the possession construction from Lesson 7. `aŋha gaŋnɯi bizab dɔŋ` says “I have two books.” There is no need to replace the whole sentence when the number changes.

Bodo allows variation in the order of noun, numeral, and modifier phrases; many traditional expressions place a counted phrase after the noun. The course uses the classifier-numeral-before-noun order in these examples, a pattern explicitly found in the reference. It is not an assertion that every Bodo noun phrase must follow English modifier order.

The source marks tones on isolated numerals but often omits them in running morphology. The introductory tone disclaimer applies here too. Do not interpret the unaccented running numeral as a claim that number words have no lexical tone.

### Common trap

Do not use `gaŋ-` for every thing you can count. Nor should you add an ordinary plural suffix automatically just because English says “books.” A count phrase already specifies plurality through the numeral. Learn wider classifier choices from dependable examples rather than guessing from rough shape alone.

### Practice

Use the classifier-numeral-before-noun pattern.

1. One book.
2. Two books.
3. Two houses.
4. I have three books.

***Answers:***

1. `gaŋse bizab`
2. `gaŋnɯi bizab`
3. `gɔŋnɯi nɔ`
4. `aŋha gaŋtham bizab dɔŋ`

### Why these answers work

The classifier identifies the counting category, while the numeral identifies the quantity. The first three are noun phrases. The fourth places a counted book phrase inside possessive existence. The existence verb does not change for the number three.

### Retrieve and recombine

Say that you have two books, then count one house. Explain which change concerns quantity and which concerns classifier category. Finally, locate one of the books at home using a separate location sentence.

## Lesson 22: Recognise a marked subject

### Outcome and subject morphology

You will recognise subject marking on a definite or discourse-established noun phrase. Bodo can use a nominative suffix with several sound-conditioned forms. A consonant-final ordinary noun can take `-a`, while the personal pronoun `bi` takes `-jɯ` in the marked form `bijɯ`. This marker is distinct from habitual `-jɯ` on a verb.

### Think before reading on

If the same sequence `jɯ` appears after a pronoun and after an action stem, must it have the same function? No. In `bijɯ`, it marks the subject expression. In `zajɯ`, it contributes habitual aspect to the eating verb. Word category and position are necessary clues.

### Subject forms to inspect

| Bodo | Meaning | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| bi | he/she | plain pronoun |
| bijɯ | he/she, marked as subject | pronoun plus nominative |
| bizaba | the book, marked as subject | noun plus nominative |
| lɯgɯa | the friend, marked as subject | noun plus nominative |
| bisɯrɯ | they, marked as subject | plural pronoun plus nominative |
| -a | nominative on selected nouns | noun suffix here |
| -jɯ | nominative on vowel-final pronouns | not habitual in this position |
| -ɯ | nominative on consonant-final pronouns | category-conditioned form |

### Read a whole marked sentence

`bijɯ ɯŋkham zabai` means “He or she has eaten rice.” The marked subject precedes the object and perfect verb. `lɯgɯa phɯigɯn` means “The friend will come.” `bisɯrɯ gabɯn thaŋgɯn` means “They will go tomorrow.” The nominative marker helps identify the subject without changing the future verb's person agreement.

Compare `aŋ lɯgɯkhɯo nugɯn`, “I will see the friend,” with `lɯgɯa phɯigɯn`, “The friend will come.” The friend is an object in the first and a subject in the second. Its ending changes according to role. That is a more reliable analysis than assuming the first noun always has one fixed suffix and the second noun another.

Earlier short pronoun examples used a common unmarked frame. Subject marking interacts with definiteness and discourse, so this lesson should not be converted into a demand that every sentence must contain it. The aim is recognition and controlled use of clear forms, not a false universal requirement.

### Common trap

Do not confuse nominative `-a` with the negative `-a` attached to a verb. In `lɯgɯa`, the suffix follows a noun and marks its role. In `thaŋa`, it follows an action stem and negates the action. Reading only the final letter is insufficient.

### Practice

Use the marked subject indicated by the lesson.

1. She has eaten rice. (use bijɯ)
2. The friend will come. (mark the subject)
3. They will go tomorrow. (use bisɯrɯ)
4. The book. (subject-marked noun only)

***Answers:***

1. `bijɯ ɯŋkham zabai`
2. `lɯgɯa phɯigɯn`
3. `bisɯrɯ gabɯn thaŋgɯn`
4. `bizaba`

### Why these answers work

The suffixes attach to subjects according to the selected noun or pronoun shape. The action morphology remains independently meaningful. The final prompt deliberately asks for a noun form rather than a complete sentence.

### Retrieve and recombine

Make “friend” the subject of a coming sentence, then the object of a seeing sentence. Identify each suffix's function. Finally, distinguish a subject `-jɯ` from a habitual `-jɯ` in two separate examples.

## Lesson 23: Express ability and necessity

### Outcome and a nonfinite action

You will say that you can go and that you need to go. Bodo can place a nonfinite action in `-nɯ` before a modal auxiliary. `aŋ thaŋnɯ hagɯn` means “I can go” in the prospective possibility pattern used here. The action is marked as a complement, while the following auxiliary carries the finite future morphology.

### Think before reading on

If two verbs occur together, must both be independently future-marked? No. In this construction, `thaŋnɯ` identifies the action whose possibility is under discussion, and `hagɯn` supplies the modal predicate. Adding `-gɯn` to the first verb as well would ignore the division of grammatical work.

### Modal vocabulary

| Bodo | Meaning | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| -nɯ | nonfinite marker on a verb | introduces the action complement |
| ha | can; may | modal auxiliary |
| naŋ | need | modal auxiliary |
| hagɯn | can/will be able, prospective | finite modal form |
| naŋgɯn | need to; should, prospective | necessity form |
| thaŋnɯ | to go | action complement |
| phɯinɯ | to come | action complement |
| gabɯn | tomorrow | time expression |

### Possibility is not the same as a promise

`nɯŋthaŋ phɯinɯ hagɯn` means “You can come,” respectfully, in a context of practical possibility. It does not necessarily mean that the addressee has promised to come. `aŋ gabɯn thaŋnɯ naŋgɯn` means “I need to go tomorrow.” The obligation or necessity differs from ability even though the proposed action is the same.

`zɯŋ thaŋnɯ hagɯn` means “We can go.” `zɯŋ gabɯn thaŋnɯ naŋgɯn` means “We need to go tomorrow.” The group pronoun does not change the modal verb's person ending. It changes who has the possibility or necessity.

The noun-attached `-nɯ` in a giving sentence marked a recipient. Here the same-looking suffix attaches to a verb and marks a nonfinite complement. This is another example of why a grammatical form must be interpreted in its environment. It is not contradictory for a language to reuse a sound sequence in related or distinct functions.

### Common trap

Do not interpret every `-nɯ` as English “to a person.” Nor should you treat “can,” “need,” and “will” as identical commitments. A speaker can be able to go, need to go, and still not have stated a definite plan to go.

### Practice

Use the prospective modal patterns taught here.

1. I can go.
2. We can go.
3. You can come. (honorific)
4. I need to go tomorrow.

***Answers:***

1. `aŋ thaŋnɯ hagɯn`
2. `zɯŋ thaŋnɯ hagɯn`
3. `nɯŋthaŋ phɯinɯ hagɯn`
4. `aŋ gabɯn thaŋnɯ naŋgɯn`

### Why these answers work

The first action takes the nonfinite marker, and the modal follows as the finite predicate. The first three express possibility; the fourth expresses necessity. The honorific in the third remains part of the subject pronoun.

### Retrieve and recombine

State that you can come, that you need to go tomorrow, and that you will go tomorrow. Explain how the three statements differ. Then identify a recipient `-nɯ` and a verbal nonfinite `-nɯ` in earlier lessons.

## Lesson 24: Connect two actions in order

### Outcome and a linked action

You will say that you will act after doing something else. Bodo can mark a preceding action with a conjunctive form in `-na` or `-nanɯi`. The earlier action is nonfinite, while the final verb carries the clause's main temporal commitment. This is useful for plans such as drinking tea and then going home.

### Think before reading on

In “After drinking water, I will go home,” which action carries the explicit promise about the future? The final going event does. The drinking action sets up the sequence. Bodo can reflect this by marking the first action as linked rather than giving both verbs an independent future suffix.

### Sequence toolkit

| Bodo | Meaning | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| -na | conjunctive link | marks a preceding action |
| -nanɯi | conjunctive link | another selected linking form |
| lɯŋna | having drunk; after drinking | nonfinite action |
| thaŋnanɯi | having gone; go and | nonfinite action |
| phɯi | come | possible final action |
| nɔsim | homeward endpoint | destination |
| saha | tea | first-action object |
| dɯi | water | first-action object |

### Build the sequence as two meaningful chunks

`aŋ saha lɯŋna nɔsim thaŋgɯn` means “After drinking tea, I will go home.” `aŋ dɯi lɯŋna phɯigɯn` means “After drinking water, I will come.” The first action sets the sequence, while the last action takes the future suffix. The speaker is understood as performing both actions in these examples.

`thaŋnanɯi phɯi` means “Go and come.” This compact linked instruction uses an imperative final action rather than a future statement. It demonstrates that a nonfinite first action can combine with different types of final predicate. You should still identify the intended participants and speech act before forming a longer sequence.

Do not assume that the conjunction means precisely the same temporal relation in every possible discourse. The selected examples use a natural earlier-action interpretation. Bodo has other subordinating and linking devices, but this small pattern is enough to turn separate meal and travel statements into a coherent plan.

### Common trap

Do not give the first action both a future suffix and the nonfinite link in this selected construction. Also avoid switching the subject halfway through without learning how the language makes that switch clear. Our examples intentionally keep one subject across the linked actions.

### Practice

Link the earlier action to the final one.

1. After drinking tea, I will go home.
2. After drinking water, I will come.
3. After drinking water, we will go home.
4. Go and come. (use thaŋnanɯi)

***Answers:***

1. `aŋ saha lɯŋna nɔsim thaŋgɯn`
2. `aŋ dɯi lɯŋna phɯigɯn`
3. `zɯŋ dɯi lɯŋna nɔsim thaŋgɯn`
4. `thaŋnanɯi phɯi`

### Why these answers work

The first verb is nonfinite and linked to a final predicate. The first three end with a future verb; the fourth ends with an imperative. The third changes the shared participant to a group while preserving the event order.

### Retrieve and recombine

First give two separate statements about drinking tea and going home. Then join them with the learned link. Explain which action is earlier and where the future information appears in the combined sentence.

## Lesson 25: Rebuild a visit and a daily account

### Outcome and cumulative use

You will combine a request, a statement about a current activity, a past event, and a future plan. This review asks you to choose among constructions rather than learn another list of suffixes. A useful foundation requires distinguishing identity, existence, possession, action aspect, time, and speech act in real messages.

### Think before reading on

A visitor has arrived. You invite them to drink water, explain that you have eaten, and say that you will go home tomorrow. Which clause is a request, which reports accomplishment, and which states a plan? Build each separately before connecting them into an exchange.

### Retrieval map

| Bodo | Meaning | Main decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| phɯidɯ | please come | imperative |
| dɯi lɯŋdɯ | please drink water | object plus request |
| aŋha bizab dɔŋ | I have a book | possessive existence |
| aŋ ɯŋkham zabai | I have eaten rice | perfect accomplishment |
| aŋ undugasinɯ dɔŋ | I am sleeping | progressive |
| aŋ phɯidɯŋmɯn | I came | completed past |
| aŋ thaŋgɯn | I will go | future |
| nɯŋthaŋ phɯigɯnna | will you come, honorific | future question |

### A short exchange to understand, not recite

Host: `phɯidɯ`, “Please come.” Host: `dɯi lɯŋdɯ`, “Please drink water.” Visitor: `aŋ ɯŋkham zabai`, “I have eaten rice.” Host: `nɯŋthaŋ gabɯn phɯigɯnna?`, “Will you come tomorrow?” Visitor: `aŋ gabɯn phɯigɯn`, “I will come tomorrow.”

Each line has a specific purpose. The request does not predict an event; the perfect does not express ownership; the question does not promise attendance. If you can explain those differences, you can replace the drink, day, or participant without relying on a memorised script.

For a small daily account, combine `aŋ mɯija phɯidɯŋmɯn`, “I came yesterday,” with `aŋ nɔwao dɔŋ`, “I am at home,” and `aŋ gabɯn thaŋgɯn`, “I will go tomorrow.” The time words help organise the story, but the verbal constructions still provide essential information.

### Common trap

Do not flatten all similar-looking endings into one meaning. `dɔŋ` and `-dɯŋ` have different vowels and functions; `-ni` can mark possession or a mood in different positions; `-mɯn` can participate in past or plural morphology. Their environments are part of what you have learned.

### Practice

Recombine the patterns with the specified details.

1. Please drink tea.
2. I have two books.
3. I came yesterday.
4. Will you go home tomorrow? (honorific)

***Answers:***

1. `saha lɯŋdɯ`
2. `aŋha gaŋnɯi bizab dɔŋ`
3. `aŋ mɯija phɯidɯŋmɯn`
4. `nɯŋthaŋ gabɯn nɔsim thaŋgɯnna?`

### Why these answers work

The first is an imperative with an object. The second embeds a counted noun phrase inside possessive existence. The third places an event in the past with aspect and tense morphology. The fourth combines respectful address, a future day, a destination, and a question suffix.

### Retrieve and recombine

Create four short statements about an identity or location, a possession, a completed event, and a future plan. Ask one respectful question. Then identify a grammatical choice you can explain and a pronunciation point you still need to hear from a fluent speaker.
