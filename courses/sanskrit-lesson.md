# Sanskrit: A pattern-first foundation

This course teaches basic Classical Sanskrit grammar in Devanagari with IAST romanisation. It is a foundation for reading and deliberate classroom conversation, not Vedic recitation, a reconstruction of ancient everyday speech or a claim of modern native usage. Long vowels ā ī ū contrast with a i u; e and o are inherently long. ṛ is a vowel; ṭ ḍ ṇ are retroflex, ś ṣ s differ, and an h after a stop marks aspiration. ṃ is anusvāra and ḥ is visarga. Before Lesson 22, examples deliberately display separate word forms with external sandhi suspended, like a grammatical word-by-word analysis. They are not models of continuously sandhied classical prose. Lesson 22 contrasts connected forms; Lessons 23–25 return to the same explicitly separated teaching convention. Internal word morphology is retained throughout.

## Lesson 1: Actions with I, you, he and she

### Build the pattern

You will build a complete sentence meaning I read and change the person performing the action. अहम् aham means I, त्वम् tvam means you singular, सः saḥ means he, and सा sā means she. पठामि paṭhāmi means I read, पठसि paṭhasi means you read, and पठति paṭhati means he or she reads. The verb marks person, but the ordinary third-person present does not change for a male versus female subject. Sanskrit often omits a pronoun when the ending and context identify the person. We retain it initially to expose the pattern. Final word forms are displayed separately, with external sandhi suspended as stated in the introduction.

### Think before reading on

If paṭhati works with he, must it acquire a feminine ending when the subject becomes she?

No. Sā paṭhati uses the same finite verb. Sanskrit grammatical gender matters in nouns, pronouns and adjectives, but it does not create a masculine-feminine distinction in this finite present ending. Person and number are the relevant agreement categories here.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| अहम् पठामि। | Aham paṭhāmi. | I read. |
| त्वम् पठसि। | Tvam paṭhasi. | You read. (singular) |
| सः पठति। | Saḥ paṭhati. | He reads. |
| सा पठति। | Sā paṭhati. | She reads. |
| अहम् गच्छामि। | Aham gacchāmi. | I go. |
| त्वम् गच्छसि। | Tvam gacchasi. | You go. (singular) |

### Worked examples

`अहम् पठामि।` - `Aham paṭhāmi.` means “I read.” The first-person ending identifies the speaker, and the long ā before mi belongs to this present form.

`त्वम् पठसि।` - `Tvam paṭhasi.` means “You read. (singular)” The second-person ending replaces the first-person material, making the action belong to the single addressee.

`सः पठति।` - `Saḥ paṭhati.` means “He reads.” The third-person singular ending identifies a person being described rather than the speaker or listener.

### Common trap

Do not call -ti a masculine ending. It is third-person singular in this pattern. Also distinguish aspirated ṭh in paṭh from an English t followed by a separate h sound.

### Practice

Build these meanings in Sanskrit. Keep the stated person and register.

1. I read.
2. You read. (singular)
3. He reads.
4. She reads.
5. I go.
6. You go. (singular)

***Answers:***

1. `अहम् पठामि।` - `Aham paṭhāmi.`
2. `त्वम् पठसि।` - `Tvam paṭhasi.`
3. `सः पठति।` - `Saḥ paṭhati.`
4. `सा पठति।` - `Sā paṭhati.`
5. `अहम् गच्छामि।` - `Aham gacchāmi.`
6. `त्वम् गच्छसि।` - `Tvam gacchasi.`

### Why these answers work

Answer 4: The feminine pronoun changes the referent without changing the ordinary third-person singular finite verb ending.

Answer 5: The going verb has its own present stem gaccha-, but it shares the first-person ending pattern with reading.

Answer 6: The second-person ending transfers to the going stem, allowing a new action to be built from an already understood agreement pattern.

### Retrieve and recombine

Say that you read, that another person reads and that you go. Change one participant at a time and identify the verb material that changes with person.


## Lesson 2: Identity, the verb be and careful sounds

### Build the pattern

You will identify yourself as a student and recognise the irregular present of be. अस्मि asmi means I am, असि asi means you are, and अस्ति asti means is. These forms differ from the ordinary -āmi, -asi, -ati pattern of the first lesson. छात्रः chātraḥ is a male student, while छात्रा chātrā is a female student. The predicate noun is nominative, like a subject noun, because it identifies the person rather than receiving an action. Sanskrit can omit the present copula in suitable identity statements, but this lesson includes it to teach the forms. Pronounce aspirated ch as one consonant with extra breath, not as c plus a separate syllable.

### Think before reading on

Does a female speaker need a different first-person verb for I am, or a different form of student?

The verb stays asmi. The noun changes to chātrā to identify a female student. This separates noun gender from finite verb agreement, preventing the mistaken assumption that every word must acquire a feminine ending.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| अहम् छात्रः अस्मि। | Aham chātraḥ asmi. | I am a male student. |
| अहम् छात्रा अस्मि। | Aham chātrā asmi. | I am a female student. |
| त्वम् छात्रः असि। | Tvam chātraḥ asi. | You are a male student. (singular) |
| सा छात्रा अस्ति। | Sā chātrā asti. | She is a female student. |
| सः छात्रः अस्ति। | Saḥ chātraḥ asti. | He is a male student. |
| अहम् अत्र अस्मि। | Aham atra asmi. | I am here. |

### Worked examples

`अहम् छात्रः अस्मि।` - `Aham chātraḥ asmi.` means “I am a male student.” The predicate noun is masculine nominative, while the first-person copula identifies the speaker independently of noun gender.

`अहम् छात्रा अस्मि।` - `Aham chātrā asmi.` means “I am a female student.” The feminine noun changes the description, but asmi remains the same first-person form used by a male speaker.

`त्वम् छात्रः असि।` - `Tvam chātraḥ asi.` means “You are a male student. (singular)” The copula takes second-person agreement because the student is the addressee rather than the speaker.

### Common trap

Do not attach ordinary -āmi to the stem of be to invent its present. Asmi, asi and asti are the actual common forms and should be recognised together.

### Practice

Build these meanings in Sanskrit. Keep the stated person and register.

1. I am a male student.
2. I am a female student.
3. You are a male student. (singular)
4. She is a female student.
5. He is a male student.
6. I am here.

***Answers:***

1. `अहम् छात्रः अस्मि।` - `Aham chātraḥ asmi.`
2. `अहम् छात्रा अस्मि।` - `Aham chātrā asmi.`
3. `त्वम् छात्रः असि।` - `Tvam chātraḥ asi.`
4. `सा छात्रा अस्ति।` - `Sā chātrā asti.`
5. `सः छात्रः अस्ति।` - `Saḥ chātraḥ asti.`
6. `अहम् अत्र अस्मि।` - `Aham atra asmi.`

### Why these answers work

Answer 4: The feminine pronoun and noun identify the same person, while asti is simply third-person singular.

Answer 5: The subject and predicate noun both use nominative forms; neither is an object receiving an action.

Answer 6: Atra supplies a place instead of an identity noun, showing another useful environment for the irregular copula.

### Retrieve and recombine

Introduce yourself as a student using the appropriate noun, then describe another student. Read the long vowel in chātrā and the visarga in chātraḥ carefully.


## Lesson 3: Questions and the negative particle

### Build the pattern

You will ask whether an action happens, ask where someone goes and deny an action. किम् kim can introduce a yes-or-no question in elementary prose. कुत्र kutra means where, and अत्र atra means here while तत्र tatra means there. न na is a common negative particle placed before the material it negates, often before the verb in a short sentence. Sanskrit does not need an auxiliary corresponding to English do. A question can retain ordinary person agreement on the main verb. A negative statement also retains the affirmative finite verb, unlike languages with a separate negative conjugation for every such sentence.

### Think before reading on

If I do not read contains the English support verb do, must Sanskrit add a second finite verb beside paṭhāmi?

No. Aham na paṭhāmi expresses the negative directly. The particle na changes the proposition's polarity, while paṭhāmi still supplies reading and first-person agreement. The English auxiliary is a feature of English grammar, not a required extra Sanskrit word.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| किम् त्वम् पठसि? | Kim tvam paṭhasi? | Do you read? (singular) |
| कुत्र त्वम् गच्छसि? | Kutra tvam gacchasi? | Where do you go? (singular) |
| अहम् न पठामि। | Aham na paṭhāmi. | I do not read. |
| सा न गच्छति। | Sā na gacchati. | She does not go. |
| सः तत्र अस्ति। | Saḥ tatra asti. | He is there. |
| किम् सः अत्र अस्ति? | Kim saḥ atra asti? | Is he here? |

### Worked examples

`किम् त्वम् पठसि?` - `Kim tvam paṭhasi?` means “Do you read? (singular)” The initial question particle asks for confirmation while the verb retains the ending for the single addressee.

`कुत्र त्वम् गच्छसि?` - `Kutra tvam gacchasi?` means “Where do you go? (singular)” The information question asks for a place, and the going verb continues to agree with the second-person subject.

`अहम् न पठामि।` - `Aham na paṭhāmi.` means “I do not read.” Na negates the reading action without requiring a second finite verb equivalent to English do.

### Common trap

Do not change paṭhasi to paṭhati merely because the sentence is a question. Direct singular address remains second-person unless an honorific construction later requires otherwise.

### Practice

Build these meanings in Sanskrit. Keep the stated person and register.

1. Do you read? (singular)
2. Where do you go? (singular)
3. I do not read.
4. She does not go.
5. He is there.
6. Is he here?

***Answers:***

1. `किम् त्वम् पठसि?` - `Kim tvam paṭhasi?`
2. `कुत्र त्वम् गच्छसि?` - `Kutra tvam gacchasi?`
3. `अहम् न पठामि।` - `Aham na paṭhāmi.`
4. `सा न गच्छति।` - `Sā na gacchati.`
5. `सः तत्र अस्ति।` - `Saḥ tatra asti.`
6. `किम् सः अत्र अस्ति?` - `Kim saḥ atra asti?`

### Why these answers work

Answer 4: The negative particle leaves third-person singular agreement intact, and the feminine subject does not change the verb ending.

Answer 5: The place word supplies location, while the irregular third-person copula remains the same form introduced in identity statements.

Answer 6: The question particle checks a location statement without an English-style inversion of the subject and copula.

### Retrieve and recombine

Ask a friend where they go and whether they read. Then deny your own reading and locate another person there, preserving the correct person endings.


## Lesson 4: Masculine nouns as subjects and objects

### Build the pattern

You will distinguish a man who sees from a man who is seen. नरः naraḥ is a masculine nominative singular, while नरम् naram is accusative singular. बालः bālaḥ means boy, and बालम् bālam is its object form. पश्यति paśyati means sees. Sanskrit case endings carry grammatical roles, so word order is more flexible than in English. The neutral examples use subject, object, verb to make the structure visible, but reversing the two noun phrases does not automatically reverse who sees whom. Learn the nominative-accusative contrast first with one regular masculine a-stem pattern.

### Think before reading on

If naraḥ bālam paśyati is reordered as bālam naraḥ paśyati, do the endings make the boy become the observer?

No. Naraḥ remains nominative and bālam remains accusative. Their roles survive the reordering. English relies much more heavily on position, so a Sanskrit reader must attend to endings rather than identifying the first noun automatically as the subject.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| नरः बालम् पश्यति। | Naraḥ bālam paśyati. | The man sees the boy. |
| बालः नरम् पश्यति। | Bālaḥ naram paśyati. | The boy sees the man. |
| अहम् नरम् पश्यामि। | Aham naram paśyāmi. | I see the man. |
| त्वम् बालम् पश्यसि। | Tvam bālam paśyasi. | You see the boy. (singular) |
| बालम् नरः पश्यति। | Bālam naraḥ paśyati. | The man sees the boy. (object first) |
| नरः न गच्छति। | Naraḥ na gacchati. | The man does not go. |

### Worked examples

`नरः बालम् पश्यति।` - `Naraḥ bālam paśyati.` means “The man sees the boy.” The nominative man performs the seeing, while the accusative boy is the person seen despite their ordinary English-like order.

`बालः नरम् पश्यति।` - `Bālaḥ naram paśyati.` means “The boy sees the man.” Both noun endings change when the participants exchange roles; merely swapping their positions would not accomplish the same transformation.

`अहम् नरम् पश्यामि।` - `Aham naram paśyāmi.` means “I see the man.” The object retains accusative marking, while the finite verb changes to first-person because the speaker now performs the seeing.

### Common trap

Do not treat every word ending in m as a masculine object. Neuter nouns have their own patterns, introduced next, and the same visible ending can serve different grammatical functions.

### Practice

Build these meanings in Sanskrit. Keep the stated person and register.

1. The man sees the boy.
2. The boy sees the man.
3. I see the man.
4. You see the boy. (singular)
5. The man sees the boy. (object first)
6. The man does not go.

***Answers:***

1. `नरः बालम् पश्यति।` - `Naraḥ bālam paśyati.`
2. `बालः नरम् पश्यति।` - `Bālaḥ naram paśyati.`
3. `अहम् नरम् पश्यामि।` - `Aham naram paśyāmi.`
4. `त्वम् बालम् पश्यसि।` - `Tvam bālam paśyasi.`
5. `बालम् नरः पश्यति।` - `Bālam naraḥ paśyati.`
6. `नरः न गच्छति।` - `Naraḥ na gacchati.`

### Why these answers work

Answer 4: The object ending remains independent of the verb's second-person agreement with the addressee who sees.

Answer 5: The reordered sentence keeps the same roles because accusative and nominative endings continue to identify object and subject.

Answer 6: A noun subject selects third-person singular agreement just as a third-person pronoun did in earlier negative statements.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe a man seeing a boy, then reverse the participants by changing both case endings. Finally reorder one sentence without changing its meaning, explaining why the roles stay stable.


## Lesson 5: Neuter nouns and destinations

### Build the pattern

You will use fruit, water and a village destination. फलम् phalam means fruit and is neuter singular; its nominative and accusative forms are identical. जलम् jalam, water, behaves similarly in the cases used here. खादति khādati means eats, and पिबति pibati means drinks. ग्रामः grāmaḥ is a masculine village noun; ग्रामम् grāmam can mark the destination of going. Accusative therefore has more than one use: it marks many direct objects and can also mark a goal of motion. The verb and context help distinguish eating a fruit from going to a village. No gendered finite ending is required for a neuter subject.

### Think before reading on

If phalam ends in m when it is present here, must it be an object merely because naram was an object in the previous lesson?

No. Neuter nominative and accusative singular coincide. You must consider the noun's gender and the whole sentence. Case endings are powerful clues, but their visible shapes are not unique labels in every noun class.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| फलम् अत्र अस्ति। | Phalam atra asti. | The fruit is here. |
| अहम् फलम् खादामि। | Aham phalam khādāmi. | I eat fruit. |
| सा जलम् पिबति। | Sā jalam pibati. | She drinks water. |
| अहम् जलम् पिबामि। | Aham jalam pibāmi. | I drink water. |
| सः ग्रामम् गच्छति। | Saḥ grāmam gacchati. | He goes to the village. |
| किम् त्वम् फलम् खादसि? | Kim tvam phalam khādasi? | Do you eat fruit? (singular) |

### Worked examples

`फलम् अत्र अस्ति।` - `Phalam atra asti.` means “The fruit is here.” The neuter noun is nominative as the subject of presence, despite sharing its shape with the accusative singular.

`अहम् फलम् खादामि।` - `Aham phalam khādāmi.` means “I eat fruit.” The same visible noun form is now an object, while the first-person eating verb identifies the agent.

`सा जलम् पिबति।` - `Sā jalam pibati.` means “She drinks water.” The neuter substance is accusative as the object of drinking, and the feminine subject leaves the finite ending unchanged.

### Common trap

Do not translate grāmam with an added Sanskrit preposition to simply because English says to the village. The accusative can express the destination directly in this basic construction.

### Practice

Build these meanings in Sanskrit. Keep the stated person and register.

1. The fruit is here.
2. I eat fruit.
3. She drinks water.
4. I drink water.
5. He goes to the village.
6. Do you eat fruit? (singular)

***Answers:***

1. `फलम् अत्र अस्ति।` - `Phalam atra asti.`
2. `अहम् फलम् खादामि।` - `Aham phalam khādāmi.`
3. `सा जलम् पिबति।` - `Sā jalam pibati.`
4. `अहम् जलम् पिबामि।` - `Aham jalam pibāmi.`
5. `सः ग्रामम् गच्छति।` - `Saḥ grāmam gacchati.`
6. `किम् त्वम् फलम् खादसि?` - `Kim tvam phalam khādasi?`

### Why these answers work

Answer 4: The drinking verb changes person while the water object remains in the same case and position.

Answer 5: The accusative village marks a destination rather than something directly affected by eating or seeing.

Answer 6: The question particle and second-person verb frame a question while the neuter accusative object keeps its familiar form.

### Retrieve and recombine

Say that fruit is here, that you eat fruit and that you go to a village. Explain why the same ending shape does not imply the same role in all three.


## Lesson 6: Feminine nouns and their object forms

### Build the pattern

You will use a girl, a garland and a school as feminine nouns. बालिका bālikā is nominative singular and बालिकाम् bālikām is accusative singular. माला mālā, garland, and शाला śālā, hall or school, follow the same ā-stem contrast in these cases. Grammatical gender is not restricted to living beings: a garland is feminine without being a female person. The finite verb still agrees in person and number rather than masculine or feminine gender. A feminine destination can take the accusative just as the masculine village did. The long ā remains important in both subject and object forms.

### Think before reading on

Why does a garland use a feminine noun pattern even though it is not a woman or girl?

Grammatical gender is a property of the noun class, not a universal statement about biological sex. It governs related noun and adjective forms. Learn a noun's gender with its usable forms instead of guessing from the object's real-world properties.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| बालिका पठति। | Bālikā paṭhati. | The girl reads. |

| माला अत्र अस्ति। | Mālā atra asti. | The garland is here. |
| सा मालाम् पश्यति। | Sā mālām paśyati. | She sees the garland. |
| अहम् शालाम् गच्छामि। | Aham śālām gacchāmi. | I go to the school. |
| बालिका जलम् पिबति। | Bālikā jalam pibati. | The girl drinks water. |

### Worked examples

`बालिका पठति।` - `Bālikā paṭhati.` means “The girl reads.” The feminine nominative noun is the subject, while the finite verb uses the same third-person ending as with a boy.

`अहम् बालिकाम् पश्यामि।` - `Aham bālikām paśyāmi.` means “I see the girl.” The feminine noun becomes accusative with long ām, marking the person seen by the first-person observer.

`माला अत्र अस्ति।` - `Mālā atra asti.` means “The garland is here.” The inanimate garland is grammatically feminine, and its nominative form acts as the subject of the copula.

### Common trap

Do not replace every feminine nominative ā with a short a when forming the object. The accusative retains the long vowel and adds m in this regular pattern.

***Answers:***

Answer 5: The accusative feminine noun marks the destination of motion, extending the role already seen with grāmam.

## Lesson 7: Object pronouns and respectful address

### Build the pattern

You will say me and you as objects and recognise a respectful address construction. माम् mām means me, while त्वाम् tvām means you singular as object. They differ from subject aham and tvam. भवान् bhavān is respectful masculine singular address, and भवती bhavatī is respectful feminine singular address. These honorific expressions take third-person verb agreement even when English translates them as you. Thus bhavān paṭhati and bhavatī paṭhati address a person respectfully. The construction reflects the grammatical behaviour of the honorific expression rather than the ordinary second-person pronoun. It is useful in deliberate classroom Sanskrit; actual social usage depends on setting.

### Think before reading on

If bhavān refers to the person being addressed, should its verb automatically be paṭhasi because English uses you?

No. The honorific expression takes third-person agreement, giving paṭhati. Ordinary tvam uses paṭhasi. Translate the intended relationship, but preserve the grammatical agreement required by the Sanskrit expression you actually choose.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| सः माम् पश्यति। | Saḥ mām paśyati. | He sees me. |
| अहम् त्वाम् पश्यामि। | Aham tvām paśyāmi. | I see you. (singular) |
| भवान् पठति। | Bhavān paṭhati. | You read. (respectful, male) |
| भवती पठति। | Bhavatī paṭhati. | You read. (respectful, female) |

## Lesson 8: The locative for being somewhere

### Build the pattern

You will locate people and objects using the locative case. ग्रामे grāme means in the village, गृहे gṛhe means in the house or at home, and वने vane means in the forest. These forms come from regular masculine or neuter a-stems. A feminine ā-stem uses a different singular ending: शालायाम् śālāyām means in the school or hall. The locative identifies where an event or state occurs, while the accusative can identify a destination. The vowel ṛ in gṛha is a syllabic sound, not simply English r after an omitted vowel. English in does not need an independent Sanskrit word here.

### Think before reading on

How does grāme differ from grāmam if both refer to the same village?

Grāme locates a person or action in the village. Grāmam can mark the destination of going. Case changes the noun's relationship to the event rather than changing which village is meant.

## Lesson 9: Possession through the genitive

### Build the pattern

You will identify an owner and express having a resource. मम mama means my or of me, while तव tava means your or of you singular. नरस्य narasya means of the man, and बालिकायाः bālikāyāḥ means of the girl. These genitive forms can modify a noun such as पुस्तकम् pustakam, book. A genitive possessor with an existential can also express possession: mama pustakam asti, I have a book or my book exists in context. Sanskrit does not require one universal verb have corresponding to English. The possessor's case and the possessed thing's role remain distinct; owning a book does not make the owner the object of the verb.

### Think before reading on

Why does my book use mama rather than the subject pronoun aham directly before pustakam?

The speaker appears as a possessor, so the genitive form is required. English likewise distinguishes I from my. The possessed noun keeps whatever case its role in the larger sentence requires, which may be nominative or accusative.

## Lesson 10: Commands and respectful invitations

### Build the pattern

You will ask someone to read, come and sit. पठ paṭha is a direct singular imperative, while पठतु paṭhatu is third-person singular imperative, literally let him or her read. With an honorific such as bhavān, the third-person form functions as respectful address. आगच्छ āgaccha means come, while आगच्छतु āgacchatu is the third-person imperative. उपविश upaviśa means sit down; उपविशतु upaviśatu is the corresponding third-person form. Sanskrit's imperative is not merely an ordinary present with a pronoun omitted. The forms express commands, requests or wishes according to context, and respectful wording does not eliminate the need for appropriate tone.

### Think before reading on

Why can bhavān paṭhatu be addressed to a listener even though paṭhatu is grammatically third-person?

The honorific itself takes third-person agreement. The imperative follows that same pattern, so the phrase can serve as a respectful request. Ordinary tvam would instead pair with the direct second-person command paṭha.

## Lesson 11: Exactly two people and the dual

### Build the pattern

You will describe exactly two participants using the dual. आवाम् āvām means we two, युवाम् yuvām means you two, and तौ tau means those two males or a masculine pair. The present forms पठावः paṭhāvaḥ, पठथः paṭhathaḥ and पठतः paṭhataḥ express first-, second- and third-person dual respectively. Notice the aspiration difference between th and t. A masculine a-stem subject such as बालः becomes बालौ bālau for two boys. Sanskrit dual is not an optional decorative plural: it is the ordinary grammatical number for exactly two in the standard paradigm. More than two will use the plural introduced next.

### Think before reading on

If two boys read, should the verb be the singular paṭhati merely because there is one coordinated group?

No. The subject refers to two individuals and takes dual agreement, paṭhataḥ. The noun bālau likewise marks two. Counting actual participants matters more than treating the group as one English phrase.

| आवाम् गच्छावः। | Āvām gacchāvaḥ. | We two go. |

### Worked examples

`आवाम् पठावः।` - `Āvām paṭhāvaḥ.` means “We two read.” The first-person dual includes the speaker and exactly one other person, and the verb explicitly marks that number.

`युवाम् पठथः।` - `Yuvām paṭhathaḥ.` means “You two read.” The second-person dual addresses exactly two people, with aspirated th distinguishing its ending from third-person dual.

`तौ पठतः।` - `Tau paṭhataḥ.` means “Those two males read.” The third-person dual describes a masculine pair, and the unaspirated t belongs to the appropriate agreement ending.

Do not confuse paṭhathaḥ, you two read, with paṭhataḥ, they two read. Aspiration is a grammatical sound contrast here, not a spelling detail that can be ignored.

## Lesson 12: Three or more and the plural

Masculine बालाः bālāḥ means boys, while neuter फलानि phalāni means fruits. A neuter plural subject uses plural verb agreement in ordinary Sanskrit, unlike rules found in some other classical languages. First-person and second-person plural pronouns do not themselves specify gender. Keep the dual for exactly two and the plural for three or more in this foundation.

Should three fruits use a singular verb merely because the noun is neuter?

## Lesson 13: Adjectives agree with the noun they describe

### Build the pattern

You will describe a beautiful boy, girl and fruit while tracking case. सुन्दर sundara is an adjective stem. Its masculine nominative singular is सुन्दरः sundaraḥ, feminine nominative सुन्दरा sundarā, and neuter nominative or accusative सुन्दरम् sundaram. An adjective agrees with its noun in gender, number and case, although different declension classes need not always have identical-looking endings. The basic a- and ā-stem examples here make agreement especially visible. The adjective's ending follows the described noun's role, not the speaker's gender. A beautiful boy seen by the speaker needs accusative sundaram bālam, not nominative sundaraḥ bālaḥ.

### Think before reading on

If I see a beautiful girl, should the adjective agree with I or with girl?

It agrees with the girl noun it modifies. Both the adjective and bālikā take feminine accusative forms because that noun phrase is the object. The speaker separately determines first-person agreement on the finite verb.

3. `सुन्दरम् फलम् अत्र अस्ति।` - `Sundaram phalam atra asti.`
4. `अहम् सुन्दरम् बालम् पश्यामि।` - `Aham sundaram bālam paśyāmi.`
5. `अहम् सुन्दराम् बालिकाम् पश्यामि।` - `Aham sundarām bālikām paśyāmi.`
6. `अहम् सुन्दरम् फलम् पश्यामि।` - `Aham sundaram phalam paśyāmi.`

### Why these answers work

Answer 4: The whole masculine noun phrase becomes accusative because it is seen, while the speaker determines the first-person verb ending.

Answer 5: The adjective and noun both retain long ā before accusative m, showing agreement within the feminine object phrase.

Answer 6: The neuter object has the same visible shape as its nominative counterpart, so sentence structure and verb meaning clarify its role.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe a beautiful fruit as present, then as something you see. Compare the unchanged neuter shape with the masculine and feminine subject-to-object changes.


## Lesson 14: Giving to someone with the dative

### Build the pattern

You will identify a recipient using the dative case. बालाय bālāya means to or for the boy, and बालिकायै bālikāyai means to or for the girl. मह्यम् mahyam means to me, while तुभ्यम् tubhyam means to you singular. ददाति dadāti means gives, and ददामि dadāmi means I give. This irregular present does not follow every detail of paṭhati, so learn its practical forms directly. The thing given is normally accusative, while the recipient is dative. One sentence can therefore contain a subject, a transferred object and a recipient, each with its own role.

### Think before reading on

In I give fruit to the girl, should the girl use the same accusative ending as the fruit?

No. The fruit is the transferred object; the girl is the recipient. Bālikāyai marks the latter role. English often uses to for recipients, but Sanskrit can express the relation through the noun ending itself.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| अहम् बालाय फलम् ददामि। | Aham bālāya phalam dadāmi. | I give fruit to the boy. |
| अहम् बालिकायै जलम् ददामि। | Aham bālikāyai jalam dadāmi. | I give water to the girl. |
| सः मह्यम् पुस्तकम् ददाति। | Saḥ mahyam pustakam dadāti. | He gives me a book. |
| अहम् तुभ्यम् पुस्तकम् ददामि। | Aham tubhyam pustakam dadāmi. | I give you a book. (singular) |
| बालिका बालाय फलम् ददाति। | Bālikā bālāya phalam dadāti. | The girl gives fruit to the boy. |
| किम् त्वम् मह्यम् जलम् ददासि? | Kim tvam mahyam jalam dadāsi? | Do you give me water? (singular) |

### Worked examples

`अहम् बालाय फलम् ददामि।` - `Aham bālāya phalam dadāmi.` means “I give fruit to the boy.” The dative boy receives the accusative fruit, while the first-person giving verb identifies the speaker as the giver.

`अहम् बालिकायै जलम् ददामि।` - `Aham bālikāyai jalam dadāmi.` means “I give water to the girl.” The feminine dative ending differs from masculine -āya, but its recipient function is the same in the giving construction.

`सः मह्यम् पुस्तकम् ददाति।` - `Saḥ mahyam pustakam dadāti.` means “He gives me a book.” Mahyam is the recipient form of the first-person pronoun, distinct from subject aham and object mām.

### Common trap

Do not confuse dative bālāya with genitive bālasya. Giving something to a boy and identifying something as the boy's are different relations, even when the same person is involved.

6. `किम् त्वम् मह्यम् जलम् ददासि?` - `Kim tvam mahyam jalam dadāsi?`

### Why these answers work

Answer 4: The second-person dative marks the addressee as recipient, while the book remains the transferred accusative object.

Answer 5: The feminine subject, masculine recipient and neuter object each carry the form required by their own role.

Answer 6: The question uses the second-person form dadāsi, while mahyam identifies the speaker as the recipient rather than the giver.

## Lesson 15: Tools and companions with the instrumental

### Build the pattern

You will say what tool someone uses and who accompanies them. लेखन्या lekhanyā means with a pen, from feminine लेखनी lekhanī. बालेन bālena means with the boy, and मया mayā means with or by me. Instrumental case can mark means or an accompanying participant when combined with सह saha, with. A tool used for writing does not require saha in the basic example. लिखति likhati means writes. The same English with can correspond to different semantic relations, so identify whether the noun names an instrument or a companion. The adjective or pronoun form must fit the instrumental role.

### Think before reading on

Does with a pen have exactly the same meaning relationship as with a boy, even though English uses with in both?

No. A pen is an instrument of writing, while the boy can be a companion in going. Sanskrit uses instrumental forms for both in these examples, but saha makes the accompanying relationship explicit for the companion.

## Lesson 16: Starting points and the ablative

### Build the pattern

You will say where a movement begins. ग्रामात् grāmāt means from the village, गृहात् gṛhāt means from the house, and वनात् vanāt means from the forest. These are ablative singular forms of regular a-stem nouns. आगच्छति āgacchati means comes, while गच्छति gacchati means goes. A journey can name both a source in the ablative and a destination in the accusative. The locative introduced earlier instead names a place where something happens. English from, to and in make a useful three-way comparison, but Sanskrit expresses these relations through case forms rather than obligatory separate prepositions.

### Think before reading on

If you change from the village to in the village, can the noun remain grāmāt?

No. Grāmāt marks a source, while grāme marks a location. The noun identifies the same place but changes its relationship to the event. Treat case selection as a decision about meaning rather than a decorative ending.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| अहम् ग्रामात् आगच्छामि। | Aham grāmāt āgacchāmi. | I come from the village. |
| सा गृहात् आगच्छति। | Sā gṛhāt āgacchati. | She comes from the house. |
| बालः वनात् आगच्छति। | Bālaḥ vanāt āgacchati. | The boy comes from the forest. |
| अहम् गृहात् ग्रामम् गच्छामि। | Aham gṛhāt grāmam gacchāmi. | I go from the house to the village. |
| सः ग्रामे अस्ति। | Saḥ grāme asti. | He is in the village. |
| किम् त्वम् वनात् आगच्छसि? | Kim tvam vanāt āgacchasi? | Do you come from the forest? (singular) |

### Worked examples

`अहम् ग्रामात् आगच्छामि।` - `Aham grāmāt āgacchāmi.` means “I come from the village.” The ablative village marks the source, and the prefixed coming verb identifies movement toward the relevant point.

`सा गृहात् आगच्छति।` - `Sā gṛhāt āgacchati.` means “She comes from the house.” The house noun takes the same regular ablative ending, while the feminine subject does not require a special finite ending.

`बालः वनात् आगच्छति।` - `Bālaḥ vanāt āgacchati.` means “The boy comes from the forest.” The neuter forest noun can mark a source with -āt, showing that the ending pattern spans masculine and neuter a-stems.

### Common trap

Do not interpret every -āt as an English past marker just because it resembles a verb ending elsewhere. Here it belongs to an ablative noun and says where the journey starts.

Answer 4: The source is ablative and the destination accusative, making the two spatial relationships explicit within one sentence.

Answer 5: The locative contrasts with source and destination forms by locating the person rather than describing a journey's endpoint.

## Lesson 17: A basic narrative past: the imperfect

### Build the pattern

You will report completed past actions with a small set of imperfect forms. अपठम् apaṭham means I read in the past, अपठत् apaṭhat means he or she read, and अगच्छम् agaccham means I went. The initial a- is the augment, and the final endings differ from present forms. Traditional grammar associates this tense with past events not of today, while actual literary usage and other past constructions are richer than one English label suggests. This foundation uses ह्यः hyaḥ, yesterday, to create unambiguous introductory past contexts. Sanskrit also has perfect and aorist systems; this lesson does not present the imperfect as the only past tense.

### Think before reading on

Can I went be formed by adding yesterday to gacchāmi and leaving the present verb unchanged in these ordinary narrative exercises?

No. Use agaccham for the imperfect first-person form. The time word locates the event, while the verb supplies the chosen past construction. Both the augment and the ending help distinguish it from the present.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| अहम् ह्यः अपठम्। | Aham hyaḥ apaṭham. | I read yesterday. |
| सः ह्यः अपठत्। | Saḥ hyaḥ apaṭhat. | He read yesterday. |
| सा ह्यः अपठत्। | Sā hyaḥ apaṭhat. | She read yesterday. |
| अहम् ग्रामम् अगच्छम्। | Aham grāmam agaccham. | I went to the village. |
| बालिका ग्रामम् अगच्छत्। | Bālikā grāmam agacchat. | The girl went to the village. |
| अहम् ह्यः न अपठम्। | Aham hyaḥ na apaṭham. | I did not read yesterday. |

### Worked examples

`अहम् ह्यः अपठम्।` - `Aham hyaḥ apaṭham.` means “I read yesterday.” The augment and first-person past ending distinguish the imperfect from present paṭhāmi, while hyaḥ provides the past day.

`सः ह्यः अपठत्।` - `Saḥ hyaḥ apaṭhat.` means “He read yesterday.” The past stem stays but the ending becomes third-person singular, identifying a person being described rather than the speaker.

`सा ह्यः अपठत्।` - `Sā hyaḥ apaṭhat.` means “She read yesterday.” The feminine subject shares the ordinary third-person imperfect form with the masculine subject, just as in the present.

### Common trap

Do not keep present -āmi after the augment and assume the result is the imperfect. The past series has its own endings, so agaccham is not agacchāmi.

## Lesson 18: Describing a completed action with participles

### Build the pattern

You will recognise a common past-active participial construction. पठितवान् paṭhitavān describes a male who has read, while पठितवती paṭhitavatī describes a female who has read. With aham, these can state I read or I have read in a basic classroom context, often without an expressed present copula. Unlike the finite imperfect, this participle agrees in gender with the person it describes. गतवान् gatavān and गतवती gatavatī similarly express having gone. The English translation depends on context and should not be forced into a one-to-one present-perfect equivalence. This construction is especially common in modern pedagogical conversation, while Classical Sanskrit has a wider range of past-expression strategies.

### Think before reading on

Why can a male and female speaker both use apaṭham but need different paṭhitavān and paṭhitavatī forms here?

Apaṭham is a finite first-person past verb without gender agreement. The -tavat participle behaves like a describing nominal and therefore agrees with its subject's gender. The difference follows from the grammatical type of the predicate.

`अहम् पठितवान्।` - `Aham paṭhitavān.` means “I have read. (male speaker)” The past-active participle is masculine nominative singular because it describes a male speaker, with the copula left unexpressed.

## Lesson 19: A simple future with familiar person endings

### Build the pattern

You will state a plan for tomorrow using the simple future. पठिष्यामि paṭhiṣyāmi means I will read, पठिष्यति paṭhiṣyati means he or she will read, and गमिष्यामि gamiṣyāmi means I will go. The future stem of go is based on gam-, not the present stem gaccha-. श्वः śvaḥ means tomorrow, while अद्य adya means today. Future formation varies across verbs, so this lesson uses a small verified set rather than claiming a universal attach-anywhere suffix. Person endings resemble the present series, helping you reuse an earlier distinction while learning new tense stems.

### Think before reading on

Should the future of gacchāmi be made by inserting a future marker after the entire present stem gaccha-?

For this verb, the common future is gamiṣyāmi. The root and present stem differ, and future formation uses its own stem. Recognising this prevents a mechanically generated form that looks plausible but is not the model taught here.

| --- | --- | --- |

| सा श्वः पठिष्यति। | Sā śvaḥ paṭhiṣyati. | She will read tomorrow. |

| अहम् श्वः न गमिष्यामि। | Aham śvaḥ na gamiṣyāmi. | I will not go tomorrow. |

### Worked examples

`अहम् श्वः पठिष्यामि।` - `Aham śvaḥ paṭhiṣyāmi.` means “I will read tomorrow.” The future stem and first-person ending combine with tomorrow to describe a later activity of the speaker.

`सा श्वः पठिष्यति।` - `Sā śvaḥ paṭhiṣyati.` means “She will read tomorrow.” The third-person future does not acquire a feminine ending; the pronoun supplies the subject's gender.

## Lesson 20: Wanting to act and being able to act

### Build the pattern

You will express a desired action and an ability using infinitives. पठितुम् paṭhitum means to read, and गन्तुम् gantum means to go. इच्छामि icchāmi means I want, while इच्छति icchati means he or she wants. शक्नोमि śaknomi means I can, and शक्नोति śaknoti means he or she can. The infinitive stays unchanged while the finite verb carries person and number. The infinitive of go again differs from the present stem gaccha-, showing why a dictionary root and its derived forms should not be confused. Sanskrit can express ability and desire without importing an English infinitive marker as a separate word.

### Think before reading on

In I want to read and she wants to read, which part changes: the infinitive or the wanting verb?

The infinitive paṭhitum stays the same. Icchāmi becomes icchati to agree with the subject. Separating the action named by the infinitive from the finite predicate makes longer sentences easier to construct and interpret.

## Lesson 21: Connecting nouns and successive actions

### Build the pattern

You will join two participants with च ca and connect actions with a nonfinal form. Since two participants are involved, the combined subject can take dual agreement. पठित्वा paṭhitvā means having read, and गत्वा gatvā means having gone. These indeclinable forms introduce an action prior to the main verb when the subject is shared. The final finite verb supplies the main tense and person. Do not confuse joining two noun phrases with joining a prior action to a main event.

If a boy and a girl read together, should the final verb be plural merely because English and appears in the translation?

## Lesson 22: Reading connected sandhi without losing the words

### Build the pattern

You will connect a few previously separated word forms using external sandhi. Until now the course displayed grammatical word boundaries deliberately. Continuous Sanskrit normally changes sounds at many such boundaries. Final m before a consonant commonly becomes anusvāra, so aham paṭhāmi appears as ahaṃ paṭhāmi. Final aḥ before a voiced consonant often becomes o, so bālaḥ gacchati becomes bālo gacchati. Vowel combinations can also merge across words: na asti becomes nāsti. This lesson practises only these limited patterns and does not claim to cover all visarga or vowel sandhi. The grammatical roles remain those of the underlying words even when their visible endings change.

### Think before reading on

If bālaḥ becomes bālo before gacchati, has the boy stopped being nominative and become a different case?

No. The change is conditioned by the following sound, not by a new grammatical role. Recover the underlying nominative bālaḥ while reading the connected form. Sandhi affects the surface pronunciation and spelling without automatically changing syntax.

### Useful language in context

| Native script | Romanisation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| अहं पठामि। | Ahaṃ paṭhāmi. | Connect aham + paṭhāmi: I read. |
| बालो गच्छति। | Bālo gacchati. | Connect bālaḥ + gacchati: the boy goes. |
| नरो गच्छति। | Naro gacchati. | Connect naraḥ + gacchati: the man goes. |
| जलम् अस्ति। | Jalam asti. | Connect jalam + asti: there is water. |
| नास्ति। | Nāsti. | Connect na + asti: it is not present. |
| अहं फलं खादामि। | Ahaṃ phalaṃ khādāmi. | Connect aham + phalam + khādāmi: I eat fruit. |

### Worked examples

`अहं पठामि।` - `Ahaṃ paṭhāmi.` means “Connect aham + paṭhāmi: I read.” The final m becomes anusvāra before the following consonant, while the first-person pronoun and verb retain their grammatical roles.

`बालो गच्छति।` - `Bālo gacchati.` means “Connect bālaḥ + gacchati: the boy goes.” The aḥ ending becomes o before voiced g, but the underlying noun remains masculine nominative singular.

`नरो गच्छति।` - `Naro gacchati.` means “Connect naraḥ + gacchati: the man goes.” The same visarga environment applies to another regular masculine nominative, showing that the rule is phonological rather than noun-specific.

Do not apply aḥ to o everywhere. The following sound matters, and many environments behave differently. The examples isolate a safe introductory environment rather than a universal replacement rule.

## Lesson 23: Relative statements and simple conditions

### Build the pattern

You will relate a person to an action and make a plan conditional. यः yaḥ means who or the one who in masculine nominative singular, and सः saḥ can introduce the corresponding main-clause referent. या yā and सा sā provide feminine forms. Thus the person who reads can be identified and then described in a second clause. यदि yadi means if and introduces a condition; the main clause states what follows. A relative identifies a participant; a condition presents a circumstance. Examples resume the separated teaching convention.

### Think before reading on

Does the woman who reads describe the same relationship as if the woman reads?

No. A relative expression identifies or characterises the woman; an if-clause makes reading a condition for another statement. Sanskrit distinguishes these jobs.
