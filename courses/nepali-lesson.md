# Nepali: A pattern-first foundation

This course teaches broadly standard conversational Nepali as used in Nepal, written in Devanagari. Everyday forms are distinguished from more formal written alternatives where that affects a beginner's choice. Nepali-speaking communities also live outside Nepal, and regional vocabulary and pronunciation vary. The examples do not represent every regional variety.

Editorial status: original AI-assisted draft; fluent-speaker review pending.

Romanisation is a reading aid, not a replacement alphabet. We use ā for आ, ī and ū to preserve conventional written vowel distinctions, ṭ and ḍ for retroflex consonants, ch for च, chh for छ, and sh for श and ष. A following h in kh, gh, th, dh, ph, and bh marks aspiration. A tilde marks a nasal vowel, as in mã. For nasal vowels, ĩ and ũ replace written-length distinctions to keep the reading aid consistent. Written long and short i/u do not reliably imply a length contrast in ordinary Nepali speech. Final inherent vowels are omitted where the everyday pronunciation normally omits them. Learn the full word beside its spelling rather than mechanically pronouncing every letter.

## Lesson 1: Introduce yourself with a complete sentence

### Outcome and first pattern

You will identify yourself and name an object. English puts “am” between a person and an identity. Nepali normally leaves this linking verb until the end: person, identity, linking verb. In `म विद्यार्थी हुँ।` (ma vidyārthī hũ), the words mean “I, student, am.” There is no separate word corresponding to English “a” in this sentence. You can already replace the identity without rebuilding the whole sentence.

### Think before reading on

If `म` means “I” and `नेपाली` means “Nepali,” where should the linking verb go when you say “I am Nepali”? Say the pieces in an order you think will work. Then compare: `म नेपाली हुँ।` (ma nepālī hũ). The identity changes; the first-person form `हुँ` remains. Do not infer that every English “is” will use the same Nepali word. Today we are identifying, and the next lesson will locate things.

### Small working vocabulary

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| म | ma | I |
| विद्यार्थी | vidyārthī | student |
| नेपाली | nepālī | Nepali |
| शिक्षक | shikshak | teacher |
| यो | yo | this |
| किताब | kitāb | book |
| नाम | nām | name |
| नमस्ते | namaste | respectful greeting |

### Build and inspect

`यो किताब हो।` (yo kitāb ho) means “This is a book.” Its subject is a thing being identified, so it takes `हो`, not the first-person `हुँ`. `म शिक्षक हुँ।` (ma shikshak hũ) means “I am a teacher.” The noun `शिक्षक` is conventionally masculine or generic; `शिक्षिका` (shikshikā) specifically identifies a female teacher. There is no requirement to add a masculine ending to every first-person sentence.

`मेरो नाम रीता हो।` (mero nām rītā ho) means “My name is Rita.” For now, take `मेरो नाम` as the useful chunk “my name.” Notice that the grammatical subject is the name, so the ending is `हो` even though the speaker talks about herself. “My” does not turn every sentence into an “I” sentence.

### Common trap

Do not insert a Nepali article just because the English translation contains “a.” Also keep `हुँ` nasal: the vowel has nasal resonance; it is not simply the English word “who.” The script's nasal mark is meaningful, so retain it when typing.

### Practice

Build one Nepali sentence for each prompt.

1. I am a student.
2. This is a book.
3. I am Nepali.
4. My name is Rita.

***Answers:***

1. `म विद्यार्थी हुँ।` - `Ma vidyārthī hũ.`
2. `यो किताब हो।` - `Yo kitāb ho.`
3. `म नेपाली हुँ।` - `Ma nepālī hũ.`
4. `मेरो नाम रीता हो।` - `Mero nām Rītā ho.`

### Why these answers work

The first and third answers describe the speaker's identity and therefore keep the first-person ending. The second identifies the demonstrative “this.” The fourth identifies a name. Compare the subjects before comparing the English verbs: English “am” and “is” give a useful initial clue, but the Nepali subject is what selects the form.

### Retrieve and recombine

Introduce yourself using your own name, then point to a book and identify it. Finally, imagine you are introducing yourself as a teacher. Which words change, and which ending returns? Say all three without looking at the table.

## Lesson 2: Say where something is

### Outcome and contrast

You will locate a person or object and distinguish location from identity. `किताब यहाँ छ।` (kitāb yahã chha) means “The book is here.” The sentence ends in `छ`, the ordinary third-person form for existence, location, and many descriptions. The identity sentence `यो किताब हो।` tells us what something is. The location sentence tells us where a book is. These are different jobs, even though English uses “is” for both.

### Think before reading on

You know that a book is a book. A friend now asks where it is. Would an identity statement give the missing information? Before looking below, build “The book is there” using the new location word in the table. The result is `किताब त्यहाँ छ।` (kitāb tyahã chha). The noun remains the same, and a location takes the place of an identity.

### Places and positions

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| यहाँ | yahã | here |
| त्यहाँ | tyahã | there |
| कहाँ | kahã | where |
| घर | ghar | home; house |
| कोठा | koṭhā | room |
| विद्यालय | vidyālaya | school |
| मा | mā | in; at; on |
| छु | chhu | am, for first-person state/location |

### Attach a location

`मा` follows the place and is normally attached in writing: `घरमा` (gharmā), “at home,” and `कोठामा` (koṭhāmā), “in the room.” English puts “in” before its noun; Nepali puts this marker after. `किताब कोठामा छ।` (kitāb koṭhāmā chha) is “The book is in the room.” `म घरमा छु।` (ma gharmā chhu) is “I am at home.” The first-person subject changes the final form to `छु`.

A question can keep its ordinary order: `किताब कहाँ छ?` (kitāb kahã chha), “Where is the book?” Replace the unknown location with `कहाँ`. No extra equivalent of English “do” is needed. A natural short answer is `कोठामा।` (koṭhāmā), “In the room.” Our practice uses complete sentences so the location marker and ending stay visible together.

### Sound and common trap

The nasal ending in `यहाँ`, `त्यहाँ`, and `कहाँ` belongs to the vowel. It is not a separate final “n” syllable. Keep `म` and `मा` distinct: the first is “I,” and the second is a location marker. A familiar-looking script does not justify replacing Nepali `छु` with Hindi `हूँ` in a location sentence.

### Practice

Use the location pattern, including the final verb.

1. I am at home.
2. The book is here.
3. The book is in the room.
4. Where is the book?

***Answers:***

1. `म घरमा छु।` - `Ma gharmā chhu.`
2. `किताब यहाँ छ।` - `Kitāb yahã chha.`
3. `किताब कोठामा छ।` - `Kitāb koṭhāmā chha.`
4. `किताब कहाँ छ?` - `Kitāb kahã chha?`

### Why these answers work

The first answer combines a first-person subject with `छु`. The remaining answers have a singular book as subject and use `छ`. A location question changes the information slot, not the sentence into English question order. The place marker attaches to a place noun but is unnecessary after “here,” “there,” or “where.”

### Retrieve and recombine

Recall one identity sentence from Lesson 1 and one location sentence from today. Now locate yourself at school, using `विद्यालयमा` (vidyālayamā). Explain aloud why identifying yourself as a student and locating yourself at school need different final forms.

## Lesson 3: Address people respectfully

### Outcome and social choice

You will ask a respectful location question and recognise a familiar alternative. Nepali distinguishes relationships in pronouns and verbs. `तपाईं` is a useful respectful “you” for a new adult acquaintance. `तिमी` is familiar and suits relationships where that familiarity is established. It is not a universally interchangeable shorter version. The very intimate or low-respect `तँ` exists, but this course does not make it your default production form.

### Think before reading on

Would replacing “I” with respectful “you” leave `छु` unchanged? Try predicting the location sentence before you read the model. The respectful form is `तपाईं कहाँ हुनुहुन्छ?` (tapāĩ kahã hunuhunchha), “Where are you?” The longer verb belongs to the respectful pattern. Politeness is carried by more than a greeting at the beginning.

### People and useful forms

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| तपाईं | tapāĩ | you, respectful |
| तिमी | timī | you, familiar |
| उहाँ | uhã | he/she, respectful |
| हामी | hāmī | we |
| छौ | chhau | are, with familiar you |
| छौँ | chhaũ | are, with we |
| हुनुहुन्छ | hunuhunchha | respectful is/are |
| साथी | sāthī | friend |

### Keep the relationship consistent

`तिमी घरमा छौ।` (timī gharmā chhau) means “You are at home,” addressed familiarly. `तपाईं घरमा हुनुहुन्छ।` (tapāĩ gharmā hunuhunchha) communicates the same location respectfully. `उहाँ यहाँ हुनुहुन्छ।` (uhã yahã hunuhunchha) means “He or she is here,” respectfully. The respectful third-person pronoun does not specify gender.

For a group including yourself, say `हामी यहाँ छौँ।` (hāmī yahã chhaũ), “We are here.” Compare `छौ` and `छौँ`: the written nasal mark separates familiar “you are” from “we are.” They look close, but their participants differ. A copied form can therefore change who the sentence concerns.

For polite identity questions, the established expression `तपाईं विद्यार्थी हो?` (tapāĩ vidyārthī ho) means “Are you a student?” Identity `हो` and the location/state `हुनुहुन्छ` patterns should be learned in their actual contexts. Do not force the long location form into every question merely because the addressee is respected.

### Common trap

Do not combine `तपाईं` with the familiar ending `छौ`. Nor should you assume that a respectful third-person expression refers to several people. Honorific grammar and grammatical number interact, but respect can apply to a single person.

### Practice

Use the relationship specified in each prompt.

1. Where are you? (respectful)
2. You are at home. (familiar)
3. We are here.
4. She is here. (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `तपाईं कहाँ हुनुहुन्छ?` - `Tapāĩ kahã hunuhunchha?`
2. `तिमी घरमा छौ।` - `Timī gharmā chhau.`
3. `हामी यहाँ छौँ।` - `Hāmī yahã chhaũ.`
4. `उहाँ यहाँ हुनुहुन्छ।` - `Uhã yahã hunuhunchha.`

### Why these answers work

The first and fourth share the respectful verb, although one addresses a person and the other refers to a person. The second belongs to an established familiar relationship. The third includes the speaker and retains nasalisation. The English word “she” in the prompt is supplied by context, not encoded in `उहाँ` itself.

### Retrieve and recombine

Ask a new teacher where they are, then ask a close friend the same question. Next, say that you and a friend are at school. Before speaking each time, choose the relationship and participants; only then build the sentence.

## Lesson 4: Ask yes-or-no and information questions

### Outcome and question choices

You will ask about identity, location, and an unknown object. In an ordinary spoken yes-or-no question, intonation can turn a statement into a question without rearranging it. `यो किताब हो?` (yo kitāb ho) means “Is this a book?” A written question mark reflects the questioning use. An initial `के` can also mark a yes-or-no question, but `के` has another important job: it can mean “what.”

### Think before reading on

Compare “Is this a book?” with “What is this?” In the first question, you offer a possible answer. In the second, you do not yet know the identity. Which word should occupy the unknown identity slot? `यो के हो?` (yo ke ho) asks “What is this?” The question word replaces “book.” It is not necessary to add another English-style helping verb.

### Question toolkit

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| के | ke | what; yes-or-no question marker |
| को | ko | who |
| कहाँ | kahã | where |
| कहिले | kahile | when |
| किन | kina | why |
| कसरी | kasarī | how |
| हो | ho | yes, confirming an identity statement |
| छ | chha | there is; yes, confirming existence |

### Answer the question that was asked

`उहाँ को हो?` (uhã ko ho) means “Who is he or she?” respectfully. A person's name can fill the identity slot: `उहाँ रीता हो।` (uhã rītā ho), “She is Rita.” `के किताब यहाँ छ?` (ke kitāb yahã chha) asks whether the book is here. You can answer `छ।` (chha), confirming its presence, or give the fuller `किताब यहाँ छ।`

A general conversational acknowledgment such as “yes” does not always carry the same information as echoing the relevant verb. Learn `हो` for confirmation of identity and `छ` for confirmation of existence in these examples. Later you will learn matching negative responses. It is better to connect the short response to the sentence than to treat one word as a universal translation of English “yes.”

Question words need not all come at the beginning. Nepali commonly leaves them in the position of the missing information. `कहाँ` occupies a location slot; `को` occupies a person-identity slot. Emphasis can affect natural word order, but these neutral patterns provide a stable starting point.

### Common trap

Do not read every `के` as “what.” In `के यो किताब हो?`, it introduces a yes-or-no question. In `यो के हो?`, it asks for the identity. Position and the rest of the sentence make the difference visible.

### Practice

Build the indicated question or short response.

1. What is this?
2. Is the book here? (begin with ke)
3. Who is she? (respectful)
4. Confirm that the book is here with the short existence response.

***Answers:***

1. `यो के हो?` - `Yo ke ho?`
2. `के किताब यहाँ छ?` - `Ke kitāb yahã chha?`
3. `उहाँ को हो?` - `Uhã ko ho?`
4. `छ।` - `Chha.`

### Why these answers work

The first and third ask for an identity rather than confirmation. The second offers a proposition for confirmation. The final response echoes its existence verb. These are different conversational tasks even though English may introduce several of them with “is.”

### Retrieve and recombine

Point to an unfamiliar object and ask what it is. Then ask whether a known book is in the room. Finally, explain which question could sensibly receive just `छ` as a response.

## Lesson 5: Negate identity and existence

### Outcome and two negatives

You will reject a mistaken identification and say that something is absent. Nepali has different negative forms for the two patterns already learned. Identity `हो` becomes `होइन` (hoina). Existence or ordinary third-person state `छ` becomes `छैन` (chhaina). This is not a case of putting one free-standing English “not” in front of every verb. The negative belongs to the particular construction.

### Think before reading on

Someone points to a notebook and calls it a book. Someone else looks for a book in an empty room. Are those errors about the same kind of information? Try the two negatives. `यो किताब होइन।` (yo kitāb hoina) says “This is not a book.” `कोठामा किताब छैन।` (koṭhāmā kitāb chhaina) says “There is no book in the room.” The second denies existence at a location, not the category of an object.

### Useful contrasts

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| होइन | hoina | is not, for identity |
| छैन | chhaina | is not; there is not |
| छैनँ | chhainã | am not, state/location |
| छैनौँ | chhainaũ | are not, with we |
| हुनुहुन्न | hunuhunna | is/are not, respectful |
| कापी | kāpī | notebook |
| कलम | kalam | pen |
| आज | āja | today |

### Follow the subject

`म घरमा छैनँ।` (ma gharmā chhainã) means “I am not at home.” The nasalised first-person ending matters. `हामी घरमा छैनौँ।` (hāmī gharmā chhainaũ) means “We are not at home.” `उहाँ यहाँ हुनुहुन्न।` (uhã yahã hunuhunna) means “He or she is not here,” respectfully. The honorific does not disappear when the sentence becomes negative.

To correct an identification, you can use two simple clauses: `यो किताब होइन। यो कापी हो।` (yo kitāb hoina; yo kāpī ho), “This is not a book. This is a notebook.” The correction supplies the right identity after rejecting the wrong one. This is often more useful than a bare “no.” Similarly, after saying the book is not here, you can locate it elsewhere.

### Common trap

Do not use `होइन` to deny the presence of a pen in your room. You would be treating the statement as an identity claim. The distinction is about the kind of predicate, not about whether something is permanent or temporary. A permanent location still uses the location construction.

### Practice

Choose the negative required by the sentence.

1. This is not a book.
2. There is no pen here.
3. I am not at home.
4. She is not here. (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `यो किताब होइन।` - `Yo kitāb hoina.`
2. `यहाँ कलम छैन।` - `Yahã kalam chhaina.`
3. `म घरमा छैनँ।` - `Ma gharmā chhainã.`
4. `उहाँ यहाँ हुनुहुन्न।` - `Uhã yahã hunuhunna.`

### Why these answers work

The first denies an identity. The second denies existence and has “pen,” not the location, as its grammatical subject. The third requires a first-person form, and the fourth maintains respectful reference. In each case, the negative reflects information already present in the affirmative construction.

### Retrieve and recombine

Correct a friend who thinks you are at home: first deny the location, then say you are at school. Next, point to a notebook and correct a wrong identification. Keep the two negative families separate while changing the nouns.

## Lesson 6: Build everyday actions with objects

### Outcome and verb-last thinking

You will say what you eat, read, and do habitually. In `म किताब पढ्छु।` (ma kitāb paḍhchhu), “I read a book,” the object comes before the verb. The ordinary order is subject, object, verb. Unlike an identity sentence, an action sentence already has its own finite verb. You do not add a separate `हुँ` after `पढ्छु`.

### Think before reading on

English uses “I drink water.” If Nepali places the object before the action, where should “water” go? Now consider a vocabulary difference: Nepali often uses `खानु`, “to eat,” for drinking familiar beverages too. `म पानी खान्छु।` (ma pānī khānchhu) is a natural “I drink water.” Translating each English verb with one permanently fixed dictionary equivalent would miss this ordinary usage.

### Action vocabulary

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| खानु | khānu | to eat; drink |
| पढ्नु | paḍhnu | to read; study |
| गर्नु | garnu | to do |
| लेख्नु | lekhnu | to write |
| पानी | pānī | water |
| भात | bhāt | cooked rice |
| चिया | chiyā | tea |
| काम | kām | work |

### From a dictionary form to a sentence

The dictionary forms end in `नु`, but they are not ready-made “I” statements. Learn these first-person pairs: `खानु` becomes `खान्छु` (khānchhu); `पढ्नु` becomes `पढ्छु` (paḍhchhu); `गर्नु` becomes `गर्छु` (garchhu); `लेख्नु` becomes `लेख्छु` (lekhchhu). The surface stem is not obtained by mechanically adding the same letters to every full infinitive. Notice each complete pair.

`म काम गर्छु।` (ma kām garchhu) means “I work.” `म चिया खान्छु।` (ma chiyā khānchhu) means “I drink tea.” `म नेपाली पढ्छु।` (ma nepālī paḍhchhu) means “I study Nepali.” These forms can describe regular behaviour; context may also make them a present or future commitment. To explicitly describe an action currently in progress, we will introduce a separate construction.

A bare object like `किताब` need not announce whether the English translation should have “a” or “the.” The conversation supplies that information. Likewise, do not add an object marker to every object just because another language does. We will introduce person and recipient marking when it is useful.

### Common trap

Do not build `म पढ्नु` as a complete ordinary statement meaning “I read.” The infinitive names the action, while `पढ्छु` makes the first-person statement. Also preserve aspiration in `पढ्नु`: the `ḍh` sound is different from plain `ḍ`.

### Practice

Use first-person everyday action forms.

1. I read a book.
2. I drink tea.
3. I work.
4. I study Nepali.

***Answers:***

1. `म किताब पढ्छु।` - `Ma kitāb paḍhchhu.`
2. `म चिया खान्छु।` - `Ma chiyā khānchhu.`
3. `म काम गर्छु।` - `Ma kām garchhu.`
4. `म नेपाली पढ्छु।` - `Ma nepālī paḍhchhu.`

### Why these answers work

Every answer places its object or complement before a conjugated action. The second uses the Nepali beverage use of “eat.” The first and fourth share a verb whose English meaning shifts between reading and studying according to the object. No additional identity copula belongs after these finite forms.

### Retrieve and recombine

Replace tea with water, then replace the book with a notebook. Finally, say where you study Nepali by bringing in `घरमा` from Lesson 2. Keep the new location before the final action.

## Lesson 7: Ask about other people's routines

### Outcome and agreement

You will ask what a respected person does and describe a shared routine. The action ending responds to the subject and the relationship, just as location verbs did. `तपाईं के गर्नुहुन्छ?` (tapāĩ ke garnuhunchha) means “What do you do?” It can ask about occupation or the activity under discussion. Context decides how broadly to interpret “do.”

### Think before reading on

You can say `म काम गर्छु।` If you now include a friend and mean “we work,” should the first-person singular ending remain? Compare `हामी काम गर्छौँ।` (hāmī kām garchhaũ). The group includes the speaker, and the ending is plural first person. It is neither the familiar “you” form nor the respectful form.

### Useful action forms

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| गर्छु | garchhu | I do |
| गर्छौँ | garchhaũ | we do |
| गर्छौ | garchhau | you do, familiar |
| गर्नुहुन्छ | garnuhunchha | you/he/she does, respectful |
| पढ्नुहुन्छ | paḍhnuhunchha | reads/studies, respectful |
| खानुहुन्छ | khānuhunchha | eats/drinks, respectful |
| बिहान | bihān | morning |
| हरेक दिन | harek din | every day |

### Work through the choices

`तपाईं नेपाली पढ्नुहुन्छ।` (tapāĩ nepālī paḍhnuhunchha) means “You study Nepali,” respectfully. `उहाँ बिहान चिया खानुहुन्छ।` (uhã bihān chiyā khānuhunchha) means “He or she drinks tea in the morning,” respectfully. The time expression comes before the final action and can precede the object. We are not translating English prepositions word for word: `बिहान` works as a time expression here without an added “in.”

`तिमी के गर्छौ?` (timī ke garchhau) is the familiar version of “What do you do?” Comparing that with `तपाईं के गर्नुहुन्छ?` shows two coordinated changes. The pronoun changes, and the verb changes. If you only change the pronoun, the resulting mixture no longer matches the model's relationship.

`हामी हरेक दिन पढ्छौँ।` (hāmī harek din paḍhchhaũ) means “We study every day.” You can omit a recoverable object when the conversation already establishes the subject being studied. Subject pronouns can also be omitted in clear contexts, but keeping them initially lets you see the agreement choices.

### Common trap

Respectful forms are not evidence that several people are doing the action. `उहाँ` can refer to one respected teacher. Conversely, `हामी` genuinely includes more than the speaker alone in these examples. Do not collapse politeness and number into one English-style plural rule.

### Practice

Use the stated level of address.

1. What do you do? (respectful)
2. We work.
3. You study Nepali. (respectful)
4. What do you do? (familiar)

***Answers:***

1. `तपाईं के गर्नुहुन्छ?` - `Tapāĩ ke garnuhunchha?`
2. `हामी काम गर्छौँ।` - `Hāmī kām garchhaũ.`
3. `तपाईं नेपाली पढ्नुहुन्छ।` - `Tapāĩ nepālī paḍhnuhunchha.`
4. `तिमी के गर्छौ?` - `Timī ke garchhau?`

### Why these answers work

The respectful questions and statements use the extended `नुहुन्छ` form. The shared routine uses a nasalised first-person plural ending. The familiar question has its own shorter ending. “What” stays where the missing activity complement belongs, immediately before the action in these short examples.

### Retrieve and recombine

Ask a respected acquaintance whether they drink tea in the morning. Then answer on behalf of yourself and a friend that you study every day. Identify the relationship before assembling each verb.

## Lesson 8: Say what you do not do

### Outcome and action negatives

You will deny a routine and ask a negative-free question to clarify someone's habits. Action negation introduces another family of forms. `म चिया खाँदिनँ।` (ma chiyā khãdinã) means “I do not drink tea.” This does not use the identity negative `होइन` or simply append the existence negative `छैन` to a positive finite verb. Learn the negative action together with its subject.

### Think before reading on

If “I work” is `म काम गर्छु`, could you express its negative by saying “I work is not”? That would combine two unrelated finite structures. The compact first-person negative is `म काम गर्दिनँ।` (ma kām gardinã). Compare `गर्छु` and `गर्दिनँ` as complete affirmative and negative choices.

### Negative toolkit

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| गर्दिनँ | gardinã | I do not do |
| पढ्दिनँ | paḍhdinã | I do not read/study |
| खाँदिनँ | khãdinã | I do not eat/drink |
| गर्नुहुन्न | garnuhunna | does not do, respectful |
| पढ्नुहुन्न | paḍhnuhunna | does not read, respectful |
| खानुहुन्न | khānuhunna | does not eat/drink, respectful |
| कफी | kaphī | coffee |
| राति | rāti | at night |

### Contrast habits carefully

`म राति काम गर्दिनँ।` (ma rāti kām gardinã) means “I do not work at night.” This denies work in the specified period, not necessarily work at every time. `म कफी खाँदिनँ।` (ma kaphī khãdinã) means “I do not drink coffee.” You could still drink tea. The scope of the negative is clarified by the other words in the sentence and the conversation.

`उहाँ कफी खानुहुन्न।` (uhã kaphī khānuhunna) means “He or she does not drink coffee,” respectfully. The final respectful negative can also appear in other contexts, including prohibition; here it is a statement about a person's routine. Do not infer a universal meaning from the ending alone without looking at the whole clause.

To ask without assuming the answer, use the positive question: `तपाईं कफी खानुहुन्छ?` (tapāĩ kaphī khānuhunchha), “Do you drink coffee?” A complete reply `म कफी खाँदिनँ` makes the intended denial explicit. A simple English “no” might otherwise hide which Nepali construction you need.

### Common trap

Nasalisation is visible in both the stem and ending of `खाँदिनँ`. Do not reduce it to the spelling of a familiar Hindi form. Nepali first-person negatives also vary in casual spelling and speech, but this course preserves the standard written distinctions shown in the keys.

### Practice

Negate the specified routine.

1. I do not work.
2. I do not drink coffee.
3. I do not read at night.
4. She does not drink tea. (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `म काम गर्दिनँ।` - `Ma kām gardinã.`
2. `म कफी खाँदिनँ।` - `Ma kaphī khãdinã.`
3. `म राति पढ्दिनँ।` - `Ma rāti paḍhdinã.`
4. `उहाँ चिया खानुहुन्न।` - `Uhã chiyā khānuhunna.`

### Why these answers work

The first three are first-person singular action negatives. The fourth keeps respectful reference. Time and object information remain before the final verb. None of the answers denies an identity or the existence of an object, so the earlier negatives `होइन` and `छैन` are not the relevant forms here.

### Retrieve and recombine

Name one drink you consume and one you do not. Then make the same contrast about reading in the morning and at night. Keep your positive and negative endings separate rather than adding a single memorised “not.”

## Lesson 9: Go somewhere and come from somewhere

### Outcome and direction

You will describe a destination and a starting point. `म विद्यालय जान्छु।` (ma vidyālaya jānchhu) means “I go to school.” Nepali can use a bare destination with a motion verb in common expressions. A location marker is not automatically required wherever English uses “to.” `म घर जान्छु।` (ma ghar jānchhu), “I go home,” is another useful example.

### Think before reading on

Compare being at home with going home. Which piece tells your listener whether you are already there or moving toward it? In `म घरमा छु`, the final verb locates you. In `म घर जान्छु`, the motion verb describes going. A dictionary list of places cannot make this distinction on its own; the whole construction does the work.

### Movement vocabulary

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| जानु | jānu | to go |
| आउनु | āunu | to come |
| जान्छु | jānchhu | I go |
| आउँछु | āũchhu | I come |
| बाट | bāṭa | from; by way of |
| बजार | bajār | market |
| बस | bas | bus |
| पैदल | paidal | on foot |

### Starting point and means

`म बजारबाट आउँछु।` (ma bajārbāṭa āũchhu) means “I come from the market.” The marker attaches after the place: `बजारबाट`. `म बसबाट जान्छु।` (ma basbāṭa jānchhu) means “I go by bus.” Here the same marker introduces means of transport. Context and the noun help distinguish a source from a means. That is normal grammatical flexibility, not an error in the dictionary.

`म पैदल जान्छु।` (ma paidal jānchhu) means “I go on foot.” Do not add an English-style preposition to `पैदल`. `तपाईं कहाँ जानुहुन्छ?` (tapāĩ kahã jānuhunchha) asks respectfully “Where do you go?” In an immediate context it can also ask where someone is going. A later lesson will give an explicitly progressive form.

Coming and going depend on a conversational viewpoint. “Come” usually presents movement toward the relevant location of the speaker or another established centre. It is not simply a synonym for any motion back. First decide the destination and viewpoint, then choose the verb.

### Common trap

Do not interpret every `बाट` as “from” and produce “from bus” in your mental English. Conversely, a market marked with `बाट` is a starting point here, not automatically transport. Read a marker with the noun and the main verb that surround it.

### Practice

Build a motion sentence with the supplied meaning.

1. I go home.
2. I come from the market.
3. I go by bus.
4. Where do you go? (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `म घर जान्छु।` - `Ma ghar jānchhu.`
2. `म बजारबाट आउँछु।` - `Ma bajārbāṭa āũchhu.`
3. `म बसबाट जान्छु।` - `Ma basbāṭa jānchhu.`
4. `तपाईं कहाँ जानुहुन्छ?` - `Tapāĩ kahã jānuhunchha?`

### Why these answers work

The first uses a common bare destination. The next two use `बाट` for two related but distinct roles. The respectful question changes the verb as well as the pronoun. First-person `आउँछु` preserves a nasal vowel that the romanisation shows explicitly.

### Retrieve and recombine

Say that you go to the market on foot. Next, say that you come from school. Finally, contrast “I am at home” and “I go home” without translating the English words one at a time.

## Lesson 10: Place routines on a simple timeline

### Outcome and time expressions

You will say when a routine happens and distinguish today, yesterday, and tomorrow. Nepali has separate common words `हिजो` for yesterday and `भोलि` for tomorrow. A learner who knows Hindi should not assume that a single familiar word must cover both directions. Let the Nepali time word establish the day, then choose a verb construction appropriate to the event.

### Think before reading on

You know `म पढ्छु`, “I study.” How could you turn it into an explicit morning routine without changing the person? Place `बिहान` before the action: `म बिहान पढ्छु।` (ma bihān paḍhchhu). The time expression adds information; it does not itself require a new subject ending.

### A small time map

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| आज | āja | today |
| हिजो | hijo | yesterday |
| भोलि | bholi | tomorrow |
| अहिले | ahile | now |
| बिहान | bihān | morning |
| दिउँसो | diũso | daytime; afternoon |
| बेलुका | belukā | evening |
| बजे | baje | o'clock, after a number |
| सात | sāt | seven |
| आठ | āṭh | eight |

### Time comes before the final action

`म सात बजे पढ्छु।` (ma sāt baje paḍhchhu) means “I study at seven.” `हामी बिहान काम गर्छौँ।` (hāmī bihān kām garchhaũ) means “We work in the morning.” A time phrase can also go first: `बिहान म पढ्छु।` (bihān ma paḍhchhu). That gives the morning a stronger framing role. Both keep the main action at the end.

`तपाईं कहिले आउनुहुन्छ?` (tapāĩ kahile āunuhunchha) asks “When do you come?” or, in a plan, “When will you come?” The ordinary nonpast can refer to a scheduled or intended future event when the context supports it. `म भोलि आउँछु।` (ma bholi āũchhu) is “I will come tomorrow.” Do not make an absolute rule that every English future must contain a separate Nepali future suffix.

For a precise appointment, “at seven” may need morning or evening clarification. `बिहान सात बजे` means “at seven in the morning.” Numbers beyond those in the table can be learned gradually; a foundation sentence remains useful before you know an entire clock vocabulary.

### Common trap

Do not attach a present habitual form to `हिजो` and assume the time word alone has made a normal past-tense sentence. Nepali has past forms, which we will learn later. Today's future use of the nonpast does not license arbitrary tense substitution in every direction.

### Practice

Use the time expression indicated.

1. I study in the morning.
2. I study at seven.
3. I will come tomorrow.
4. When will you come? (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `म बिहान पढ्छु।` - `Ma bihān paḍhchhu.`
2. `म सात बजे पढ्छु।` - `Ma sāt baje paḍhchhu.`
3. `म भोलि आउँछु।` - `Ma bholi āũchhu.`
4. `तपाईं कहिले आउनुहुन्छ?` - `Tapāĩ kahile āunuhunchha?`

### Why these answers work

The first two add a time phrase to the established first-person action. The third uses tomorrow to establish a future plan, and the fourth asks for that missing time respectfully. Word order remains useful without a separate English-style “will.”

### Retrieve and recombine

Arrange a tomorrow-morning visit at eight. Say one sentence with the day and time, then ask a respected friend when they will come. Retrieve the nasal vowel in “I come” and the respectful verb in the question.

## Lesson 11: Describe what is happening now

### Outcome and ongoing action

You will distinguish an action in progress from a routine. Nepali commonly forms an explicit progressive with a verb in `दै` plus the state auxiliary already introduced. `म पढ्दै छु।` (ma paḍhdai chhu) means “I am reading/studying.” Compare `म पढ्छु`, which can state a routine. The progressive presents the activity as unfolding at the relevant time.

### Think before reading on

A friend asks what you are doing at this moment. Would “I study every day” answer the immediate question? Build an ongoing statement using “read.” The participial form `पढ्दै` names the ongoing action, and `छु` supplies the first-person present auxiliary. Both pieces contribute something; neither should be discarded in the complete model.

### Ongoing forms

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| पढ्दै | paḍhdai | reading; studying |
| गर्दै | gardai | doing |
| खाँदै | khãdai | eating; drinking |
| जाँदै | jãdai | going |
| आउँदै | āũdai | coming |
| लेख्दै | lekhdai | writing |
| अहिले | ahile | now |
| पत्र | patra | letter |

### Combine with a known auxiliary

`म अहिले पत्र लेख्दै छु।` (ma ahile patra lekhdai chhu) means “I am writing a letter now.” The object still precedes the action. `हामी काम गर्दै छौँ।` (hāmī kām gardai chhaũ) means “We are working.” The action form stays `गर्दै`; the auxiliary carries first-person plural agreement.

`तपाईं के गर्दै हुनुहुन्छ?` (tapāĩ ke gardai hunuhunchha) asks respectfully “What are you doing?” `उहाँ चिया खाँदै हुनुहुन्छ।` (uhã chiyā khãdai hunuhunchha) says “He or she is drinking tea,” respectfully. This is a useful moment to retrieve Lesson 3: the same honorific auxiliary used for location now helps construct ongoing action.

You may encounter spellings that join the auxiliary more closely, such as `पढ्दैछु`. This course separates the parts to keep the pattern visible. That editorial spacing choice does not create a new tense. Learners should recognise the construction in continuous running text as well as in carefully spaced lessons.

### Common trap

The `दै` form is not a universal translation of English words ending in “ing.” English “Reading is useful” treats reading as an activity noun, which calls for another Nepali structure. Here the progressive means that someone is engaged in an action at a time. Also preserve stem nasalisation in `खाँदै` and `जाँदै`.

### Practice

Describe an action currently in progress.

1. I am reading a book.
2. We are working.
3. What are you doing? (respectful)
4. She is drinking tea. (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `म किताब पढ्दै छु।` - `Ma kitāb paḍhdai chhu.`
2. `हामी काम गर्दै छौँ।` - `Hāmī kām gardai chhaũ.`
3. `तपाईं के गर्दै हुनुहुन्छ?` - `Tapāĩ ke gardai hunuhunchha?`
4. `उहाँ चिया खाँदै हुनुहुन्छ।` - `Uhã chiyā khãdai hunuhunchha.`

### Why these answers work

The progressive action form and auxiliary divide the grammatical work. The action tells us what is in progress, while the auxiliary identifies the subject relationship. The first two differ in number; the last two maintain respect. Objects and question words still belong before the final verb group.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe yourself going to the market right now, then give your usual morning routine. Explain which sentence presents an unfolding event. Finally, change your progressive sentence to one about a respected teacher.

## Lesson 12: Make a polite request

### Outcome and direct usefulness

You will ask someone to sit, speak, or give you an item politely. A request is not merely a statement with a respectful pronoun. Nepali has imperative forms, including the broadly useful polite ending seen in `बस्नुहोस्` (basnuhos), “Please sit.” The addressee is often understood, so the pronoun can be omitted without making the request impolite.

### Think before reading on

Compare telling someone “You sit here” as a description with inviting them to sit here. Which sentence purpose is needed at a meeting? `यहाँ बस्नुहोस्।` (yahã basnuhos) is an invitation or request to sit here. The imperative replaces a statement ending; you do not add it after a complete present-tense verb.

### Request forms

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| बस्नुहोस् | basnuhos | please sit/stay |
| आउनुहोस् | āunuhos | please come |
| जानुहोस् | jānuhos | please go |
| भन्नुहोस् | bhannuhos | please say/tell |
| दिनुहोस् | dinuhos | please give |
| बोल्नुहोस् | bolnuhos | please speak |
| बिस्तारै | bistārai | slowly; gently |
| फेरि | pheri | again |

### Put the requested action last

`बिस्तारै बोल्नुहोस्।` (bistārai bolnuhos) means “Please speak slowly.” `फेरि भन्नुहोस्।` (pheri bhannuhos) means “Please say it again.” These are useful repair tools from the beginning of real conversation. They let you ask for a manageable repetition instead of pretending to understand.

`पानी दिनुहोस्।` (pānī dinuhos) means “Please give me water,” with the recipient recoverable from context. For an explicit recipient, use the chunk `मलाई` (malāī), “to me”: `मलाई पानी दिनुहोस्।` (malāī pānī dinuhos). We will examine that marker next. The water remains the thing given, and the recipient is a separate participant.

A separate `कृपया` (kripayā), “please,” may be added, particularly in formal notices or careful requests. The polite imperative itself already carries a respectful request form, so repeating the English idea of “please” is not mechanically necessary. Tone of voice and the situation still affect how a request feels.

### Common trap

Do not use an infinitive such as `बस्नु` alone as your default polite request just because a dictionary translates it “to sit.” Nor should you assume `बस्नु` always means sitting: it also means staying or residing in other constructions. The chair, location, and conversation help select the meaning.

### Practice

Make one polite request per prompt.

1. Please sit here.
2. Please speak slowly.
3. Please say it again.
4. Please give me water. (use malāī)

***Answers:***

1. `यहाँ बस्नुहोस्।` - `Yahã basnuhos.`
2. `बिस्तारै बोल्नुहोस्।` - `Bistārai bolnuhos.`
3. `फेरि भन्नुहोस्।` - `Pheri bhannuhos.`
4. `मलाई पानी दिनुहोस्।` - `Malāī pānī dinuhos.`

### Why these answers work

Each answer uses a polite imperative instead of a descriptive present form. Location and manner come before the requested action. The fourth includes both a recipient and the item to be given, so it demonstrates a larger sentence without changing the central request pattern.

### Retrieve and recombine

Ask a visitor to come here and sit. Then ask for a pen instead of water. Finally, imagine that you missed a word: request repetition, followed by slower speech. Use separate short requests rather than an unlearned long sentence.

## Lesson 13: Mark a recipient and a person affected

### Outcome and participant roles

You will distinguish “I” from “to me” and build a giving sentence. `म` is the ordinary subject pronoun. `मलाई` marks a recipient or experiencer in several useful constructions. In `उहाँ मलाई किताब दिनुहुन्छ।` (uhã malāī kitāb dinuhunchha), “He or she gives me a book,” the giver, recipient, and thing given each have a different job.

### Think before reading on

In English, “I give her a book” contains two people. Which one is doing the giving, and which one receives the book? Nepali makes that recipient role visible with `लाई`. `म उहाँलाई किताब दिन्छु।` (ma uhãlāī kitāb dinchhu) means “I give him or her a book,” respectfully referring to the recipient. The main verb is first person because the giver is the speaker.

### People in marked forms

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| मलाई | malāī | to me; me |
| तपाईंलाई | tapāĩlāī | to you, respectful |
| उहाँलाई | uhãlāī | to him/her, respectful |
| हामीलाई | hāmīlāī | to us |
| साथीलाई | sāthīlāī | to a friend; the friend as object |
| दिनु | dinu | to give |
| दिन्छु | dinchhu | I give |
| भेट्नु | bheṭnu | to meet |

### Keep giver and recipient separate

`म साथीलाई कलम दिन्छु।` (ma sāthīlāī kalam dinchhu) means “I give a pen to a friend.” The pen is not marked with `लाई` in this ordinary example. The friend receives it. `तपाईं मलाई किताब दिनुहुन्छ।` (tapāĩ malāī kitāb dinuhunchha) means “You give me a book,” respectfully. Changing who gives requires changing the subject and final verb, not merely moving `लाई` around.

The same marker also appears with many specific human objects: `म उहाँलाई भेट्छु।` (ma uhãlāī bheṭchhu), “I meet him or her.” It does not mean “to” in every English translation. Think of grammatical roles rather than assigning it a single English preposition. The precise conditions for object marking extend beyond this foundation, so our production examples use clearly appropriate people and recipients.

A recipient's respectful pronoun does not automatically make the giver's verb respectful. In `म उहाँलाई किताब दिन्छु`, the respectful reference belongs to the recipient, while first-person `दिन्छु` agrees with the speaker. This helps prevent a common overextension of honorific forms.

### Common trap

Do not translate “me” as `म` in every position. Nepali distinguishes the unmarked subject from forms used after or with grammatical markers. Also avoid attaching `लाई` to every inanimate object merely to imitate English word order or another South Asian language.

### Practice

Identify the giver before choosing the verb.

1. I give a pen to a friend.
2. I give her a book. (respectful recipient)
3. You give me a book. (respectful you)
4. I meet her. (respectful reference)

***Answers:***

1. `म साथीलाई कलम दिन्छु।` - `Ma sāthīlāī kalam dinchhu.`
2. `म उहाँलाई किताब दिन्छु।` - `Ma uhãlāī kitāb dinchhu.`
3. `तपाईं मलाई किताब दिनुहुन्छ।` - `Tapāĩ malāī kitāb dinuhunchha.`
4. `म उहाँलाई भेट्छु।` - `Ma uhãlāī bheṭchhu.`

### Why these answers work

The marked people are recipients or specific human objects. The final verb follows the unmarked subject's person and respect level. The first two share a first-person giver, while the third has a respected addressee as giver. The fourth shows why `लाई` cannot be reduced to English “to.”

### Retrieve and recombine

Give a book to a friend in one sentence, then ask a respected person to give you a pen. Finally, describe meeting that person. Track which participant has `लाई` in each sentence.

## Lesson 14: Say what belongs to someone

### Outcome and ownership

You will identify your belongings and recognise agreement inside a possessive phrase. `मेरो किताब` (mero kitāb) means “my book.” This construction identifies an owner or relation, rather than saying that an object is currently available. The genitive forms related to `को` change with the following noun in relevant gender and number contexts.

### Think before reading on

In “my mother” and “my father,” does “my” tell you the speaker's gender? No. Likewise, the difference between Nepali `मेरी आमा` and `मेरो बुबा` belongs to the following noun, not to whether the owner is a woman or a man. The forms are `मेरी आमा` (merī āmā), “my mother,” and `मेरो बुबा` (mero bubā), “my father.”

### Possessive vocabulary

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| मेरो | mero | my, masculine/default singular |
| मेरी | merī | my, feminine singular |
| मेरा | merā | my, plural in agreeing uses |
| तपाईंको | tapāĩko | your, respectful default form |
| उहाँको | uhãko | his/her, respectful default form |
| आमा | āmā | mother |
| बुबा | bubā | father |
| घर | ghar | house; home |

### Identify a belonging

`यो मेरो कलम हो।` (yo mero kalam ho) means “This is my pen.” `यो तपाईंको किताब हो?` (yo tapāĩko kitāb ho) asks “Is this your book?” respectfully. `उहाँको घर यहाँ छ।` (uhãko ghar yahã chha) means “His or her house is here.” The possessive phrase can serve as the subject of a location sentence as well as the complement of an identity sentence.

A named owner can take the marker directly: `रीताको घर` (rītāko ghar), “Rita's house.” The owner's name does not require an English apostrophe. `रीताको घर यहाँ छ।` (rītāko ghar yahã chha) locates that house. `रीता` being a woman's name does not make the marker feminine; it is the possessed noun's grammatical behaviour that matters.

Nepali gender agreement is not identical to Hindi's system, and actual conversational practice varies, especially with inanimate nouns and plural modifiers. This course demonstrates clear kinship contrasts and stable default noun phrases rather than forcing a simplistic masculine/feminine label onto every object. Later reading will expose more agreement detail.

### Common trap

Do not choose `मेरी` because the speaker is female. A female speaker still says `मेरो बुबा`. Also distinguish `को` in possession from the question word `को`, “who.” Their spelling is the same, but one attaches to an owner and the other fills a missing person slot.

### Practice

Build the requested possessive phrase or sentence.

1. This is my pen.
2. Is this your book? (respectful)
3. Rita's house is here.
4. My mother. (a noun phrase)

***Answers:***

1. `यो मेरो कलम हो।` - `Yo mero kalam ho.`
2. `यो तपाईंको किताब हो?` - `Yo tapāĩko kitāb ho?`
3. `रीताको घर यहाँ छ।` - `Rītāko ghar yahã chha.`
4. `मेरी आमा` - `Merī āmā`

### Why these answers work

The first two identify an owner through a possessive phrase. The third uses a named owner as part of a location statement. The last is deliberately a phrase, not an incomplete attempted sentence: its feminine form relates to “mother.” Neither the pen's owner nor Rita controls that agreement choice.

### Retrieve and recombine

Identify a book as a friend's, then locate your own house. Finally, build “my father” and “my mother” side by side. Explain which noun determines the visible possessive difference.

## Lesson 15: Have something with you

### Outcome and possession as availability

You will say that you have an item and ask whether someone has it. Nepali often expresses possession using `सँग`, “with,” and an existence verb. `मसँग कलम छ।` (masãga kalam chha) means “I have a pen.” Literally, a pen exists with me. This is a different structure from “This is my pen,” which identifies ownership.

### Think before reading on

Suppose a borrowed pen is in your bag. Can you truthfully say you have a pen without claiming you own it? Yes. That is why the “with me” construction is useful. The subject controlling the ordinary existence verb is the pen, so `मसँग कलम छ` ends in `छ`, not first-person `छु`.

### Availability vocabulary

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| सँग | sãga | with |
| मसँग | masãga | with me; I have |
| तपाईंसँग | tapāĩsãga | with you, respectful |
| पैसा | paisā | money |
| समय | samaya | time |
| टिकट | ṭikaṭ | ticket |
| छैन | chhaina | there is not |
| पनि | pani | also; too |

### Presence, absence, and a question

`मसँग टिकट छ।` (masãga ṭikaṭ chha) means “I have a ticket.” `मसँग पैसा छैन।` (masãga paisā chhaina) means “I do not have money.” The negative is the existence negative from Lesson 5, because the sentence asserts or denies availability. It is not an ordinary first-person action negative.

`तपाईंसँग कलम छ?` (tapāĩsãga kalam chha) asks respectfully “Do you have a pen?” The respected possessor does not turn the pen into an honorific subject. Consequently, `छ` remains. `मसँग पनि कलम छ।` (masãga pani kalam chha) means “I also have a pen,” with `पनि` highlighting that possession is true of me too.

The construction extends naturally to resources such as time: `मसँग समय छैन।` (masãga samaya chhaina), “I do not have time.” For family relationships, other possessive and existential patterns can be preferred; do not assume every English “have” must be translated with `सँग`. English uses “have” for meals, illnesses, relatives, and experiences, which Nepali need not package together.

### Common trap

Do not choose `छु` just because an English translation begins “I have.” The Nepali “I” appears inside a marked phrase, while the available item is what exists. This is the same reason `मलाई` did not automatically control the giver's verb in Lesson 13.

### Practice

Use the with-plus-existence construction.

1. I have a pen.
2. I have a ticket.
3. I do not have money.
4. Do you have a pen? (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `मसँग कलम छ।` - `Masãga kalam chha.`
2. `मसँग टिकट छ।` - `Masãga ṭikaṭ chha.`
3. `मसँग पैसा छैन।` - `Masãga paisā chhaina.`
4. `तपाईंसँग कलम छ?` - `Tapāĩsãga kalam chha?`

### Why these answers work

The marker places the possessor in a “with” phrase. The available thing is paired with `छ`, or with `छैन` if absent. The final question is respectful through its possessor pronoun while retaining ordinary agreement for a pen. There is no need to invent a Nepali verb matching every English use of “have.”

### Retrieve and recombine

Say that you have a book but no pen using two short sentences. Then ask a respected colleague for a pen, using the request pattern from Lesson 12. Contrast availability with the claim that the pen belongs to you.

## Lesson 16: Describe an object and compare two choices

### Outcome and description

You will describe a room or object and make a simple comparison. A quality can come before a noun, as in `ठूलो घर` (ṭhūlo ghar), “a big house,” or act as the predicate with `छ`: `घर ठूलो छ।` (ghar ṭhūlo chha), “The house is big.” The difference between a phrase and a sentence matters. A descriptive noun phrase alone does not make the same assertion as a completed clause.

### Think before reading on

If you are pointing to two rooms and want to say one is bigger, does repeating “big” identify the comparison standard? Nepali introduces the standard with `भन्दा`: `यो कोठा त्यो कोठाभन्दा ठूलो छ।` (yo koṭhā tyo koṭhābhandā ṭhūlo chha), “This room is bigger than that room.” The adjective need not acquire an English “-er” ending.

### Description toolkit

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ठूलो | ṭhūlo | big |
| सानो | sāno | small |
| राम्रो | rāmro | good; nice |
| नयाँ | nayã | new |
| पुरानो | purāno | old, not new |
| महँगो | mahãgo | expensive |
| सस्तो | sasto | inexpensive |
| भन्दा | bhandā | than |
| त्यो | tyo | that |

### Work from quality to comparison

`यो किताब नयाँ छ।` (yo kitāb nayã chha) means “This book is new.” `त्यो घर पुरानो छ।` (tyo ghar purāno chha) means “That house is old.” These use descriptive `छ`, not identity `हो`. The distinction is not reducible to permanent versus temporary: an old building can stay old, yet it remains a description.

`यो किताब त्यो किताबभन्दा सस्तो छ।` (yo kitāb tyo kitābbhandā sasto chha) means “This book is cheaper than that book.” Read the comparison in chunks: this book; than that book; inexpensive; is. `भन्दा` follows the comparison standard. You do not have to move the adjective into English order to make the comparison understandable.

Some adjectives have agreeing forms, such as `सानो` and feminine `सानी` (sānī). Others, including `नयाँ`, do not follow the same visible `ओ` to `ई` pattern. The object descriptions here use stable beginner forms. Do not manufacture a feminine ending for every adjective solely from its English translation.

### Common trap

`पुरानो` usually contrasts with newness; it is not the general polite way to describe an elderly person. Vocabulary meaning changes with the kind of noun described. Also remember that “that” points to the second object, while `भन्दा` establishes the comparison relation.

### Practice

Describe or compare the specified objects.

1. The house is big.
2. This book is new.
3. That house is old.
4. This book is cheaper than that book.

***Answers:***

1. `घर ठूलो छ।` - `Ghar ṭhūlo chha.`
2. `यो किताब नयाँ छ।` - `Yo kitāb nayã chha.`
3. `त्यो घर पुरानो छ।` - `Tyo ghar purāno chha.`
4. `यो किताब त्यो किताबभन्दा सस्तो छ।` - `Yo kitāb tyo kitābbhandā sasto chha.`

### Why these answers work

The first three predicate a quality using `छ`. The fourth retains that descriptive pattern and adds a comparison standard marked with `भन्दा`. There is no separate comparative adjective ending. The longer phrase still leads to a single final existence/state verb.

### Retrieve and recombine

Compare two rooms for size, then two books for price. Next, identify one object as your book rather than describing its quality. Decide whether that last sentence belongs to the identity or descriptive pattern before choosing the verb.

## Lesson 17: Count items and ask a price

### Outcome and small quantities

You will request a small number of ordinary objects and ask how much something costs. Nepali often combines a number with a classifier appropriate to what is counted. For many ordinary objects, `वटा` is useful: `दुई वटा किताब` (duī vaṭā kitāb), “two books.” For people, `जना` is common: `तीन जना विद्यार्थी` (tīn janā vidyārthī), “three students.”

### Think before reading on

If an English noun becomes plural after “two,” must Nepali always add its plural marker after a number too? In the basic counting phrase, the number and classifier already make the quantity clear. `दुई वटा किताब` is sufficient. Do not automatically stack every marker that your English translation suggests.

### Counting and shopping vocabulary

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| एक | ek | one |
| दुई | duī | two |
| तीन | tīn | three |
| चार | chār | four |
| पाँच | pãch | five |
| वटा | vaṭā | classifier for many ordinary things |
| जना | janā | classifier for people |
| कति | kati | how much; how many |
| पर्छ | parchha | costs, in a price question |
| रुपैयाँ | rupaiyã | rupee; rupees |

### Quantity and price are different questions

`दुई वटा कलम दिनुहोस्।` (duī vaṭā kalam dinuhos) means “Please give me two pens.” It combines the count phrase with a request you already know. `यो कति पर्छ?` (yo kati parchha) asks “How much does this cost?” The price construction uses `पर्छ`, a form whose other meanings will be useful later.

For a simple quoted amount, say `पाँच रुपैयाँ।` (pãch rupaiyã), “Five rupees.” This is a fragment, but a perfectly useful price answer when the question already establishes what is being priced. We are practising small numbers, not claiming that this amount is a realistic current price for every item.

`कति जना विद्यार्थी?` (kati janā vidyārthī) asks “How many students?” in an established context. The choice of classifier helps identify the category being counted. You will also hear fused or special counting forms, particularly with one and people. This lesson chooses transparent patterns with two and three rather than pretending one classifier works for every noun.

### Common trap

Do not use `जना` for books simply because English calls both people and books “countable.” Nepali classifier choices encode distinctions that English usually leaves unmarked. Also separate `पानी` (pānī), water, from `पनि` (pani), also: their vowels and meanings differ.

### Practice

Give a phrase, request, or question as specified.

1. Two books. (use vaṭā)
2. Three students. (use janā)
3. Please give me two pens.
4. How much does this cost?

***Answers:***

1. `दुई वटा किताब` - `Duī vaṭā kitāb`
2. `तीन जना विद्यार्थी` - `Tīn janā vidyārthī`
3. `दुई वटा कलम दिनुहोस्।` - `Duī vaṭā kalam dinuhos.`
4. `यो कति पर्छ?` - `Yo kati parchha?`

### Why these answers work

The first two match the classifier to the category. The third transfers a quantity phrase into an imperative. The fourth asks about cost rather than number of objects. Phrase answers are deliberate where the prompt asks for a phrase, so there is no missing finite verb to repair.

### Retrieve and recombine

Ask for three books and then ask their price in the immediate context. Next, count four students. Notice which information changes the classifier and which changes only the numeral.

## Lesson 18: Express a preference and a need

### Outcome and the experiencer

You will say what you like and what you need. Nepali often puts the person experiencing a preference in a `लाई` phrase. `मलाई चिया मन पर्छ।` (malāī chiyā man parchha) means “I like tea.” The expression `मन पर्नु` is learned as a preference construction. Translating English “I like” with an unmarked `म` and an invented direct equivalent would miss its structure.

### Think before reading on

You already know that `मलाई` can mean “to me.” Does that force every sentence containing it to be about receiving an object? No. Here it marks the experiencer of liking. The same grammatical marker can organise several types of participant. Start from the complete preference pattern rather than a rigid English gloss.

### Preferences and needs

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| मन पर्छ | man parchha | is liked; appeals to someone |
| मन पर्दैन | man pardaina | is not liked |
| चाहिन्छ | chāhinchha | is needed |
| चाहिँदैन | chāhĩdaina | is not needed |
| खाना | khānā | food; meal |
| दूध | dūdh | milk |
| फलफूल | phalphūl | fruit |
| सहायता | sahāyatā | assistance |

### Build a useful contrast

`मलाई कफी मन पर्दैन।` (malāī kaphī man pardaina) means “I do not like coffee.” This is not the same as “I do not drink coffee”: a person could like it but avoid drinking it. `तपाईंलाई चिया मन पर्छ?` (tapāĩlāī chiyā man parchha) asks respectfully whether the addressee likes tea. The respectful experiencer does not change `पर्छ` into a respectful action ending.

`मलाई पानी चाहिन्छ।` (malāī pānī chāhinchha) means “I need water.” `मलाई सहायता चाहिन्छ।` (malāī sahāyatā chāhinchha) means “I need assistance.” A need statement can motivate a request, but it is not itself the same grammatical form as `पानी दिनुहोस्`, “Please give water.” Choose whether you are describing a need or directly asking someone to act.

You can decline an offered item with `मलाई चिया चाहिँदैन।` (malāī chiyā chāhĩdaina), “I do not need tea.” In actual conversation, a courteous acknowledgment can soften a refusal. This lesson concentrates on making the need and preference distinction explicit, without prescribing one fixed social script for every household.

### Common trap

Do not select a first-person action ending merely because the English translation begins “I.” The experiencer is marked with `लाई`. Also avoid treating “like,” “need,” “have,” and “consume” as interchangeable: a person may have tea, like it, need water, and currently be drinking nothing.

### Practice

Use the preference or need pattern requested.

1. I like tea.
2. I do not like coffee.
3. Do you like tea? (respectful)
4. I need water.

***Answers:***

1. `मलाई चिया मन पर्छ।` - `Malāī chiyā man parchha.`
2. `मलाई कफी मन पर्दैन।` - `Malāī kaphī man pardaina.`
3. `तपाईंलाई चिया मन पर्छ?` - `Tapāĩlāī chiyā man parchha?`
4. `मलाई पानी चाहिन्छ।` - `Malāī pānī chāhinchha.`

### Why these answers work

All four identify an experiencer with `लाई`, but the last uses a need predicate rather than a liking expression. The negative preference changes `पर्छ` to `पर्दैन`. Respect is preserved in the experiencer pronoun, while the predicate follows this construction's grammar.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe one food you like, one drink you do not like, and one item you need now. Then turn the need into a polite request. Explain what changes when you move from a statement to a request.

## Lesson 19: Talk about a completed journey

### Outcome and simple past

You will say where you went or came yesterday. Common motion verbs have past forms that must be learned as actual Nepali patterns. `म हिजो बजार गएँ।` (ma hijo bajār gaẽ) means “I went to the market yesterday.” The present `जान्छु` does not simply acquire an English-style past suffix. The stem changes to the past form built on `गए`.

### Think before reading on

If the time word is yesterday, which established nonpast form needs replacing? Start with `म बजार जान्छु`, then use `गएँ`: `म बजार गएँ।` (ma bajār gaẽ). Adding `हिजो` makes the day explicit, but the verb itself now also presents a completed past event.

### Past movement and state forms

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| गएँ | gaẽ | I went |
| आयो | āyo | he/it came, ordinary masculine/default |
| आएँ | āẽ | I came |
| जानुभयो | jānubhayo | went, respectful |
| आउनुभयो | āunubhayo | came, respectful |
| थिएँ | thiẽ | I was |
| हुनुहुन्थ्यो | hunuhunthyo | was/were, respectful |
| हिजो | hijo | yesterday |

### Follow the participant into the past

`म हिजो घरमा थिएँ।` (ma hijo gharmā thiẽ) means “I was at home yesterday.” This is a past location, not a completed journey. `उहाँ हिजो आउनुभयो।` (uhã hijo āunubhayo) means “He or she came yesterday,” respectfully. `तपाईं हिजो कहाँ जानुभयो?` (tapāĩ hijo kahã jānubhayo) asks respectfully “Where did you go yesterday?”

First-person `गएँ`, `आएँ`, and `थिएँ` preserve nasalisation. Honorific past motion uses the extended forms in the table. These are practical forms worth recognising whole before analysing every historical component. They are not a license to attach `भयो` after any already-conjugated verb.

Gender distinctions appear in many ordinary third-person past forms. For example, the ordinary feminine counterpart to `आयो` is `आई` (āī), “she came.” Our production examples chiefly use first person and respectful reference, allowing a useful past narrative without pretending that all third-person forms are gender-free.

### Common trap

Do not put the agent marker `ले` after “I” merely because a sentence is past. With the motion and location examples here, use plain `म`. The next lesson introduces a transitive past construction where `मैले` is appropriate. Time alone does not determine that marking.

### Practice

Describe yesterday using a past form.

1. I went to the market yesterday.
2. I came yesterday.
3. I was at home yesterday.
4. Where did you go yesterday? (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `म हिजो बजार गएँ।` - `Ma hijo bajār gaẽ.`
2. `म हिजो आएँ।` - `Ma hijo āẽ.`
3. `म हिजो घरमा थिएँ।` - `Ma hijo gharmā thiẽ.`
4. `तपाईं हिजो कहाँ जानुभयो?` - `Tapāĩ hijo kahã jānubhayo?`

### Why these answers work

The first two describe completed motion, while the third locates the speaker in the past. The fourth keeps respectful address. The first-person subject remains unmarked in all three first-person examples. Choosing a past verb does not automatically create a transitive event or require an agent marker.

### Retrieve and recombine

Say where you were yesterday, then where you went. Next, ask a respected person when they came. Contrast the state with the movement rather than treating all three English past statements as identical constructions.

## Lesson 20: Report what you bought, read, or did

### Outcome and the marked agent

You will describe a completed transitive action using `ले`. In `मैले किताब किनेँ।` (maile kitāb kinẽ), “I bought a book,” `मैले` is the agent-marked form of “I.” The action affects or concerns an object. This differs from the motion sentence `म बजार गएँ`, where the speaker goes somewhere without a direct object of that kind.

### Think before reading on

Compare “I went” and “I bought a book.” Which event contains an object being bought? Nepali commonly marks the agent of the completed transitive event with `ले`. The pronoun `म` combines into the form `मैले`, rather than a spelling invented by mechanically joining two words. Learn the pronoun's actual marked form.

### Completed actions

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| मैले | maile | I, agent-marked |
| तपाईंले | tapāĩle | you, agent-marked and respectful |
| उहाँले | uhãle | he/she, agent-marked and respectful |
| किन्नु | kinnu | to buy |
| किनेँ | kinẽ | I bought |
| पढेँ | paḍhẽ | I read/studied |
| गरेँ | garẽ | I did |
| खाएँ | khāẽ | I ate/drank |

### Objects still precede the verb

`मैले हिजो किताब पढेँ।` (maile hijo kitāb paḍhẽ) means “I read a book yesterday.” `मैले काम गरेँ।` (maile kām garẽ) means “I did the work.” `मैले चिया खाएँ।` (maile chiyā khāẽ) means “I drank tea.” Notice that the first-person ending is still visible despite the agent marker. Do not import a claim from Hindi that every ergative-marked subject must cause agreement only with the object.

`तपाईंले के किन्नुभयो?` (tapāĩle ke kinnubhayo) means “What did you buy?” respectfully. The question word occupies the object slot. `उहाँले किताब किन्नुभयो।` (uhãle kitāb kinnubhayo) reports that a respected person bought a book. The marker and the honorific verb do separate jobs: one identifies the participant's role, and the other maintains respectful agreement.

Nepali agent marking is more complex across tense, aspect, discourse, and transitivity than a single beginner rule can capture. For now, reproduce the clear completed transitive patterns here and contrast them with Lesson 19. Avoid extending the rule to every clause with an English past translation.

### Common trap

Do not change `मैले किताब किनेँ` to a verb form based solely on the book's grammatical gender. Nor should you put `लाई` on the book automatically. The agent marker and object marker are different devices, introduced for different roles.

### Practice

Use a completed transitive construction.

1. I bought a book.
2. I read a book yesterday.
3. I drank tea.
4. What did you buy? (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `मैले किताब किनेँ।` - `Maile kitāb kinẽ.`
2. `मैले हिजो किताब पढेँ।` - `Maile hijo kitāb paḍhẽ.`
3. `मैले चिया खाएँ।` - `Maile chiyā khāẽ.`
4. `तपाईंले के किन्नुभयो?` - `Tapāĩle ke kinnubhayo?`

### Why these answers work

Each answer has a transitive action and a marked agent. The first three retain first-person past endings. The question uses a respectful agent and respectful past verb. The object remains unmarked in these ordinary examples, and its position before the action supports the familiar verb-last pattern.

### Retrieve and recombine

Tell a two-sentence story: you went to the market, and you bought a pen. Explain why the first sentence begins `म` and the second begins `मैले`. Then ask a respected friend what they bought.

## Lesson 21: State an intention, an ability, and an obligation

### Outcome and three meanings

You will distinguish wanting to act, being able to act, and needing to act. These are different meanings even when a conversation concerns the same journey. `म भोलि जान्छु।` (ma bholi jānchhu) states a plan to go tomorrow. `म जान सक्छु।` (ma jāna sakchhu) states an ability or practical possibility. `म जान चाहन्छु।` (ma jāna chāhanchhu) states a wish or intention.

### Think before reading on

Can someone want to travel but lack the ability or opportunity to do so? If so, translating both meanings with one word would erase useful information. Nepali uses the action in the `न` form before these following verbs: `जान` plus `सक्छु`, or `जान` plus `चाहन्छु`. This `जान` is not the finite statement `जान्छु`.

### Action plus another predicate

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| जान | jāna | to go, before another predicate |
| गर्न | garna | to do, before another predicate |
| पढ्न | paḍhna | to read/study, before another predicate |
| बोल्न | bolna | to speak, before another predicate |
| सक्छु | sakchhu | I can |
| चाहन्छु | chāhanchhu | I want |
| पर्छ | parchha | it is necessary; must, in this pattern |
| आज | āja | today |

### Make the main action explicit

`म नेपाली बोल्न सक्छु।` (ma nepālī bolna sakchhu) means “I can speak Nepali.” In an early learner's real introduction, qualify this honestly, for example with `अलिअलि` (aliali), “a little”: `म अलिअलि नेपाली बोल्न सक्छु।` (ma aliali nepālī bolna sakchhu). Being able to build this sentence is not evidence of advanced speaking ability.

`म नेपाली पढ्न चाहन्छु।` (ma nepālī paḍhna chāhanchhu) means “I want to study Nepali.” `मैले आज काम गर्नुपर्छ।` (maile āja kām garnuparchha) means “I must work today.” In the obligation construction, `गर्नु` combines with `पर्छ`, often written together. Notice the marked participant `मैले`. This is another reason not to define `ले` merely as “the past-tense marker.”

The ordinary nonpast already expresses many plans with a future time word. Nepali also has other future and prospective constructions; this foundation does not force them into a single table. Choose the expression that matches whether you are planning, wanting, able, or required to act.

### Common trap

Do not confuse needing an object, `मलाई पानी चाहिन्छ`, with wanting to perform an action, `म जान चाहन्छु`. The participants and verb constructions differ. Also avoid retaining the full finite `जान्छु` before `सक्छु`; the action must take the linking form shown here.

### Practice

Express the requested meaning precisely.

1. I can go.
2. I want to study Nepali.
3. I can speak a little Nepali.
4. I must work today.

***Answers:***

1. `म जान सक्छु।` - `Ma jāna sakchhu.`
2. `म नेपाली पढ्न चाहन्छु।` - `Ma nepālī paḍhna chāhanchhu.`
3. `म अलिअलि नेपाली बोल्न सक्छु।` - `Ma aliali nepālī bolna sakchhu.`
4. `मैले आज काम गर्नुपर्छ।` - `Maile āja kām garnuparchha.`

### Why these answers work

The first three put a nonfinite action before the verb of ability or desire. The fourth uses the obligation construction with a marked participant. “Can,” “want,” and “must” make distinct claims about the same possible action, so the choice is semantic as well as grammatical.

### Retrieve and recombine

Say that you want to go to the market tomorrow, that you can go by bus, and that you must work today. Keep these as separate clauses and explain how each changes the listener's understanding of the plan.

## Lesson 22: Join ideas with and, but, and because

### Outcome and connected speech

You will connect two known clauses without losing their individual grammar. `र` (ra) joins compatible pieces, and `तर` (tara) marks a contrast. `म पढ्छु र काम गर्छु।` (ma paḍhchhu ra kām garchhu) means “I study and work.” The second clause can omit the repeated subject because the speaker remains the subject of both actions.

### Think before reading on

Suppose you like tea but have none available. Would “and” clearly express the contrast you want? `मलाई चिया मन पर्छ, तर मसँग चिया छैन।` (malāī chiyā man parchha, tara masãga chiyā chhaina) uses “but.” Notice that connecting the clauses does not make their participant marking identical. Liking keeps `मलाई`, while availability keeps `मसँग`.

### Connecting words

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| र | ra | and |
| तर | tara | but |
| किनभने | kinabhane | because |
| त्यसैले | tyasaile | therefore; so |
| पनि | pani | also; too |
| मात्र | mātra | only |
| व्यस्त | vyasta | busy |
| फुर्सद | phursad | free time |

### Add a reason without hiding the clauses

`म आज घरमा छु किनभने म व्यस्त छु।` (ma āja gharmā chhu kinabhane ma vyasta chhu) means “I am at home today because I am busy.” Both clauses are complete first-person state statements. The reason comes after `किनभने`. This explicit pattern is useful for a beginner; Nepali has other compact reason constructions that can be learned later.

`मसँग फुर्सद छैन, त्यसैले म घरमा छु।` (masãga phursad chhaina, tyasaile ma gharmā chhu) means “I have no free time, so I am at home.” Here the first clause gives the situation and the second gives a consequence. Whether the consequence makes sense depends on the context. Grammar provides a relation; it does not guarantee that every invented reason is sensible.

`म पनि पढ्छु।` (ma pani paḍhchhu) means “I also study.” Placing `पनि` after `म` can foreground that the speaker, too, is included. `म चिया मात्र खान्छु।` (ma chiyā mātra khānchhu) means “I drink only tea” in the relevant contrast. The position of a focusing word helps show which part is included or limited.

### Common trap

Do not erase markers when joining sentences. The speaker can appear as `म`, `मलाई`, or `मसँग` because each clause organises its participants differently. A conjunction links clauses; it does not make them share one grammatical frame.

### Practice

Connect the meanings as requested.

1. I study and work.
2. I also study.
3. I drink only tea.
4. I like tea, but I do not have tea.

***Answers:***

1. `म पढ्छु र काम गर्छु।` - `Ma paḍhchhu ra kām garchhu.`
2. `म पनि पढ्छु।` - `Ma pani paḍhchhu.`
3. `म चिया मात्र खान्छु।` - `Ma chiyā mātra khānchhu.`
4. `मलाई चिया मन पर्छ, तर मसँग चिया छैन।` - `Malāī chiyā man parchha, tara masãga chiyā chhaina.`

### Why these answers work

The first shares a recoverable subject across two actions. The next two focus different parts of a clause. The last preserves the experiencer of liking and the possessor of an unavailable item. Its contrast is meaningful because preference does not imply possession.

### Retrieve and recombine

Join a positive routine and a contrasting negative routine with “but.” Next, explain a simple situation using “because.” Check each clause independently before connecting them, especially the markers attached to the speaker.

## Lesson 23: Find a place and follow directions

### Outcome and spatial relationships

You will locate one place relative to another and understand a short direction. Earlier, `मा` located things in or at a place. Nepali also uses relational expressions such as `अगाडि`, “in front,” and `पछाडि`, “behind.” A noun can be linked to them through a genitive phrase: `घरको अगाडि` (gharko agāḍi), “in front of the house.”

### Think before reading on

If you say only “in front,” can a listener always know in front of what? To supply the reference point, place it before the relation: house's front. `पसल घरको अगाडि छ।` (pasal gharko agāḍi chha) means “The shop is in front of the house.” The whole location phrase then occupies the same slot that “here” occupied in Lesson 2.

### Direction vocabulary

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| अगाडि | agāḍi | in front; ahead |
| पछाडि | pachhāḍi | behind; at the back |
| नजिक | najik | near |
| टाढा | ṭāḍhā | far |
| दायाँ | dāyã | right |
| बायाँ | bāyã | left |
| सीधा | sīdhā | straight |
| पसल | pasal | shop |
| मोडिनुहोस् | moḍinuhos | please turn |

### From description to instruction

`पसल यहाँबाट टाढा छ।` (pasal yahãbāṭa ṭāḍhā chha) means “The shop is far from here.” `यहाँबाट` extends the source marker to the word “here.” `पसल घरको नजिक छ।` (pasal gharko najik chha) means “The shop is near the house.” The familiar final `छ` makes these spatial descriptions, not commands.

For instructions, use the request forms: `सीधा जानुहोस्।` (sīdhā jānuhos), “Please go straight.” `दायाँ मोडिनुहोस्।` (dāyã moḍinuhos), “Please turn right.” `बायाँ मोडिनुहोस्।` (bāyã moḍinuhos), “Please turn left.” You can combine a location answer and two short directions without constructing an elaborate unfamiliar sentence.

Imagine asking `पसल कहाँ छ?` A helpful reply might locate it near a house, then tell you to go straight and turn left. Each sentence has one job. This keeps the information easier to follow and gives the listener a chance to ask for repetition using Lesson 12.

### Common trap

Left and right depend on the traveler's orientation. A grammatically accurate direction can still be unclear if the viewpoint is unstated. Also distinguish describing a place with `छ` from directing a person with an imperative. A spatial adverb alone does not decide the sentence's purpose.

### Practice

Locate the shop or give a polite direction.

1. The shop is in front of the house.
2. The shop is near the house.
3. Please go straight.
4. Please turn left.

***Answers:***

1. `पसल घरको अगाडि छ।` - `Pasal gharko agāḍi chha.`
2. `पसल घरको नजिक छ।` - `Pasal gharko najik chha.`
3. `सीधा जानुहोस्।` - `Sīdhā jānuhos.`
4. `बायाँ मोडिनुहोस्।` - `Bāyã moḍinuhos.`

### Why these answers work

The first two place the reference point before the relational word, then finish with a location verb. The final two are imperatives directed at a traveler. The first sentence's “front” relation depends on the house, while the direction “straight” depends on the current route.

### Retrieve and recombine

Ask where a school is, then imagine it behind the house. Give two short directions to reach it. Finally, ask for the second direction again, drawing on the repair phrase from Lesson 12.

## Lesson 24: Manage a short visit and a purchase

### Outcome and a practical exchange

You will combine a greeting, a question, a quantity, and a request into a short interaction. The goal is not to memorise one entire dialogue. It is to recognise which sentence pattern solves each conversational problem. If an item changes from a pen to a book, the request structure should remain usable.

### Think before reading on

At a shop, you need to find out whether books are available before asking for two. Which earlier construction asks about existence rather than ownership? `यहाँ किताब छ?` (yahã kitāb chha) asks whether there is a book available here. `यो तपाईंको किताब हो?` asks whether a particular book belongs to the addressee. Those questions could receive different answers.

### Interaction toolkit

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| नमस्ते | namaste | respectful greeting |
| धन्यवाद | dhanyavād | thank you |
| हुन्छ | hunchha | all right; okay, in an acceptance |
| माफ गर्नुहोस् | māph garnuhos | please excuse me; sorry |
| फेरि | pheri | again |
| अलि | ali | a little; somewhat |
| दुई वटा | duī vaṭā | two, for ordinary objects |
| कति पर्छ | kati parchha | how much does it cost |

### A dialogue you can rebuild

Customer: `नमस्ते। यहाँ किताब छ?` (namaste; yahã kitāb chha), “Hello. Are books available here?” Shopkeeper: `छ।` (chha), “Yes.” Customer: `यो कति पर्छ?` (yo kati parchha), “How much does this cost?” After hearing a price, the customer can say `दुई वटा किताब दिनुहोस्।` (duī vaṭā kitāb dinuhos), “Please give me two books.” The price is deliberately left open because it depends on the item and situation.

If you did not hear the price, say `फेरि भन्नुहोस्।` (pheri bhannuhos), “Please say it again.” If the speech was too fast, say `अलि बिस्तारै बोल्नुहोस्।` (ali bistārai bolnuhos), “Please speak a little more slowly.” These repair moves are part of competent communication, not a failure of the conversation.

`हुन्छ` is an everyday acceptance in many contexts: “Okay,” “That works,” or “All right.” It also has broader grammatical uses related to happening, being, or becoming. Do not interpret it as one universal “yes” regardless of the question. The existence question above is answered clearly with `छ`.

### Common trap

Do not claim a price you did not understand. Grammatically finishing a dialogue is less useful than obtaining the missing information. Also keep `माफ गर्नुहोस्` as the complete polite expression rather than translating the English “sorry” by dropping its verb.

### Practice

Supply a suitable short line for the situation.

1. Ask whether books are available here.
2. Ask politely for two books.
3. Ask the speaker to speak a little more slowly.
4. Thank the person.

***Answers:***

1. `यहाँ किताब छ?` - `Yahã kitāb chha?`
2. `दुई वटा किताब दिनुहोस्।` - `Duī vaṭā kitāb dinuhos.`
3. `अलि बिस्तारै बोल्नुहोस्।` - `Ali bistārai bolnuhos.`
4. `धन्यवाद।` - `Dhanyavād.`

### Why these answers work

The first asks about availability, the second requests an action involving a counted object, and the third repairs the communication channel. The final acknowledgment closes the immediate exchange. None requires a new tense, so the difficulty lies in selecting the right known pattern at the right moment.

### Retrieve and recombine

Repeat the exchange with pens instead of books. Then imagine visiting a teacher: replace the availability question with a respectful question about their current activity. Keep the greeting and repair phrases available in both settings.

## Lesson 25: Rebuild a day from the patterns

### Outcome and cumulative control

You will tell a small, coherent account of yesterday, today, and tomorrow, then ask a follow-up question. This review introduces no new tense table. Its challenge is choosing among constructions you know: identity, state, possession, preference, routine, progressive, completed action, and intention. A strong foundation means making those distinctions reliably in short sentences.

### Think before reading on

You went to the market yesterday and bought a book. Today you are reading it at home. Tomorrow you want to study with a teacher. Which sentence needs `मैले`, which needs plain `म`, and which needs the progressive auxiliary? Think through participant roles and time before looking at the worked account.

### Retrieval map

| Nepali | Romanisation | Meaning or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| म गएँ | ma gaẽ | I went |
| मैले किनेँ | maile kinẽ | I bought |
| म पढ्दै छु | ma paḍhdai chhu | I am reading/studying |
| म जान चाहन्छु | ma jāna chāhanchhu | I want to go |
| मलाई मन पर्छ | malāī man parchha | I like it |
| मसँग छ | masãga chha | I have it |
| कहाँ हुनुहुन्छ | kahã hunuhunchha | where are you, respectful |
| के गर्नुभयो | ke garnubhayo | what did you do, respectful |

### A worked account

`म हिजो बजार गएँ।` (ma hijo bajār gaẽ), “I went to the market yesterday.” `मैले किताब किनेँ।` (maile kitāb kinẽ), “I bought a book.” `म अहिले घरमा किताब पढ्दै छु।` (ma ahile gharmā kitāb paḍhdai chhu), “I am reading a book at home now.” `म भोलि नेपाली पढ्न चाहन्छु।` (ma bholi nepālī paḍhna chāhanchhu), “I want to study Nepali tomorrow.”

The first sentence is motion, so its subject is plain `म`. The second is a completed transitive action, so its agent is `मैले`. The third presents an unfolding action, and the fourth expresses desire. The day words help organise the story, but the verbs still carry essential information. You cannot obtain the whole sequence by changing only yesterday into today and tomorrow.

To invite the other person into the exchange, ask `तपाईंले हिजो के गर्नुभयो?` (tapāĩle hijo ke garnubhayo), “What did you do yesterday?” This changes the interaction from a recital into a conversation. If the reply exceeds your understanding, request repetition or slower speech. You already have grammatical tools for doing so respectfully.

### Common trap

Do not judge a sentence only by whether its English translation sounds plausible. Check the Nepali subject role, respect level, predicate family, and time construction. A literal translation can hide a mismatch, especially with “have,” “like,” and “be.” Keep unfamiliar advanced forms out of your own production until their function is understood.

### Practice

Recombine the course patterns for these new messages.

1. I went home yesterday.
2. I bought a pen.
3. I am reading a book at home now.
4. What did you do yesterday? (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `म हिजो घर गएँ।` - `Ma hijo ghar gaẽ.`
2. `मैले कलम किनेँ।` - `Maile kalam kinẽ.`
3. `म अहिले घरमा किताब पढ्दै छु।` - `Ma ahile gharmā kitāb paḍhdai chhu.`
4. `तपाईंले हिजो के गर्नुभयो?` - `Tapāĩle hijo ke garnubhayo?`

### Why these answers work

The first contrasts directly with the marked agent in the second. The third combines time, location, object, ongoing action, and first-person auxiliary. The fourth invites a respected person to report a completed action. All four draw on earlier patterns while changing the practical content.

### Retrieve and recombine

Tell a four-sentence account using a past event, a current action, a preference, and a future plan. Add a respectful question. Revisit constructions you recalled only through English. Continue with listening and fluent-speaker feedback; completing this draft is not a proficiency certificate.
