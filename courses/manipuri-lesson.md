# Manipuri: A pattern-first foundation

Editorial status: original AI-assisted draft; fluent-speaker review pending.

This is a foundation in standard conversational Manipuri, also called Meitei or Meiteilon, following the Central Institute of Indian Languages teaching variety. It uses **Roman reading notation**, not Meetei Mayek. Native-script instruction is pending. Meetei Mayek and the Bengali script used in older educational materials are distinct writing systems; this course does not claim to teach either through transliteration alone.

Our consistent spelling-oriented notation uses `ch` for the sound written `c` in some linguistic transcriptions, `ng` for the single nasal sound in English sing, `sh` for the corresponding spelling in the institute examples, and `ei`, `ai`, `oi`, `ou` as written vowel sequences. A doubled consonant matters. `ph`, `th`, and `kh` indicate aspiration, not English f or the fricative in thin. Ordinary `a` includes the central vowel represented in scholarly phonemic transcription as schwa; these spellings are reading aids, not narrow phonetic instructions. Lexical tone is not comprehensively marked. Learn pronunciation with a fluent speaker or reliable recordings; unmarked tone does not mean tone is absent. We retain complete grammatical words in answers and use hyphens only to explain their components.

### References and review notes

These are original explanations and newly recombined examples, checked against grammatical descriptions, not a reproduction of an institute course. The main reference is [CIIL's An Intensive Course in Manipuri, edited by B. Syamala Kumari and N. Pramodini, 2011](https://archive.org/details/dli.language.2259), whose [public text](https://archive.org/stream/dli.language.2259/dli.language.2259_djvu.txt) supplies Bengali and Devanagari exemplars and English grammatical notes. The [CIIL catalogue](https://sanchika.ciil.org/items/29ceda31-9126-42e6-a36d-6ad43785ca02/full) establishes the edition. [Sharma's Learners' Manipuri-English Dictionary at the University of Chicago](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/dictionaries/sharma/) is a further lexical reference. [Chelliah's A Grammar of Meithei](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9783110801118/html) is a scholarly reference; the available OCR is unreliable for some phonemic characters and was not used to invent missing spellings.

This foundation deliberately limits productive verb forms to illustrated patterns. It does not cover the entire system of aspect, evidential meaning, honorific morphology, or vowel and consonant alternations. Words with matching Roman spellings can still differ in pronunciation or meaning. The final review identifies these limits explicitly.

## Lesson 1: Say who you are

**Outcome:** introduce yourself and identify a teacher using a noun followed by `-ni`.

### Notice before the rule

Compare `Ei ojani` and `Nang ojani`. If the first means “I am a teacher” and the second “You are a teacher,” which part changes? Predict whether the ending needs a different form for a woman.

Only the pronoun changes. In this pattern Manipuri does not copy the English am/are distinction or make a teacher predicate agree with the speaker's gender. Put the person first, then the identifying noun with `-ni` attached. The ending supplies the affirmative identification. A name can occupy the same position: `Ei Romani` means “I am Roma.” The name itself is Roma; the final ni belongs to the sentence.

| Form | Meaning or use |
|---|---|
| ei | I |
| nang | you, familiar singular |
| oja | teacher; respectful address to a teacher |
| ojani | am/is/are a teacher |
| Roma | a personal name |
| khurumjari | a greeting |
| thagatchari | thank you |

### Build a small introduction

`Khurumjari` can open an encounter. Follow it with `Ei Romani`, “I am Roma,” or `Ei ojani`, “I am a teacher.” These are two separate choices of identifying predicate. English requires an article in “a teacher”; Manipuri does not require you to insert an equivalent of a in this basic sentence.

`Nang Romani` identifies the addressee as Roma. It is a statement, not yet a question. A rising English-style voice is not the whole grammar of asking in Manipuri; explicit question patterns come shortly. `Oja` can also be used alone to address a teacher, so the surrounding situation tells you whether you are calling someone or describing their role.

The pronoun `nang` belongs to familiar or downward-address situations. It is useful for recognising classroom speech but is not our default recommendation for addressing an unfamiliar adult. Later we introduce `adom` and respectful requests. A socially appropriate address involves more than choosing a noun ending.

### Common trap

Do not produce `Ei ni oja` by placing a separate “am” before the noun. Here ni follows the predicate. Equally, do not add an English gender distinction to ei: the same pronoun works regardless of the speaker's gender.

### Practice

Use the pattern taught here.

1. Say: I am Roma.
2. Say: I am a teacher.
3. Tell a familiar person: You are a teacher.
4. Give the greeting from the table.

***Answers:***

1. `Ei Romani`
2. `Ei ojani`
3. `Nang ojani`
4. `Khurumjari`

### Why these answers work

In answers 1–3, ni attaches to the identifying expression rather than the subject. Roma plus ni produces Romani; oja plus ni produces ojani. The third answer changes ei to nang while leaving the predicate intact. The fourth answer is a conventional greeting, not an example of the noun-plus-ni rule.

**Retrieve and transfer:** introduce a fictional teacher named Roma with two separate statements. Then remove the name and identify only the occupation. Explain which piece of each statement tells the listener that you are making an identification.

## Lesson 2: Point out this and that

**Outcome:** identify nearby and more distant objects, and distinguish a complete statement from an object phrase.

### Think first

You hear `Masi lairikni` while someone points to a book, then see the shorter expression `lairiksi`. Both involve a book nearby. Does the second expression necessarily assert “This is a book”? Decide before reading the explanation.

`Masi lairikni` is a complete identification, “This is a book.” `Lairiksi` is “this book,” a noun phrase that can later become a subject or object. Manipuri offers both independent demonstratives and shorter forms attached to a noun. The difference is grammatical, not merely a shorter English translation.

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| masi | this |
| madu | that |
| lairik | book |
| alu | potato |
| yum | house |
| lairiksi | this book |
| lairikdu | that book |
| yumni | is a house |

### Two useful patterns

For a statement use **masi/madu + noun-ni**: `Madu yumni`, “That is a house”; `Masi aluni`, “This is a potato.” The ending ni belongs on the identifying noun. For an object phrase, attach **-si** or **-du**: `alusi`, “this potato”; `aludu`, “that potato.” The independent pronoun and the noun-attached form are related, but you should not keep the entire word masi inside a suffix.

You may encounter `asi` and `adu` as independent demonstratives as well. We begin with masi and madu so each practice answer has one clear target. The institute material uses noun-attached demonstratives to indicate definiteness. English this and that are useful starting glosses, but actual discourse reference can involve what has already been mentioned as well as physical distance.

Imagine two baskets. You point near you: `Masi aluni`. Your partner points at the other basket: `Madu aluni`. The noun and identifying ending stay unchanged; the pointing word changes. If you label the baskets, `alusi` and `aludu` identify the particular potatoes without themselves making the same assertion.

### Common trap

Do not use `lairiksi` as though its final si meant “is.” The assertion in our identification pattern comes from ni. A demonstrative identifies a referent; a copular ending builds the particular statement. This distinction will matter when we put an object before an action verb.

### Practice

Make either a statement or a phrase as requested.

1. Say: This is a book.
2. Say: That is a house.
3. Write the phrase: this potato.
4. Write the phrase: that book.

***Answers:***

1. `Masi lairikni`
2. `Madu yumni`
3. `Alusi`
4. `Lairikdu`

### Why these answers work

The first two answers contain a demonstrative subject and a predicate ending in ni. The last two are noun phrases with attached demonstratives. No identifying ni is requested there. The final consonant of lairik remains before du; you are combining the illustrated forms, not applying an English plural rule.

**Retrieve and transfer:** point to an imaginary house and identify it. Then turn that statement into a label meaning “that house.” Explain why the two expressions have different jobs even though they mention the same object.

## Lesson 3: Ask what something is

**Outcome:** ask for an object's identity and give a relevant answer without moving the predicate to the beginning.

### Predict the missing information

`Masi karino?` is asked beside an unfamiliar object. You know masi means this. If a response is `Masi lairikni`, what kind of information must kari request: a place, a person, or an identity?

`Kari` asks “what.” In this identification question, it combines with `-no`, giving `karino`. The resulting pattern is **masi/madu + karino?** The identifying expression stays after the subject. You do not introduce an English-like is before this.

| Form | Meaning or function |
|---|---|
| kari | what |
| karino | what, in the illustrated question |
| kana | who |
| kanano | who, in an identification question |
| masi | this |
| madu | that |
| oja | teacher |
| lairik | book |

### Build questions that match answers

`Madu karino?` asks “What is that?” A relevant response is `Madu lairikni`, “That is a book.” The question and response have parallel information structure: madu remains the subject, while karino is replaced by the identifying predicate lairikni.

For a person, use kana: `Nang kanano?`, “Who are you?” In a suitable familiar setting, `Ei Romani` supplies the requested identity. A stranger may prefer a more courteous introduction rather than a blunt question. These are structural examples, not a claim that every grammatically possible question is equally comfortable in every encounter.

The suffix no occurs with interrogative words in several constructions. The institute description associates it with seeking a particular response. Learn the complete illustrated question here; do not turn no into a universal replacement for every sentence ending. Later yes/no questions use a different pattern, and verbal information questions have their own forms.

If someone asks `Masi karino?`, answering with a place name alone might be unhelpful because the requested category is identity. Conversely, a question about a person's name should be answered with their identity, not merely an object they own. Choosing the right content is part of using the pattern.

### Common trap

Do not retain affirmative ni before no in `karino`. The question form here is kari plus no. “What is?” cannot be assembled by attaching every ending learned so far. Each construction licenses a particular ending sequence.

### Practice

Ask or answer the indicated identification question.

1. Ask: What is this?
2. Ask: What is that?
3. Ask a familiar person: Who are you?
4. Answer “What is that?” with: That is a potato.

***Answers:***

1. `Masi karino?`
2. `Madu karino?`
3. `Nang kanano?`
4. `Madu aluni`

### Why these answers work

Kari selects an identity of a thing; kana selects a person's identity. No completes the question forms in answers 1–3. Answer 4 returns to affirmative ni because it supplies the information rather than requesting it. Madu is retained so the reply points to the same referent.

**Retrieve and transfer:** make an imaginary exchange about a house. Ask its identity and answer the question. Then replace the object with a person and decide which question word must change.

## Lesson 4: Correct an identification

**Outcome:** say that something is not a book, house, or teacher using the negative copula `natte`.

### Think before correcting

A friend mistakes a potato for something else. You already know `Masi aluni`. Compare the endings in `Masi lairikni` and `Masi lairik natte`. Which one would correct the mistaken claim “This is a book”? Would you keep ni in the correction?

The correction uses **subject + identifying noun + natte**. `Natte` is the negative copular form in this construction. Replace the affirmative predicate ending; do not keep ni and simply add an English-style not somewhere in the sentence.

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| natte | is/am/are not, identifying negative |
| ojani | am/is/are a teacher |
| oja natte | am/is/are not a teacher |
| yumni | is a house |
| yum natte | is not a house |
| lairikni | is a book |
| lairik natte | is not a book |
| aluni | is a potato |

### Make a precise correction

`Ei oja natte` means “I am not a teacher.” It rejects an occupational identification. `Madu yum natte` means “That is not a house.” It does not mean that no house exists anywhere. The scope of the correction is the identification of the particular subject.

For a two-turn repair, hear `Masi lairikni`, then reply `Masi lairik natte`. You can follow with a separate affirmative statement, `Masi aluni`. This is useful language even with a tiny vocabulary: reject one proposed category and supply the right one.

A negative answer can be shorter when the referent is obvious, but we keep the subject in initial exercises to show the structure. Full forms help you see which item is being corrected. Later conversation can omit predictable information naturally; omission does not erase the underlying relationship between subject and predicate.

Do not expect natte to negate every kind of clause. Existence uses lei and its own negative leite. Actions likewise have their own negative formations. English “not” hides these differences because it appears in many English constructions, whereas Manipuri chooses endings according to the predicate pattern.

### Common trap

`Masi lairikni natte` is not the basic negative pattern being taught. It stacks an affirmative identification onto the negative copula. Work with the pair lairikni versus lairik natte rather than treating ni as part of the dictionary noun lairik.

### Practice

Correct the identification with natte.

1. Say: I am not a teacher.
2. Say: This is not a book.
3. Say: That is not a house.
4. After the correction, say: This is a potato.

***Answers:***

1. `Ei oja natte`
2. `Masi lairik natte`
3. `Madu yum natte`
4. `Masi aluni`

### Why these answers work

The first three answers replace affirmative identification with natte. Their nouns are left as oja, lairik, and yum before the negative word. Answer 4 supplies a positive alternative and therefore returns to aluni. Negative and affirmative endings express different claims about the same possible subject.

**Retrieve and transfer:** prepare a correction about someone's occupation and another about an object. After each negative, supply a plausible positive identity using vocabulary already introduced. Keep the two complete statements separate.

## Lesson 5: Confirm an identity politely

**Outcome:** ask a yes/no identification question and distinguish familiar nang from courteous adom.

### Predict the reply type

Compare `Adom ojara?` with `Adom kanano?`. One can be answered by confirming an occupation; the other requests a person's identity. Which question already proposes an answer for the listener to accept or reject?

`Adom ojara?` asks “Are you a teacher?” The noun oja takes the question form `-ra` in this illustrated environment. `Adom kanano?` asks “Who are you?” It does not supply the identity in advance. Learn these as two different ways of asking, rather than changing only your intonation.

| Form | Meaning or use |
|---|---|
| adom | you, courteous address |
| nang | you, familiar singular |
| ojara? | a teacher? |
| ojani | am/is/are a teacher |
| natte | negative identification |
| hoi | yes |
| alura? | a potato? |
| aluni | is a potato |

### Make a question and respond

`Masi alura?` means “Is this a potato?” A full affirmative response is `Masi aluni`. A negative response is `Masi alu natte`. The affirmative, question, and negative expressions are related choices, not endings you pile onto a single word all at once.

With adults you do not know well, adom is a useful courteous form. `Adom ojara?` gives a structural way to confirm a role. Address, relationship, and verbal honorific forms also affect politeness, so the pronoun is one part of a broader social choice. We will build respectful requests rather than assuming that adom automatically makes any command polite.

The yes/no question ending has sound-conditioned variants. The course begins with vowel-final nouns such as oja and alu, where ra is straightforward. You may hear other forms after consonants; do not attach ra indiscriminately to every noun from a dictionary. This restriction lets you produce several useful questions confidently while keeping the full phonology for later study.

A response beginning hoi confirms a positive question, but a complete statement often provides clearer practice. Negative questions can involve subtler response conventions, so we do not assume that English yes and no map mechanically onto every Manipuri response situation.

### Common trap

Do not turn `ojani` into `ojanira` in this elementary noun question. The practiced contrast is ojani, ojara, oja natte. Also avoid using nang with every stranger simply because it was the first pronoun you learned.

### Practice

Use the exact question or statement requested.

1. Ask courteously: Are you a teacher?
2. Ask: Is this a potato?
3. Confirm: I am a teacher.
4. Reject the claim: This is not a potato.

***Answers:***

1. `Adom ojara?`
2. `Masi alura?`
3. `Ei ojani`
4. `Masi alu natte`

### Why these answers work

Answers 1 and 2 put ra after vowel-final identifying nouns. Answer 3 chooses affirmative ni. Answer 4 uses the negative copula. Adom changes the address relationship in the first question, while the teacher predicate retains the same question pattern.

**Retrieve and transfer:** make a three-turn exchange consisting of a courteous occupation question, an affirmative reply, and a greeting. Then imagine the answer is negative and replace only the reply.

## Lesson 6: Say whose book it is

**Outcome:** build possessive noun phrases with genitive `-gi` and use them in identification statements.

### Think first

Compare `Ei ojani` with `Masi eigi lairikni`. The first identifies the speaker; the second identifies a book belonging to the speaker. What changed ei into an expression that can stand before lairik?

The genitive suffix gi gives `eigi`, “my.” The owner expression precedes the possessed noun. This creates **owner-gi + thing**, which can then receive the appropriate sentence ending. In `Masi eigi lairikni`, the meaning is “This is my book.” Gi belongs to the possessor, while ni identifies the entire predicate.

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| eigi | my |
| nanggi | your, familiar |
| adomgi | your, courteous |
| magi | his/her |
| eikhoigi | our |
| lairik | book |
| yum | house |
| Romagi | Roma's |

### Put ownership before the object

`Madu Romagi yumni` means “That is Roma's house.” You can exchange the owner without changing the noun: `Madu eigi yumni`, “That is my house.” With a book, `Masi adomgi lairikni` means “This is your book,” addressing the listener courteously.

Manipuri third-person possession does not force the English his/her distinction in magi. Context identifies the person. Do not add a gender ending to lairik or yum because the owner is a woman. Nor does the book require a different possessive construction simply because it is an object rather than a building.

These examples use the genitive forms shown in the table. The suffix has phonological variants with other words, so later vocabulary may show ki rather than gi. Learn the correct attached form with new consonant-final nouns instead of forcing one spelling everywhere. The distinction is about sound and construction, not masculine and feminine ownership.

A possessive phrase can be embedded inside a negative identification too: `Masi eigi lairik natte`, “This is not my book.” That sentence denies ownership of this particular book. It does not claim that the speaker owns no books. Later we use lei and leite for availability or possession statements.

### Common trap

`Ei lairik` leaves the owner unmarked in the pattern taught here. Use eigi. Also keep gi on the owner: moving it to lairik would change the grammatical relationship rather than make the same phrase in another order.

### Practice

Use the possessive forms in the table.

1. Write the phrase: my book.
2. Say: This is our book.
3. Say: That is Roma's house.
4. Say: This is not my book.

***Answers:***

1. `Eigi lairik`
2. `Masi eikhoigi lairikni`
3. `Madu Romagi yumni`
4. `Masi eigi lairik natte`

### Why these answers work

Eigi and eikhoigi identify the owner before lairik. In answer 3, gi attaches to the personal name Roma, while ni attaches to yum. The final answer preserves the possessive phrase but chooses natte, so it corrects the ownership claim rather than describing a different kind of object.

**Retrieve and transfer:** exchange a book between two imaginary people. Make one statement identifying it as yours and a second identifying it as Roma's. Explain why the word for book stays the same.

## Lesson 7: Locate people and things with lei

**Outcome:** say where a person or object is, using a place followed by `-da` and the existence/location verb lei.

### Notice the change of predicate

Compare `Madu yumni` with `Madu Imphalda lei`. One classifies something as a house; the other locates something in Imphal. Which ending answers “What is it?” and which helps answer “Where is it?”

`Ni` identifies; `lei` expresses existence, availability, residence, or location in the illustrated constructions. Use **subject + place-da + lei** for a basic location. `Madu Imphalda lei` means “That is in Imphal.” You need not attach ni after lei to translate English is.

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| lei | is located; exists; lives |
| Imphalda | in Imphal |
| Manipurda | in Manipur |
| Kerala | Kerala |
| Keralada | in Kerala |
| yumda | at/in the house |
| kadaida | where |
| ma | he/she |

### Build a small map

`Ma yumda lei` means “He/she is at home,” or at the house identified in context. `Eigi yum Imphalda lei` locates the speaker's house in Imphal. The possessive phrase eigi yum remains intact, then the location Imphalda follows, and lei completes the statement.

For a thing already under discussion, `Madu yumda lei` says it is in the house. This is a different claim from `Madu yumni`. The same English word is can correspond to different Manipuri constructions, so ask yourself whether you are classifying or locating before choosing a predicate.

The locative has sound-conditioned forms; da is appropriate in the table's illustrated words. You will encounter ta after some other final consonants. Our map uses known place forms rather than asking you to invent suffix spellings for every town immediately. A borrowed place name still needs to fit the language's suffix pattern.

To ask where someone is, one useful fuller question is `Ma kadaida leibage?` The final question form is not the same as the bare affirmative lei. Learn this whole frame now as “Where is he/she?” More advanced information-question morphology lies beyond the initial location statements.

### Common trap

Avoid `Ma yumni` when you mean “He/she is at home.” That is an identification with house, not the required location. The locative suffix and lei together distinguish the intended meaning.

### Practice

Use the illustrated location pattern.

1. Say: He/she is at home.
2. Say: My house is in Imphal.
3. Say: That is in Kerala.
4. Ask: Where is he/she?

***Answers:***

1. `Ma yumda lei`
2. `Eigi yum Imphalda lei`
3. `Madu Keralada lei`
4. `Ma kadaida leibage?`

### Why these answers work

Da marks the location, while lei supplies the predicate. Answer 2 has a possessive noun phrase as subject, showing that the pattern accepts more than a pronoun. The question replaces a known location with kadaida and uses the illustrated question form leibage.

**Retrieve and transfer:** locate an imaginary house in Manipur, then locate a person inside that house. State which noun is the subject in each sentence and which expression supplies the place.

## Lesson 8: Say what is available and what is missing

**Outcome:** use lei and leite to report possession or availability, keeping those claims distinct from identification.

### Think before choosing the negative

Compare “This is not my book” and “I do not have a book.” The first could be true while your own book sits nearby. Which of the two statements requires the availability/possession pattern rather than natte?

For the second, use **possessive expression + noun + lei/leite**. `Eigi lairik lei` says “I have a book,” literally presenting my book as available. `Eigi lairik leite` says “I do not have a book.” The negative counterpart of lei here is leite, not natte.

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| lei | exists; is available |
| leite | does not exist; is unavailable |
| eigi | my |
| eikhoigi | our |
| magi | his/her |
| lairik | book |
| thabak | work |
| khara | some; a little |

### Explain what you have

`Eikhoigi lairik lei` says “We have a book.” The construction uses eikhoigi, the possessive form, rather than the bare pronoun eikhoi. `Magi lairik leite` reports that he or she does not have a book. Neither statement needs a verb that copies English have directly.

For general availability, `Thabak khara lei` means “There is some work.” Khara follows the noun it quantifies. You can use `Lairik khara lei` to say some books are available without first identifying an owner. Exact number is not being asserted; the useful information is that a supply exists.

In a classroom, one person may say `Eigi lairik leite`, explaining a missing resource. Another can answer with a positive availability statement. This exchange works without deciding what kind of ownership is legally involved: classroom availability and possession are practical meanings in the situation. Context determines whether the book is permanently owned, borrowed, or simply at hand.

To negate a location, retain the place: `Ma yumda leite`, “He/she is not at home.” The negative now denies presence at the given location. It does not identify the person as something else. This makes lei/leite useful across three related areas: existence, possession, and location.

### Common trap

Do not replace every English “not” with natte. `Eigi lairik natte` belongs to a different identification structure and is not the illustrated way to report that you have no book. Choose the predicate family before making it negative.

### Practice

Use lei or leite as appropriate.

1. Say: I have a book.
2. Say: We do not have a book.
3. Say: There is some work.
4. Say: He/she is not at home.

***Answers:***

1. `Eigi lairik lei`
2. `Eikhoigi lairik leite`
3. `Thabak khara lei`
4. `Ma yumda leite`

### Why these answers work

The first two use possessive expressions before the available item. The third uses khara after thabak without an owner. In the fourth, yumda remains the location while leite denies presence there. The negative ending is chosen because these are lei constructions.

**Retrieve and transfer:** imagine a study group with some books but no book available to you personally. Make one general availability statement and one personal possession negative. Explain why the two can both be true.

## Lesson 9: Tell where you are from

**Outcome:** introduce a person's origin using `-dagi`, and distinguish origin from current location.

### Think first

You live in Imphal but grew up in Kerala. Would `Ei Imphalda lei` contradict `Ei Keraladagini`? Identify the contribution of da in the first sentence and dagi in the second before deciding.

The sentences can both be true. **Place-da + lei** locates you now, while **place-dagi + ni** identifies your origin. `Ei Keraladagini` means “I am from Kerala.” The source marker dagi and affirmative ni have different jobs inside the final word.

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Imphaldagi | from Imphal |
| Keraladagi | from Kerala |
| Manipurdagi | from Manipur |
| Imphaldagini | am/is/are from Imphal |
| kadaidagino? | from where? |
| adom | you, courteous |
| ma | he/she |
| natte | negative identification |

### Build an introduction with two places

`Ma Manipurdagini` means “He/she is from Manipur.” `Ma Keralada lei` locates that person in Kerala. The first phrase is not a motion verb: it does not say the person is arriving at this instant. Origin and travel can be related in conversation, but they are not identical grammatical claims.

`Adom kadaidagino?` asks “Where are you from?” A suitable response could be `Ei Imphaldagini`. Here the unknown source is replaced with an actual source phrase. The question does not need English-style inversion or an extra word corresponding to are.

To correct an origin claim, say `Ei Imphaldagi natte`, “I am not from Imphal.” Keep the source marker because the correction still concerns origin; replace the affirmative identification with natte. A following sentence can supply the correct place. This is a useful combination of two previously learned operations.

The source marker also has a t-initial variant after certain consonants. The institute material illustrates, for example, different forms after stop-final words. This lesson deliberately uses the place forms in the table. Sound-conditioned suffix spelling deserves attention, but it should not stop you building reliable introductions with a small set of known places.

### Common trap

Do not treat dagi as an optional decoration on da. Changing Imphalda to Imphaldagi changes “in Imphal” to “from Imphal.” In an introduction, that small suffix difference can change the person's entire story.

### Practice

State or ask about origin.

1. Say: I am from Manipur.
2. Say: He/she is from Kerala.
3. Ask courteously: Where are you from?
4. Say: I am not from Imphal.

***Answers:***

1. `Ei Manipurdagini`
2. `Ma Keraladagini`
3. `Adom kadaidagino?`
4. `Ei Imphaldagi natte`

### Why these answers work

The first two answers combine source dagi with identification ni. Answer 3 uses the source question frame. Answer 4 retains dagi but changes the copular portion to natte. All four concern a source, so none can be built by substituting a simple location in da alone.

**Retrieve and transfer:** invent a person who is from Manipur and now lives in Kerala. Produce two sentences that express these facts. Then ask the one question whose answer would reveal the origin rather than the present residence.

## Lesson 10: Count a small set

**Outcome:** count one to ten and place a numeral after an ordinary counted noun in a basic availability statement.

### Notice before counting

Compare `Lairik khara lei` and `Lairik ani lei`. Both concern books. If ani means two, what does it replace, and where does the quantity stand relative to the noun?

A quantity follows the noun in these simple expressions. `Lairik ani lei` means “There are two books.” You are replacing an indefinite quantity, khara, with an exact numeral. English puts two before books; the Manipuri pattern here puts lairik before ani.

| Number | Form |
|---|---|
| 1 | ama |
| 2 | ani |
| 3 | ahum |
| 4 | mari |
| 5 | manga |
| 6 | taruk |
| 7 | taret |
| 8 | nipal |
| 9 | mapal |
| 10 | tara |

### Use counting for a purpose

`Eigi lairik ama lei` says “I have one book.” `Eikhoigi lairik ahum lei` says “We have three books.” The availability construction stays in place; the counted noun phrase becomes more precise. You do not need to build a new tense or person agreement table to change the quantity.

In an identification, a noun with ama can be followed by ni: `Masi lairik amani`, “This is a book.” The earlier `Masi lairikni` remains a useful basic identification. Ama can contribute the sense of one or an individual item, depending on the construction. Do not assume that every English indefinite article must always be translated by ama.

The counting series contains sound distinctions worth practising separately. Manga has the single nasal sound written ng, not an English sequence n plus a hard g. Nipal and mapal refer to eight and nine, so the final pal does not itself tell you which number is intended. Tara is ten, whereas taruk is six; retain the final consonant in the latter.

This lesson counts ordinary objects only. Human numeral expressions and classifiers or more specialised counting patterns require their own attention. Knowing a sequence of number names is useful, but it does not automatically license every possible counted phrase in the language.

### Common trap

Avoid importing English noun order as `ani lairik` in these exercises. Also do not append an English s to lairik. The numeral already specifies the quantity in the practiced phrase.

### Practice

Write the indicated availability statement.

1. Say: There are two books.
2. Say: I have one book.
3. Say: We have three books.
4. Write the numeral word for ten.

***Answers:***

1. `Lairik ani lei`
2. `Eigi lairik ama lei`
3. `Eikhoigi lairik ahum lei`
4. `Tara`

### Why these answers work

Ani, ama, and ahum follow lairik. The owner expressions in answers 2 and 3 remain before the whole counted phrase. Lei then asserts availability. The fourth answer is a number word alone; it does not assert that ten objects exist until it is placed into a sentence.

**Retrieve and transfer:** give your imaginary study group four books and yourself two. Build both possession statements. Then change one number without changing either the possessor or lei, and explain exactly which information changed.

## Lesson 11: Speak about a group

**Outcome:** use plural pronouns and recognise noun plurals with `-shing` without changing a predicate for English-style number agreement.

### Think first

Compare `Ei ojani` and `Eikhoi ojani`. If eikhoi means we, must ni change to match English are? Then compare `lairik` and `lairikshing`: which word is explicitly marked as a plural noun?

The identifying ending remains ni. Manipuri does not make this predicate alternate between an am form and an are form. The subject supplies the participant reference. Meanwhile, shing is a noun plural marker: `lairikshing` refers to books, rather than necessarily one book.

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ei | I |
| eikhoi | we |
| nang | you, familiar singular |
| nakhoi | you, plural |
| ma | he/she |
| makhoi | they |
| lairikshing | books |
| marupshing | friends |

### Keep pronouns and noun plurals distinct

`Makhoi yumda lei` means “They are at home.” The location and lei stay as before; makhoi changes the subject's number. `Eikhoi ojani` means “We are teachers.” The English plural teachers does not require you to mechanically put shing on every Manipuri predicate noun in order to make the sentence grammatical.

`Eikhoigi lairikshing lei` presents our books as available. The possessor eikhoigi comes from the plural pronoun eikhoi plus genitive gi. It is not formed by adding shing to eigi. Pronouns have their own established plural forms, which you should learn as a small set.

With a demonstrative, the plural marker precedes the pointing ending: `lairikshingsi`, “these books”; `lairikshingdu`, “those books.” This lets you identify a specific group. Contrast lairiksi, this book, with lairikshingsi, these books. The final si still points; shing supplies the explicit noun plurality.

Plural marking and numeral phrases interact with discourse and noun type. We do not treat shing as an English s that must occur whenever a translation contains a plural. The previous lesson's lairik ani already makes the number explicit. Here the useful new task is referring to an identified set or changing a pronoun from one participant to several.

### Common trap

Do not use `eishing` for we or `nangshing` as the ordinary plural you in these exercises. The established forms are eikhoi and nakhoi. Also, nakhoi is second person plural, even if an OCR or typographical error in a source labels it otherwise.

### Practice

Use the group forms taught here.

1. Say: We are teachers.
2. Say: They are at home.
3. Write the phrase: these books.
4. Write the phrase: our books.

***Answers:***

1. `Eikhoi ojani`
2. `Makhoi yumda lei`
3. `Lairikshingsi`
4. `Eikhoigi lairikshing`

### Why these answers work

Eikhoi and makhoi identify plural participants while ojani and lei remain unchanged. In answer 3, the order is noun, plural shing, demonstrative si. Answer 4 places the possessive expression before the plural noun. These are different grammatical locations for owner and number information.

**Retrieve and transfer:** locate one friend at home, then change the subject to several friends using a plural pronoun. Next label a nearby set of books. Identify which plural device you used in each case.

## Lesson 12: Ask someone to do something

**Outcome:** give short familiar instructions using illustrated imperative forms, and recognise that dictionary forms are not commands.

### Predict the command

The vocabulary form `paba` means to read. A classroom instruction is `Lairik pau`. Which part of paba has disappeared? Why would attaching an English please to an unchanged dictionary entry fail to teach the actual imperative?

Many citation forms end in ba or pa. For these instructions, work from the verb root and the appropriate imperative form. The basic imperative suffix is u, but sounds at the boundary produce several visible forms. Learn the complete commands below instead of assuming that the dictionary form itself is the root.

| Citation form | Command | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| paba | pau | read |
| phamba | phammu | sit |
| chatpa | chatlu | go |
| lakpa | laku | come |
| haiba | haiyu | say/tell |
| yengba | yengu | look |
| thamba | thammu | keep/put |

### Put the action last

`Lairik pau` means “Read a book.” The object precedes the command. `Yumda chatlu` means “Go home.” The destination marked with da stands before the verb. `Madu yengu` means “Look at that.” You now have a productive connection between the noun phrases from earlier lessons and an action at the end.

The doubled consonant in phammu and thammu is part of the written command. Do not silently remove it because the English translation is short. Likewise, chatlu shows an alternation at the boundary between chat and the imperative; it is not made by preserving the entire citation form chatpa and adding u.

These are direct instructions appropriate to familiar situations, classroom directions, or clearly understood cooperative tasks. They are not automatically polite requests to strangers. Tone, relationship, and additional morphology matter. We introduce explicit courteous forms in the next lesson so you can distinguish structural simplicity from social appropriateness.

An imperative often omits the pronoun because the addressee is understood. `Pau` alone can mean “Read!” in context. Including an object or destination makes the practical instruction clearer without requiring a separate word for you. Omitting the subject here does not mean the action has no participant.

### Common trap

Do not say `Pabau` for the command read, or `Chatpau` for go, by attaching an ending to a dictionary entry. Learn to recognise the citation ending and use the illustrated command. Roots and full vocabulary entries serve different purposes.

### Practice

Give the direct instruction requested.

1. Say: Read a book.
2. Say: Go home.
3. Say: Look at that.
4. Say: Sit.

***Answers:***

1. `Lairik pau`
2. `Yumda chatlu`
3. `Madu yengu`
4. `Phammu`

### Why these answers work

Objects and destinations come before the final imperative. Pau, chatlu, yengu, and phammu are the complete command forms from the table. No affirmative ni or location lei is added because an instruction has its own predicate form.

**Retrieve and transfer:** create a three-step classroom task: come, sit, read a book. Say each as a separate instruction, then explain why the citation endings pa and ba are not retained in the commands.

## Lesson 13: Make a courteous request

**Outcome:** recognise respectful verbal morphology and use a small set of reliable courteous requests.

### Think about the difference

Compare `Haiyu` and `Haibiyu`. Both concern telling someone something, but the second contains bi before the imperative ending. Is bi a new object, a change in tense, or a way of shaping the social relationship?

In these request forms, bi or its sound-conditioned counterpart pi adds honorific or courteous meaning. The basic structure is **root + bi/pi + imperative**. We learn whole forms because the choice of consonant and the surface imperative spelling need attention.

| Form | Meaning or use |
|---|---|
| haiyu | tell/say, direct |
| haibiyu | please tell/say |
| laku | come, direct |
| lakpiyu | please come |
| adom | courteous you |
| oja | teacher; teacher as address |
| khara | a little; some |
| thagatchari | thank you |

### Choose a fitting interaction

`Oja, lakpiyu` means “Teacher, please come.” `Haibiyu` can be a short request for someone to tell you something already understood from the context. These are not statements that someone is currently speaking or coming. They invite an action from the addressee.

`Adom` and the honorific verb form do different work. Adom chooses an address form for the listener; bi/pi changes the verb's social expression. You can omit the pronoun in a request and still retain the courteous verb form. A title such as oja can identify whom you are addressing without becoming the grammatical object of come.

You may encounter further respectful and humble suffixes in real conversation. The institute material also discusses ja in polite speech. We do not present all such suffixes as interchangeable versions of English please. They participate in a larger system that depends on who performs the action and whose interests are being acknowledged.

A useful practical exchange is an invitation followed by thanks: `Oja, lakpiyu`; later, `Thagatchari`. The thanks is a conventional expression, not a result of adding ni to an English thank verb. Treat it as a complete form and focus on where it fits socially.

### Common trap

Do not take the bi of haibiyu and insert it anywhere in a sentence. It occurs inside the verbal form in the illustrated order. Nor should you infer that a direct imperative becomes courteous merely because its English gloss is written with “please.” The Manipuri form itself must support the intended register.

### Practice

Use the courteous forms in the table.

1. Say: Please tell.
2. Address a teacher: Please come.
3. Give the direct form meaning: Tell.
4. Thank the person.

***Answers:***

1. `Haibiyu`
2. `Oja, lakpiyu`
3. `Haiyu`
4. `Thagatchari`

### Why these answers work

Haibiyu contains the honorific element before the imperative ending, whereas haiyu is the direct form. Lakpiyu illustrates the pi variant in a respectful request to come. Oja is a form of address separated by a comma. The final answer is the conventional expression of thanks.

**Retrieve and transfer:** imagine asking a teacher to come closer and say something again. Use the two courteous forms as separate requests. Then explain which verb-internal element distinguishes the telling request from its direct counterpart.

## Lesson 14: Describe everyday actions

**Outcome:** put an object before a verb and use a small set of ordinary present or habitual action forms.

### Think first

Compare `Ei chak chai` and `Ei lairik pai`. The first says “I eat rice”; the second “I read books.” Which position contains the thing acted on? Which final element changes when you switch activities?

The ordinary order is **subject + object + verb**. The source describes the final i forms as appropriate for current or habitual action, depending on context. Here we practise habitual readings with a small reliable set: cha plus i gives chai, pa plus i gives pai, and thak plus i gives thaki.

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| chak | cooked rice; a meal in context |
| ishing | water |
| lairik | book |
| chai | eats/eat |
| thaki | drinks/drink |
| pai | reads/read |
| tami | studies/learns |
| Manipuri lon | Manipuri language |

### Build useful habits

`Eikhoi ishing thaki` means “We drink water.” `Ma lairik pai` says “He/she reads books.” The verb does not need an English third-person s equivalent when the subject changes from ei to ma. Nor do you move the object after the verb just because the English translation does so.

`Ei Manipuri lon tami` means “I study the Manipuri language.” The language expression fills the object position. This lets you describe the activity you are doing through the course. Lon means language; it is a noun, not an aspect ending. Keeping vocabulary units distinct from suffixes makes longer sentences easier to analyse.

The course spelling separates the independent object from the verb. In `chak chai`, chak is what is eaten, and chai is the eating predicate. In `ishing thaki`, ishing is the water and thaki is the drinking predicate. Rice and water use different verbs here, so do not build both from one generic English consume equivalent.

These bare object examples express ordinary activities. Some objects receive case marking, especially when person reference or specificity matters. We will introduce a controlled object marker later. The immediate goal is a dependable action frame, not a claim that every object in Manipuri is always unmarked.

### Common trap

Do not use the citation form chaba or paba as the complete finite statement here. Those forms are useful for dictionary lookup and other constructions, but our habitual predicates are chai and pai. Also avoid adding ni after every finite action verb.

### Practice

State the habit with the illustrated verb.

1. Say: I eat rice.
2. Say: We drink water.
3. Say: He/she reads books.
4. Say: I study Manipuri.

***Answers:***

1. `Ei chak chai`
2. `Eikhoi ishing thaki`
3. `Ma lairik pai`
4. `Ei Manipuri lon tami`

### Why these answers work

Each answer keeps the object before the finite verb. The choice between chai, thaki, pai, and tami depends on the action, not on English person agreement. The plural pronoun eikhoi in answer 2 does not cause a different drinking ending.

**Retrieve and transfer:** give two habits to a fictional friend: reading and drinking water. Then change the subject to we. Identify what must change and what remains grammatically the same.

## Lesson 15: Say what you do not do

**Outcome:** negate ordinary actions with the illustrated de/te forms and distinguish them from copular and existential negatives.

### Choose a predicate family

You know natte for “is not” and leite for “is not available.” Now compare `Ei lairik pai` and `Ei lairik pade`. If the second means “I do not read books,” does the negative leave the affirmative i in place?

The negative action form replaces the affirmative ending in this simple pattern. Pa plus the illustrated negative gives pade. Learn the contrast as a pair, pai/pade, rather than treating de as an independent English not that can be moved around the clause.

| Affirmative | Negative | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| chai | chade | eats / does not eat |
| pai | pade | reads / does not read |
| tami | tamde | studies / does not study |
| lei | leite | exists / does not exist |
| ojani | oja natte | is a teacher / is not a teacher |
| chak | chak | rice, unchanged object |

### Negate the right claim

`Ei chak chade` says “I do not eat rice.” `Ma lairik pade` says “He/she does not read books.” The subject and object stay in their positions; the predicate changes its polarity. You are not negating the noun by adding an English no before it.

`Eikhoi Manipuri lon tamde` means “We do not study Manipuri.” The object can be a multiword language expression, but it still precedes the negative verb. This is a useful correction when someone assumes your study subject incorrectly. You can follow with a positive statement about another activity if its vocabulary is known.

Put three negatives side by side: `Ei oja natte`, “I am not a teacher”; `Eigi lairik leite`, “I do not have a book”; `Ei lairik pade`, “I do not read books.” They describe different facts. A person could have a book but not read it; they could read books without being a teacher. The grammar keeps these meanings apart.

The action-negative suffix has sound-conditioned variants. We practise chade, pade, and tamde directly, while preserving leite as the established negative of lei. Do not take one visible spelling and attach it to every possible root. A dependable vocabulary includes the common finite forms as well as dictionary entries.

### Common trap

Avoid `Ei lairik pai de`, which keeps the affirmative predicate as though de were English not. Also avoid natte for the reading action in this frame. Choose the verb's negative form, not a negative learned from a different construction.

### Practice

Negate the specified action or availability claim.

1. Say: I do not eat rice.
2. Say: He/she does not read books.
3. Say: We do not study Manipuri.
4. Say: I do not have a book.

***Answers:***

1. `Ei chak chade`
2. `Ma lairik pade`
3. `Eikhoi Manipuri lon tamde`
4. `Eigi lairik leite`

### Why these answers work

The first three use action negatives. Answer 4 instead uses a possessive expression and the negative availability verb leite. Its subject-like owner expression is eigi, not ei, because it belongs to the possession construction introduced earlier.

**Retrieve and transfer:** make one positive habit and one negative habit for yourself. Then state that a book is unavailable to you. Explain why the availability sentence chooses a different negative from the action sentence.

## Lesson 16: Describe an action in progress

**Outcome:** contrast ordinary habits with activities happening now, using the progressive ri/li forms.

### Think first

Compare `Ei lairik pai` with `Ei lairik pari`. The first can describe your reading habit. If the second answers a question about what you are doing right now, what additional information does the r-bearing ending contribute?

The progressive presents an activity as ongoing. The institute description gives ri and li variants added to verb roots. After the vowel-final root pa, the form is pari. After other roots, consonant changes may produce li. Learn both the meaning and the complete form rather than adding an English ing equivalent to an already finite verb.

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| houjik | now |
| pari | is/am/are reading |
| chari | is/am/are eating |
| chatli | is/am/are going |
| shannari | is/am/are playing |
| shuri | is/am/are washing |
| phi | cloth/clothing |
| yumda | at/to home, according to the verb |

### Place time and objects before the verb

`Ei houjik lairik pari` means “I am reading a book now.” `Ma chak chari` means “He/she is eating rice.” The action remains sentence-final. Houjik explicitly anchors the activity to now; the progressive form shows that you are presenting it from inside its course.

`Eikhoi yumda chatli` means “We are going home.” Compare `Eikhoi yumda lei`, “We are at home.” The place phrase itself does not tell you whether the participants are already there or moving toward it. The predicate supplies that distinction. This is why learning a place suffix separately from the verb is so useful.

`Ei phi shuri` says “I am washing clothes.” It introduces a practical new action while using the familiar subject-object-verb frame. `Makhoi shannari` says “They are playing”; no direct object is needed to make that statement complete in context.

The ordinary i forms also have present uses, so the contrast is not a rigid claim that i always means habitual and ri always corresponds exactly to one English tense. The useful starting distinction is whether you choose to profile an activity as ongoing. Real conversation adds context and other suffixes to make finer aspectual distinctions.

### Common trap

Do not produce `pairi` by adding ri to the finite form pai. Start with the root pa and build pari. Likewise, chatli is a learned surface form, not proof that li can be attached indiscriminately to every verb root.

### Practice

Describe the ongoing activity.

1. Say: I am reading a book now.
2. Say: He/she is eating rice.
3. Say: We are going home.
4. Say: I am washing clothes.

***Answers:***

1. `Ei houjik lairik pari`
2. `Ma chak chari`
3. `Eikhoi yumda chatli`
4. `Ei phi shuri`

### Why these answers work

Each answer uses a progressive form rather than the ordinary habitual form. Pari and chari illustrate ri after vowel-final roots. Chatli illustrates the l form with the go root. Objects precede the verb, while the motion example uses a destination instead of a direct object.

**Retrieve and transfer:** describe a friend reading now and yourself playing now. Then turn only the reading sentence into an ordinary habit. Identify the predicate change without moving the object.

## Lesson 17: Make a plan for tomorrow

**Outcome:** state a future action with gani or kani and add a useful time word.

### Notice before planning

You know `Ei lairik pai`, “I read books.” Compare it with `Ei hayeng lairik pagani`, “I will read a book tomorrow.” Which part locates the action on a calendar, and which part marks the future predicate?

Hayeng supplies tomorrow; gani gives the future form after the root pa. The simple pattern is **subject + time/place/object + root-gani/kani**. The choice between g and k depends on the sound environment. We begin with complete illustrated verb forms rather than a universal spelling rule.

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| hayeng | tomorrow |
| ngasi | today |
| pagani | will read |
| chagani | will eat |
| chatkani | will go |
| igani | will write |
| chithi | letter |
| yumda | home, as destination |

### Give the plan a specific action

`Ei hayeng yumda chatkani` means “I will go home tomorrow.” `Ma chithi igani` means “He/she will write a letter.” In the second example, i is the write root, so igani is not merely the habitual suffix i placed before a random ending. The same written vowel can participate in different words and grammatical structures.

`Eikhoi hayeng lairik pagani` gives a shared reading plan. The verb does not change because the subject is plural. `Ei chak chagani` says “I will eat rice.” The object remains before the verb, just as it did in the ordinary present and progressive forms.

A time expression makes the plan concrete, but the future form can stand without an explicit tomorrow when the conversation already establishes the time. Conversely, hayeng does not replace the grammatical future ending in the controlled pattern we are practising. Keep both pieces when you need to communicate both the time and the intended event clearly.

Manipuri has other future-related forms that express intention, prediction, certainty, or an unfolding future situation. We do not treat gani as the only imaginable way to talk about later time. It is a productive simple foundation, and learning its boundaries is better than reducing the whole future system to one English translation.

### Common trap

Do not attach gani to the dictionary form paba or to the ordinary finite pai. The practiced future of read is pagani. Do not force `chatgani` either: the illustrated go form is chatkani, showing that consonant conditions matter.

### Practice

State the future plan.

1. Say: I will read a book tomorrow.
2. Say: We will go home tomorrow.
3. Say: He/she will write a letter.
4. Say: I will eat rice.

***Answers:***

1. `Ei hayeng lairik pagani`
2. `Eikhoi hayeng yumda chatkani`
3. `Ma chithi igani`
4. `Ei chak chagani`

### Why these answers work

Hayeng anchors the first two plans to tomorrow. Pagani, chatkani, igani, and chagani are built from their roots rather than from citation forms. The subject, object, and destination positions remain familiar even though the temporal interpretation changes.

**Retrieve and transfer:** make two plans for the same tomorrow, one involving motion and one involving a letter. Then change the subject from yourself to Roma. Explain why the future ending itself need not change for her gender.

## Lesson 18: Decline a plan and stop an action

**Outcome:** distinguish a future negative from a negative command using a small set of explicit forms.

### Think about the participants

“I will not eat” reports the speaker's future behaviour. “Do not eat” tries to control the listener's behaviour. Could one negative ending safely express both purposes? Compare charoi with chaganu before reading on.

`Charoi` is a future negative, “will not eat.” `Chaganu` is a prohibition, “Do not eat.” The first belongs to a statement; the second to a negative instruction. They share the root cha but differ in their endings and communicative function.

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| charoi | will not eat |
| paroi | will not read |
| chaganu | do not eat |
| touganu | do not do |
| chau | eat, direct command |
| tou | do, direct command |
| hayeng | tomorrow |
| chak | rice |

### Choose between refusing and prohibiting

`Ei hayeng chak charoi` means “I will not eat rice tomorrow.” This can report a plan without ordering anyone else to behave the same way. `Chak chaganu` means “Do not eat rice.” It addresses a listener and is a direct prohibition, whose social appropriateness depends on the situation.

`Ei hayeng lairik paroi` says “I will not read a book tomorrow.” Compare the positive `Ei hayeng lairik pagani`. The future negative does not preserve positive gani. As in earlier negative families, learn a contrast rather than attaching a generic not to an affirmative word.

`Madu touganu` means “Do not do that.” Madu refers back to an action under discussion. A short prohibition can be useful in a clear cooperative task, but it can sound abrupt. This lesson teaches its grammar without claiming that a direct negative imperative is the best response in every social setting.

The future negative has roi and loi variants, and prohibition also has phonological variation. We restrict production to vowel-final roots cha, pa, and tou here. This gives you a reliable foundation while avoiding guessed allomorphs for new consonant-final roots. In later study, add each new root's negative form alongside its affirmative.

### Common trap

Do not answer “Will you eat?” with a prohibition addressed to the other person. Charoi and chaganu are not interchangeable. Equally, avoid combining positive future gani with roi in this basic future-negative pattern.

### Practice

Choose the correct type of negative.

1. Say: I will not eat rice tomorrow.
2. Say: I will not read a book tomorrow.
3. Tell a listener: Do not eat rice.
4. Tell a listener: Do not do that.

***Answers:***

1. `Ei hayeng chak charoi`
2. `Ei hayeng lairik paroi`
3. `Chak chaganu`
4. `Madu touganu`

### Why these answers work

The first two contain a speaker subject and a future negative. The last two are prohibitions with an understood addressee. Their final ganu forms target the listener's action. A negative meaning alone is insufficient to choose an ending; the sentence's function matters too.

**Retrieve and transfer:** make a positive eating plan, reverse it into a future negative, then issue a prohibition about eating. Explain how the participant relationship changes in the final version.

## Lesson 19: Report a completed past action

**Outcome:** give a short past-event report using the illustrated khi forms, while recognising that this suffix has a broader aspectual history.

### Predict the time reference

Compare `Ei chak chai` with `Ei chak chakhi`. If the second reports a finished meal, what part identifies the earlier completed action? Is the first chak in the sentence the same grammatical unit as the beginning of chakhi?

The first chak is the independent noun rice. The predicate chakhi contains cha, eat, plus khi. In a simple event report, this form can be translated “ate.” Keeping the word boundary clear prevents you from mistaking repeated letters for repeated grammatical words.

| Form | Meaning in a past report |
|---|---|
| chakhi | ate |
| pakhi | read |
| toukhi | did |
| yengkhi | saw/looked at |
| chithi | letter |
| lairik | book |
| thabak | work |
| madu | that |

### Tell what happened

`Ma lairik pakhi` means “He/she read a book.” `Eikhoi thabak toukhi` means “We did work.” `Ei madu yengkhi` means “I looked at that.” These examples preserve the same subject-object-verb structure used for present and future actions. The event viewpoint changes at the predicate.

The institute material links khi to definite or confirmed actions, and it can occur in combinations referring to future certainty as well. Therefore, “past marker” is a helpful description of the simple uses here, not a complete definition of every occurrence of khi. This foundation practises finished-event reports without claiming that the suffix has only one temporal interpretation everywhere.

Compare a three-way set: `Ei lairik pai`, “I read books”; `Ei lairik pagani`, “I will read a book”; `Ei lairik pakhi`, “I read a book” in a completed past event. English spells the present and past read identically, but the Manipuri predicates remain visibly different. Use the context and suffix together to identify the intended time.

A report need not include an explicit yesterday if the conversation already concerns an earlier event. We keep our vocabulary small and use the completed predicate in a clear past-report context. More complex narrative forms, remote past meanings, and evidential distinctions require additional study beyond these four forms.

### Common trap

Do not put khi after a complete affirmative i form, such as `paikhi`, in the pattern taught here. Start with the root. Also do not infer that English past irregularity controls Manipuri morphology: English ate and did do not require unrelated Manipuri stems.

### Practice

Report the finished event.

1. Say: I ate rice.
2. Say: He/she read a book.
3. Say: We did work.
4. Say: I looked at that.

***Answers:***

1. `Ei chak chakhi`
2. `Ma lairik pakhi`
3. `Eikhoi thabak toukhi`
4. `Ei madu yengkhi`

### Why these answers work

Each predicate uses root plus khi in a completed-event report. The rice noun chak remains separate from the eat predicate chakhi. Changing the subject to ma or eikhoi does not introduce an English-style irregular past form or a gender ending.

**Retrieve and transfer:** imagine finishing a book and a small piece of work. Report both actions with the same subject. Then turn the reading report into a plan for tomorrow, changing the relevant temporal material.

## Lesson 20: Connect a completed action to now

**Outcome:** recognise and use a small set of re/le completion forms without treating them as a universal substitute for every English past tense.

### Think first

A friend offers you a meal, and you want to say you have eaten. Compare `Ei chak chakhi` with `Ei chak chare`. Both can concern eating that is over. Which form would you choose in the present-completion frame practised in this lesson?

The frame here uses re or le after the verb root. `Ei chak chare` means “I have eaten rice.” The source presents these as present-perfect forms, while also describing other discourse uses of re. We focus on completion relevant to the present conversation, not a mechanical translation of every English have.

| Form | Meaning in this frame |
|---|---|
| chare | has/have eaten |
| pare | has/have read |
| chatle | has/have gone |
| yengle | has/have seen/looked |
| lairik | book |
| yumda | home, as destination |
| madu | that |
| ei | I |

### Give a current update

`Ma lairik pare` says “He/she has read a book.” `Eikhoi yumda chatle` says “We have gone home.” The latter is an update about completed motion, not the same as simply asserting `Eikhoi yumda lei`, “We are at home.” Context determines which information the listener needs.

`Ei madu yengle` means “I have seen that.” The l form appears in yengle and chatle, while vowel-final roots in chare and pare show r. These surface differences should be learned with the verbs. Do not erase the consonants in pursuit of one visually identical suffix.

English have in “I have eaten” is an auxiliary, while “I have a book” is a possession statement. Manipuri keeps these patterns distinct: `Ei chak chare` is completion; `Eigi lairik lei` is possession. Translating the English word have directly in both sentences would obscure their different grammatical structures.

Completion negatives have additional forms and meanings, including cessation in some environments. We therefore do not ask you to manufacture a negative by attaching de to chare or chatle. The earlier simple negatives remain useful, but they are not automatically a complete account of perfect or cessation meanings.

### Common trap

Do not use lei as an auxiliary copied from English have and append it to a past form. The illustrated completion is carried by the verbal form itself. Also, do not assume that chare and chakhi are interchangeable in every discourse simply because both may translate with an English past expression.

### Practice

Give the current completion update.

1. Say: I have eaten rice.
2. Say: He/she has read a book.
3. Say: We have gone home.
4. Say: I have seen that.

***Answers:***

1. `Ei chak chare`
2. `Ma lairik pare`
3. `Eikhoi yumda chatle`
4. `Ei madu yengle`

### Why these answers work

The first two use the r form after vowel-final roots. The latter two use illustrated l forms. None contains the possession expression eigi because the English have translations describe completed events, not ownership. The destination in answer 3 still precedes the motion predicate.

**Retrieve and transfer:** respond to an offer of food with a completion statement. Then separately report possession of a book. Explain why English have appears in both translations even though the Manipuri constructions differ.

## Lesson 21: Say what you want to do

**Outcome:** express a desired action with ningi, keeping desire distinct from a definite future plan.

### Think first

Compare `Ei yumda chatkani` and `Ei yumda chatningi`. One states a future going event; the other says the speaker wants to go. Could someone want to go home and still be unable to do so? Use that possibility to separate the two meanings.

The desire construction combines a verb root with ning and the ordinary affirmative ending. `Chatningi` means “want to go.” In the pattern **subject + desired action-ningi**, the action remains inside the final predicate. There is no need to introduce an English to before the root.

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| chatningi | want/wants to go |
| yengningi | want/wants to see/look |
| tamningi | want/wants to study/learn |
| chaningi | want/wants to eat |
| yumda | home, as destination |
| lairik | book |
| Manipuri lon | Manipuri language |
| chak | rice |

### State a preference without promising

`Ei Manipuri lon tamningi` means “I want to study Manipuri.” It describes a wish, not a certificate of current ability. `Ma chak chaningi` means “He/she wants to eat rice.” The person who wants is also the understood person who would perform the desired action in these simple examples.

`Eikhoi lairik yengningi` means “We want to look at a book.” This can be useful when browsing materials before deciding what to read. Compare it with `Eikhoi lairik pagani`, “We will read a book.” Looking, reading, wanting, and planning are separate contributions, even if they occur during the same visit.

The source treats this construction as a combination involving the wish element ning. Learn the assembled form from the root: chat plus ning plus i gives chatningi. Do not add ningi after the already completed future chatkani. That would not be the elementary desire pattern you have learned.

English want also occurs with nouns and with clauses involving different subjects. This lesson does not claim that the same simple construction automatically covers “I want you to go” or every noun-object preference. Our productive scope is one participant wanting to perform a known action. That still allows practical recombination across motion, learning, eating, and looking.

### Common trap

Do not treat chatningi as a stronger promise than chatkani. It may communicate desire without commitment or feasibility. In conversation, a listener may need a follow-up about the actual plan. Grammatical meaning helps avoid a practical misunderstanding.

### Practice

Express the desired action.

1. Say: I want to go home.
2. Say: We want to study Manipuri.
3. Say: He/she wants to eat rice.
4. Say: I want to look at a book.

***Answers:***

1. `Ei yumda chatningi`
2. `Eikhoi Manipuri lon tamningi`
3. `Ma chak chaningi`
4. `Ei lairik yengningi`

### Why these answers work

Each final predicate contains the desired action plus ningi. The objects and destination occupy their usual positions before the verb. The subject can be singular or plural without changing the desire ending for English-style agreement. None of these statements by itself guarantees that the action will happen.

**Retrieve and transfer:** make one wish and one definite future plan involving different activities. Then exchange the predicates and explain how the speaker's commitment has changed even if the vocabulary remains familiar.

## Lesson 22: Describe a place or object

**Outcome:** place descriptive forms before a noun and distinguish a descriptive phrase from a complete predication.

### Notice two jobs

Compare `phajaba yum` and `Yumsi phajei`. Both concern a beautiful house, but only one is a full statement about this house. Which expression contains the noun-attached demonstrative, and which uses a descriptive form to modify the noun?

`Phajaba yum` means “a beautiful house.” The descriptive form precedes the noun. `Yumsi phajei` means “This house is beautiful.” In the second, phajei is the predicate. The difference resembles the earlier distinction between a phrase and a complete sentence, but now the descriptive word also changes form.

| Form | Meaning or use |
|---|---|
| phajaba | beautiful, before a noun |
| phajei | is beautiful, predicate |
| aphaba | good, before a noun |
| achaoba | big, before a noun |
| apikpa | small, before a noun |
| ariba | old, before a noun |
| yamna | very; much |
| yum | house |

### Make a richer identification

`Masi phajaba yumni` means “This is a beautiful house.” The descriptive phrase phajaba yum becomes the identifying predicate and receives ni at its end. `Madu apikpa yumni` means “That is a small house.” You are not attaching ni to every modifier separately.

`Masi ariba lairikni` says “This is an old book.” `Madu achaoba yum natte` says “That is not a big house.” The negative keeps the descriptive phrase together before natte. In each case, the modifier narrows the kind of object being identified.

For a direct property statement, `Yumsi yamna phajei` means “This house is very beautiful.” Yamna strengthens the description before the predicate. It does not mark a noun plural here. Context and grammatical position help distinguish the related quantity and degree uses of a word such as yamna.

Many descriptive forms in Manipuri are closely related to verbal morphology. The ba/pa forms are therefore not merely English adjectives with extra letters. We practise specific modifiers and one clear predicative pair rather than inventing a finite form for every adjective in the table. A new descriptive word should be learned with its attested sentence patterns.

### Common trap

Do not assume that phajaba can replace phajei unchanged in every full property statement. Conversely, do not insert the finite phajei directly before yum as though it were automatically the same modifying phrase. The form and its grammatical job belong together.

### Practice

Use the descriptive construction requested.

1. Write the phrase: a beautiful house.
2. Say: This is an old book.
3. Say: That is a small house.
4. Say: This house is very beautiful.

***Answers:***

1. `Phajaba yum`
2. `Masi ariba lairikni`
3. `Madu apikpa yumni`
4. `Yumsi yamna phajei`

### Why these answers work

The first three place a modifier before its noun. Answers 2 and 3 then use ni to identify the subject. The fourth instead has a definite subject yumsi and the finite property predicate phajei, strengthened by yamna. These are related ways of describing, not identical word sequences.

**Retrieve and transfer:** identify a large house and an old book. Then choose the house and describe its beauty in a separate property statement. Explain why the final predicate differs between identification and direct description.

## Lesson 23: Keep the person roles clear

**Outcome:** recognise subject, object, and recipient marking in a few practical help and giving expressions.

### Think first

Compare `ei`, `eibu`, and `eingonda`. All refer to the speaker. If one is used in “help me” and another in “give it to me,” why might a language need more than one form corresponding to English me?

A participant can occupy different roles. Eibu is an object form of ei, while eingonda is a recipient/directional form in the illustrated giving expression. The source analyses the latter with ngon before da for singular personal pronouns. This is not simply the location form you would predict by attaching da directly to ei.

| Form | Meaning or role |
|---|---|
| ei | I, ordinary pronoun |
| eina | I, explicitly subject-marked |
| eibu | me, object |
| nangbu | you, familiar object |
| eingonda | to me |
| nangonda | to you, familiar |
| mateng pangba | to help |
| piraku | give toward the speaker, direct request |

### Build a helpful exchange

`Eibu mateng khara pangu` means “Help me a little.” Eibu identifies the person receiving help; mateng khara expresses a little assistance; pangu completes the direct request. The phrase mateng pangba is a useful lexical combination, so learn its parts in their actual order rather than translating help into an unrelated single English-shaped verb.

`Eingonda piraku` means “Give it to me,” in the illustrated speaker-oriented request. The rak element contributes movement toward the speaker in this kind of verb form. It is not the same as the source marker dagi on a place name. The object can be omitted when both people know what is being passed.

You will also see explicit subject forms such as eina and mana, built with na. `Eina lairik pai` is a reading statement with the subject marked. Earlier examples used plain pronouns where the subject relationship was clear. Do not infer that every unmarked noun is necessarily an object or that all subjects in every context must carry the same visible marker.

Full case distribution interacts with animacy, definiteness, discourse, and sound alternations. Our goal here is modest but useful: recognise three distinct participant roles and use verified personal forms in a real exchange. This avoids treating all English object pronouns as one universal Manipuri form.

### Common trap

Do not substitute `eida` for eingonda in the giving frame merely because da marked places earlier. Singular personal recipients use the additional ngon element in this construction. Also do not add bu to a whole English sentence; it belongs to the relevant noun or pronoun.

### Practice

Use the personal forms from this lesson.

1. Say: Help me a little.
2. Say: Give it to me.
3. Write the object form meaning: me.
4. Say “I read books” with explicitly marked subject eina.

***Answers:***

1. `Eibu mateng khara pangu`
2. `Eingonda piraku`
3. `Eibu`
4. `Eina lairik pai`

### Why these answers work

The first answer marks the helped person with bu. The second uses the recipient form containing ngon plus da. The third is a pronoun form alone, not a complete clause. The fourth demonstrates subject na while keeping object and verb in their established order.

**Retrieve and transfer:** imagine borrowing a book from a friend. Use the request to give it to you, then thank the friend. Separately ask for a little help. Identify why the two requests use different forms of the first-person pronoun.

## Lesson 24: Link actions in a sensible order

**Outcome:** connect an earlier action to a later one with raga/laga, and use a simple contrasting conjunction.

### Predict the order

Compare `Ei chak charaga lairik pagani` with two isolated future statements about eating and reading. If charaga means “after eating,” what relationship becomes explicit in the linked sentence that was not guaranteed by the isolated statements?

The linked construction orders the events. **Earlier action-raga/laga + final predicate** presents one action as preceding another. The final predicate carries the main finite form. In the example, the meaning is “After eating rice, I will read a book.” Reading is the future action; eating supplies its preceding condition in time.

| Form | Meaning or use |
|---|---|
| charaga | after eating |
| paraga | after reading |
| chatlaga | after going |
| pagani | will read |
| chagani | will eat |
| aduga | and then |
| adubu | but |
| hayeng | tomorrow |

### Connect familiar actions

`Ei lairik paraga chak chagani` means “After reading a book, I will eat rice.” The order is now the reverse of the opening example. Both sentences contain the same two activities, but their temporal connection differs. This is a useful test of whether you are reading the endings rather than just spotting vocabulary.

A direct instruction can also follow a linked earlier action: `Lairik paraga phammu`, “After reading the book, sit.” The source illustrates paraga before an imperative. The person addressed is understood as performing both actions in this controlled pattern. More complex clauses with changing subjects need additional care.

For contrast, use adubu between two full statements: `Ei yumda chatningi, adubu ei chatkani` would contrast a wish and plan poorly because the meanings naturally align. A meaningful contrast is `Ei lairik yengningi, adubu eigi lairik leite`, “I want to look at a book, but I do not have a book.” The second clause explains an obstacle to the first.

Aduga can link discourse with “and then,” but it does not by itself replace every grammatical connection made by raga/laga. English and often leaves the exact relation vague; a nonfinite sequence form can make the order clearer. Choose the relation you want the listener to understand.

### Common trap

Do not attach raga after the full future pagani. The earlier-action form here is paraga, built from pa. Also avoid choosing adubu merely because you need a joining word: the clauses should have a genuine contrast or obstacle relationship.

### Practice

Link the actions in the specified order.

1. Say: After eating rice, I will read a book.
2. Say: After reading a book, I will eat rice.
3. Instruct: After reading the book, sit.
4. Write the word meaning: but.

***Answers:***

1. `Ei chak charaga lairik pagani`
2. `Ei lairik paraga chak chagani`
3. `Lairik paraga phammu`
4. `Adubu`

### Why these answers work

Charaga and paraga place the first action before the second. The main future ending belongs to the final predicate in answers 1 and 2. Answer 3 ends in an imperative instead, showing that a preceding-action expression can frame an instruction as well as a statement.

**Retrieve and transfer:** make a two-step plan for a study visit, then reverse the order of its activities. Finally add a separate obstacle statement with adubu. Check that the contrast adds information rather than merely joining unrelated sentences.

## Lesson 25: Manage a short study visit

**Outcome:** combine identification, location, possession, requests, and plans in a coherent exchange, and recognise what still needs further study.

### Think before building the dialogue

A visitor knows who you are but cannot find the book you intend to read. Which question is useful now: an identity question, a location question, or a question about tomorrow? Decide based on the missing information, not on which pattern is easiest to remember.

A good exchange responds to the actual gap. You can identify the book with `Masi eigi lairikni`, locate an available item with a place-da plus lei statement, or report that no book is available with `Eigi lairik leite`. These are different contributions, even when all concern the same study visit.

| Communicative job | Useful form |
|---|---|
| greet | Khurumjari |
| identify yourself | Ei Romani |
| invite courteously | Oja, lakpiyu |
| report availability | Eigi lairik lei |
| request the item | Eingonda piraku |
| state a learning wish | Ei Manipuri lon tamningi |
| describe ongoing reading | Ei lairik pari |
| thank | Thagatchari |

### Follow a coherent exchange

Imagine Roma showing a teacher a study space. Roma opens with `Khurumjari` and invites the teacher with `Oja, lakpiyu`. When the book is identified, `Masi eigi lairikni` clarifies whose it is. The teacher can then refer to a known object without repeating its entire description.

Roma says `Ei Manipuri lon tamningi`, explaining the wish to learn. A current activity can be reported with `Ei lairik pari`; a later plan uses `Ei hayeng lairik pagani`. The distinction between wanting, doing now, and planning prevents the exchange from becoming a list of roughly related English translations.

If an item needs passing, `Eingonda piraku` supplies a direct practical request, followed by `Thagatchari`. Remember that the direct request and the courteous invitation have different social forms. A real speaker may choose additional honorific morphology according to the relationship; a small foundation cannot replace that social judgement.

You can now recombine several real Manipuri structures, but this is not complete command of the language. Native-script reading, lexical tone, detailed pronunciation, sound-conditioned suffix alternations, full question paradigms, and richer aspect and honorific systems remain study goals. The Roman notation is deliberately a reading aid rather than a claim of full phonemic transcription.

### Common trap

Do not measure progress only by how many endings you can append. A successful utterance needs the right predicate family, appropriate participant marking, and a relevant conversational purpose. More suffixes do not automatically make a sentence more accurate or more courteous.

### Practice

Produce the requested contribution to the study visit.

1. Say: This is my book.
2. Say: I want to study Manipuri.
3. Say: I am reading a book now.
4. Say: After eating rice, we will read a book.

***Answers:***

1. `Masi eigi lairikni`
2. `Ei Manipuri lon tamningi`
3. `Ei houjik lairik pari`
4. `Eikhoi chak charaga lairik pagani`

### Why these answers work

The first identifies ownership with a possessive phrase and ni. The second chooses a desire predicate; the third chooses a progressive predicate and explicit now. The fourth combines a plural subject, an earlier-action expression, and a final future predicate. Every answer draws on an earlier construction while serving a different conversational job.

**Retrieve and transfer:** create a six-turn study visit from memory. Include a greeting, a courteous invitation, a question, a relevant answer, one wish or plan, and thanks. Then change the available object or the participants and rebuild the exchange without copying the original word order from English.
