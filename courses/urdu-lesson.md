# Urdu: A pattern-first foundation

Editorial status: original AI-assisted draft; fluent-speaker review pending.

This course uses modern standard conversational Urdu, with respectful آپ as the default for an unfamiliar adult. The main script is Urdu's Perso-Arabic orthography, normally displayed in a Nastaʿlīq style. Font appearance varies by device; a Naskh rendering does not turn the language into Arabic. Urdu has regional and community variation across Pakistan, India and the diaspora. The course avoids treating either heavily Persianised literary diction or Hindi-oriented vocabulary as the only authentic everyday register.

Romanisation uses ā, ī and ū for long vowels; ṭ, ḍ and ṛ for retroflex sounds; kh for خ, gh for غ, and explicit aspirated pairs such as th and bh where appropriate. A combining tilde marks vowel nasalisation. Thus maĩ and hū̃ contain nasal vowels. This readable system does not encode every historical spelling distinction. Native spelling is always the primary written form.

### References and review notes

Northwestern University's [Zer o Zabar](https://openbooks.library.northwestern.edu/zerozabar/) was consulted for script structure and its chapter on [pe, gāf, alif and lām](https://openbooks.library.northwestern.edu/zerozabar/chapter/pe-gaf-alif-lam/) for joining behaviour and alif-madd. The [Urdu resources bibliography](https://openbooks.library.northwestern.edu/zerozabar/chapter/resources-for-further-study/) identifies further grammars and dictionaries. The course's explanations, examples and exercises are original. Fluent review should check regional conversational preferences and the romanisation of loanwords before treating this as a publication-ready textbook.

## Lesson 1: Say who you are

### Outcome and thinking point

Introduce yourself and describe your present condition. English “I am” differs from “you are”. Before reading the Urdu examples, predict whether the language might also connect each person with a particular form of be. Keep that small linking word at the end rather than copying English word order.

`میں طالب علم ہوں۔` - `Maĩ tālib-e ilm hū̃.` means “I am a student.” `آپ طالب علم ہیں۔` - `Āp tālib-e ilm haĩ.` respectfully says “You are a student.” There is no separate indefinite article in this neutral identification. The identity expression sits between the person and the linking word.

### Your first building blocks

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| میں | maĩ | I |
| آپ | āp | you, respectful |
| وہ | voh | he, she, that |
| طالب علم | tālib-e ilm | student |
| استاد | ustād | teacher |
| خوش | khush | happy |
| ہوں | hū̃ | am |
| ہیں | haĩ | are, plural or respectful |
| ہے | hai | is |

### Build a sentence, then change one part

`میں خوش ہوں۔` - `Maĩ khush hū̃.` means “I am happy.” `وہ خوش ہے۔` - `Voh khush hai.` means “He or she is happy.” Urdu's ordinary third-person pronoun does not itself encode male versus female. Context supplies the reference, and other parts of some sentences will reveal grammatical gender later.

Urdu is read from right to left. The letters change shape according to how they join, but they retain their identity. Learn a familiar whole word such as `آپ` and notice its letters rather than treating each visual shape as a different alphabet character. The long initial vowel has alif-madd, `آ`; its mark is meaningful, not decorative punctuation.

`طالب علم` is a useful student expression. A female student may also be identified more specifically with `طالبہ` - `tāliba`. For this first pattern, the goal is a clear identity sentence, not an assumption that every occupation has one universally preferred gender-neutral form. Ask speakers how they refer to themselves in their own educational context.

### Common trap

Do not use singular `ہے` after respectful `آپ`. Respectful agreement is grammatically plural even when the listener is one person. Also distinguish `ہیں`, with nasalisation, from singular `ہے`; the difference is not an optional flourish.

### Practice

Build a complete Urdu sentence.

1. I am happy.
2. I am a student.
3. You are happy. (respectful)
4. That person is happy.

***Answers:***

1. `میں خوش ہوں۔` - `Maĩ khush hū̃.`
2. `میں طالب علم ہوں۔` - `Maĩ tālib-e ilm hū̃.`
3. `آپ خوش ہیں۔` - `Āp khush haĩ.`
4. `وہ خوش ہے۔` - `Voh khush hai.`

### Why these answers work

The first two preserve first-person agreement while changing the description. The respectful listener selects `ہیں`, and the ordinary singular third person selects `ہے`. No English article needs a separate Urdu word in these identifications.

### Retrieve and recombine

Imagine joining a class. Introduce yourself, describe yourself as happy, and identify another learner. Say which part changes when you move from yourself to the other person, and which part changes when you address someone respectfully.

## Lesson 2: Identify things and ask what they are

### Outcome and thinking point

Point to something and ask its identity. In English, the question “What is this?” rearranges the statement “This is tea.” Can Urdu simply put a question word into the unknown identity slot? Try the substitution before reading the answer.

`یہ کیا ہے؟` - `Yeh kyā hai?` means “What is this?” `یہ چائے ہے۔` - `Yeh chāe hai.` answers “This is tea.” The pointing word stays first and the copula stays last. The question is not a command to reorder every part of the sentence.

### Things within reach

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| یہ | yeh | this, this person |
| وہ | voh | that, that person |
| کیا | kyā | what; yes-no question marker |
| کون | kaun | who |
| کتاب | kitāb | book |
| قلم | qalam | pen |
| پانی | pānī | water |
| چائے | chāe | tea |
| دودھ | dūdh | milk |

### Two uses of کیا

`کیا یہ پانی ہے؟` - `Kyā yeh pānī hai?` means “Is this water?” Here `کیا` introduces the whole proposition rather than replacing an unknown identity. In speech, intonation can also produce a yes-no question without an initial marker. The explicit form is helpful while learning to read.

`وہ کون ہے؟` - `Voh kaun hai?` asks “Who is that?” Select `کون` for a person's identity and `کیا` for a thing's identity. A speaker can of course ask what a person's role is in a suitable context, but the basic distinction gives you a reliable first question.

`یہ قلم ہے۔` - `Yeh qalam hai.` means “This is a pen.” The q in the reading aid corresponds to `ق`, traditionally pronounced farther back than k. Actual pronunciation varies across speakers. Preserve the spelling even when you hear a pronunciation closer to k; a pronunciation variation does not justify rewriting every qāf as kāf.

Urdu often omits short vowel marks in ordinary text. The word's consonants, longer vowels, vocabulary and context help the reader recover the pronunciation. Romanisation here supplies that support, but eventually the native word should be recognised directly rather than deciphered only through English letters.

### Common trap

Do not translate every occurrence of `کیا` as “what”. Its position and sentence structure distinguish an information question from a yes-no question. Also do not assume that a missing written short vowel means no vowel is pronounced.

### Practice

Ask or answer the identification question.

1. What is this?
2. This is a book.
3. Is that milk?
4. Who is that?

***Answers:***

1. `یہ کیا ہے؟` - `Yeh kyā hai?`
2. `یہ کتاب ہے۔` - `Yeh kitāb hai.`
3. `کیا وہ دودھ ہے؟` - `Kyā voh dūdh hai?`
4. `وہ کون ہے؟` - `Voh kaun hai?`

### Why these answers work

The first question replaces the unknown identity; the third checks an entire proposed identity. Book appears without an indefinite article. The last asks about a person and therefore uses the basic person question word.

### Retrieve and recombine

Point to a drink and ask what it is. Ask whether another drink is water, then identify a nearby pen. Finally turn the pointing pattern towards a person and choose the question word that requests their identity.

## Lesson 3: Correct a statement politely

### Outcome and thinking point

Say no, negate a description and make a correction explicit. If a statement ends with a form of be, where can the negative sit without disturbing the person-description structure? Compare positive and negative examples before creating your own correction.

`میں بیمار نہیں ہوں۔` - `Maĩ bīmār nahī̃ hū̃.` means “I am not ill.” The negative `نہیں` comes before the final copula here. `یہ چائے نہیں ہے۔` - `Yeh chāe nahī̃ hai.` means “This is not tea.” The same negative works with a person or an object being described.

### Negation and acknowledgement

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| نہیں | nahī̃ | no, not |
| ہاں | hā̃ | yes |
| جی | jī | respectful acknowledgement particle |
| بیمار | bīmār | ill |
| ٹھیک | ṭhīk | fine, correct |
| تیار | taiyār | ready |
| مصروف | masrūf | busy |
| معاف کیجیے | muāf kījie | excuse me, forgive me |

### A complete correction helps

`کیا آپ تیار ہیں؟` - `Kyā āp taiyār haĩ?` asks “Are you ready?” `جی ہاں۔` - `Jī hā̃.` is a respectful yes; `جی نہیں۔` - `Jī nahī̃.` is a respectful no. If the question itself is negative or confusing, use the complete statement `میں تیار ہوں۔` or `میں تیار نہیں ہوں۔` to make your meaning unambiguous.

`وہ مصروف نہیں ہے۔` - `Voh masrūf nahī̃ hai.` means “He or she is not busy.” Respect for a third person can change the agreement to plural `ہیں`; ordinary singular is used here to isolate negation. Social grammar is a choice about the referent, not a mechanical rule that every unseen person must take singular agreement.

The `ھ` in `ٹھیک` participates in an aspirated consonant spelling. Compare this do-chashmī he with other he forms rather than assuming every h-like letter stands for a full separate syllable. Aspiration is an audible puff attached to the consonant; it is not the English th sound in “think”.

### Common trap

`جی` does not always mean yes. It can acknowledge attention, mark respect or appear with a negative reply. Read the rest of the turn. Similarly, the course's full negative clauses show a copula, but everyday speech often shortens predictable material without changing the intended meaning.

### Practice

Give a clear correction or short reply.

1. I am not ill.
2. This is not tea.
3. I am ready.
4. Give a respectful short no.

***Answers:***

1. `میں بیمار نہیں ہوں۔` - `Maĩ bīmār nahī̃ hū̃.`
2. `یہ چائے نہیں ہے۔` - `Yeh chāe nahī̃ hai.`
3. `میں تیار ہوں۔` - `Maĩ taiyār hū̃.`
4. `جی نہیں۔` - `Jī nahī̃.`

### Why these answers work

Negation precedes the copula while preserving person agreement. The affirmative ready sentence contains no negative. The final response combines a respectful particle with the actual negative word, so its meaning cannot be obtained from `جی` alone.

### Retrieve and recombine

Correct a mistaken drink identification and then answer a question about whether you are busy. Give one short reply and one complete reply. Explain when the complete statement is more helpful than a bare yes or no.

## Lesson 4: Choose your form of you

### Outcome and thinking point

Address someone appropriately while maintaining grammatical agreement. English has one general you. Urdu distinguishes relationships through pronouns and their associated verbs. Before choosing a form, ask whether the listener is an unfamiliar adult, a familiar peer or somebody addressed with strong intimacy.

`آپ ٹھیک ہیں۔` - `Āp ṭhīk haĩ.` is respectful. `تم ٹھیک ہو۔` - `Tum ṭhīk ho.` addresses a familiar person or group. `تو` - `tū` exists for very intimate, poetic, devotional or socially marked contexts, and can sound insulting in others. This foundation keeps it out of productive practice.

### Pronouns in context

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| آپ | āp | respectful you |
| تم | tum | familiar you |
| ہم | ham | we |
| یہ | yeh | this person, these |
| وہ | voh | that person, those |
| ہو | ho | are with tum |
| ہیں | haĩ | are, plural or respectful |
| دوست | dost | friend |

### Number and respect overlap

`ہم دوست ہیں۔` - `Ham dost haĩ.` means “We are friends.” `آپ میرے دوست ہیں۔` - `Āp mere dost haĩ.` respectfully says “You are my friend.” The final plural form does not by itself prove there are several listeners. Honorific agreement and numerical plurality share forms.

`وہ استاد ہیں۔` - `Voh ustād haĩ.` can refer respectfully to one teacher. It can also refer to several teachers, depending on context. The near and distant pronouns do not always reveal number in ordinary writing; agreement and the situation help the reader interpret them.

Changing from `آپ` to `تم` is more than changing the dictionary entry for you. In the simple copular pattern, `ہیں` changes to `ہو`. Later, action verbs and imperatives also reflect this distinction. Keep a whole address pattern consistent within an exchange unless the relationship or deliberate tone changes.

Urdu speakers differ in family address habits. Some use respectful forms even with close relatives; others use familiar forms warmly. Observe how people address one another, and let the person concerned guide your choice. A respectful default is useful without claiming that familiarity is inherently rude.

### Common trap

Do not translate `آپ` as “you all” every time or assume `تم` is universally offensive. Number, closeness, age and local convention interact. The dependable beginner rule is to choose respectful `آپ` for an unfamiliar adult and keep its agreement consistent.

### Practice

Use the specified relationship.

1. You are fine. (respectful)
2. You are fine. (familiar)
3. We are friends.
4. That person is a teacher. (respectful reference)

***Answers:***

1. `آپ ٹھیک ہیں۔` - `Āp ṭhīk haĩ.`
2. `تم ٹھیک ہو۔` - `Tum ṭhīk ho.`
3. `ہم دوست ہیں۔` - `Ham dost haĩ.`
4. `وہ استاد ہیں۔` - `Voh ustād haĩ.`

### Why these answers work

The respectful second person and respectful third-person reference use plural-style agreement. Familiar `تم` selects `ہو`, while we selects `ہیں`. The teacher sentence can refer to one respected person because grammatical plurality also carries social meaning.

### Retrieve and recombine

Address a new teacher and then a familiar classmate. State that you are friends with another learner. Explain how you can refer to one teacher using a form that also appears with several people.

## Lesson 5: Describe nouns with gender and number

### Outcome and thinking point

Describe a room and a book, then make them plural. If Urdu adjectives can change their endings, should a woman's room receive a feminine adjective? Focus on the noun being described rather than the person speaking or owning it.

`یہ کمرہ بڑا ہے۔` - `Yeh kamrā baṛā hai.` means “This room is large.” `یہ کتاب بڑی ہے۔` - `Yeh kitāb baṛī hai.` means “This book is large.” Room is grammatically masculine and book feminine. Their grammatical class controls the changing adjective.

### Describing pairs

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| بڑا کمرہ | baṛā kamrā | large room |
| بڑی کتاب | baṛī kitāb | large book |
| چھوٹا | chhoṭā | small, masculine singular |
| چھوٹی | chhoṭī | small, feminine |
| نیا | nayā | new, masculine singular |
| نئی | naī | new, feminine |
| اچھا | achchhā | good, masculine singular |
| اچھی | achchhī | good, feminine |
| خوب صورت | khūb sūrat | beautiful, unchanged |

### Move from one to several

`یہ کمرے بڑے ہیں۔` - `Yeh kamre baṛe haĩ.` means “These rooms are large.” The regular masculine noun ending changes from ā to e, and the adjective follows. `یہ کتابیں بڑی ہیں۔` - `Yeh kitābẽ baṛī haĩ.` means “These books are large.” The feminine noun has a plural form, but the adjective `بڑی` does not acquire an extra plural ending here.

`یہ کتاب خوب صورت ہے۔` - `Yeh kitāb khūb sūrat hai.` means “This book is beautiful.” Many adjectives do not change with gender. Learn a new adjective's behaviour rather than assuming every final sound must be replaced by ī for a feminine noun.

Inside a noun phrase, the adjective usually comes before the noun: `نئی کتاب` is “new book”. As the main description in a sentence, it follows the topic noun before the copula: `کتاب نئی ہے` says “The book is new.” Both arrangements are useful; they perform different jobs within the sentence.

### Common trap

Do not assume a noun's final written letter always reveals its gender. Some useful patterns exist, but nouns such as `کتاب` require learning their class. A phrase such as `اچھی کتاب` gives you the word and its agreement together.

### Practice

Match the adjective to the noun.

1. This room is small.
2. This book is new.
3. These rooms are large.
4. These books are good.

***Answers:***

1. `یہ کمرہ چھوٹا ہے۔` - `Yeh kamrā chhoṭā hai.`
2. `یہ کتاب نئی ہے۔` - `Yeh kitāb naī hai.`
3. `یہ کمرے بڑے ہیں۔` - `Yeh kamre baṛe haĩ.`
4. `یہ کتابیں اچھی ہیں۔` - `Yeh kitābẽ achchhī haĩ.`

### Why these answers work

Room selects masculine forms and book feminine forms. Plural rooms change both noun and adjective; plural books change the noun while the feminine adjective retains its ī form. Plurality still appears in the copula of both plural statements.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe a room, two rooms, a book and several books. Then imagine a different speaker saying the same descriptions. Explain why changing the speaker does not alter the adjectives attached to those objects.

## Lesson 6: Names and ownership

### Outcome and thinking point

Give your name and identify whose object something is. Predict the form of my before masculine room and feminine book. The owner's sex is not the relevant clue; the possessed noun supplies the agreement information.

`میرا نام سارا ہے۔` - `Merā nām Sārā hai.` means “My name is Sara.” `میری کتاب` - `merī kitāb` means “my book”. The word name is masculine, so Sara uses `میرا` in the introduction. It would remain masculine for a different speaker's name.

### Possessive forms

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| میرا | merā | my, masculine singular |
| میری | merī | my, feminine |
| میرے | mere | my, masculine plural or oblique |
| آپ کا | āp kā | your, masculine singular |
| آپ کی | āp kī | your, feminine |
| ہمارا | hamārā | our, masculine singular |
| نام | nām | name |
| کس کا | kis kā | whose, masculine singular |

### Link an owner to a thing

`آپ کا نام کیا ہے؟` - `Āp kā nām kyā hai?` respectfully asks “What is your name?” The sentence ends in singular `ہے` because the subject is the one name. The respectful listener appears inside the possessive phrase, not as the grammatical subject of be.

`یہ سارا کی کتاب ہے۔` - `Yeh Sārā kī kitāb hai.` means “This is Sara's book.” `یہ سارا کا کمرہ ہے۔` - `Yeh Sārā kā kamrā hai.` means “This is Sara's room.” The link after the owner's name agrees with the thing owned. English apostrophe-s does not change, but Urdu kā, kī and ke do.

`یہ کس کی کتاب ہے؟` - `Yeh kis kī kitāb hai?` asks “Whose book is this?” You do not know the owner, yet you can select kī because the book is known. This is an example of solving grammar from available information rather than waiting to know every word in the answer.

### Common trap

Do not select the final copula from the person embedded in a possessive. “Your name” is one name, while “your books” are several books. The head noun of the whole phrase governs the existence or identification statement.

### Practice

Build a name or ownership sentence.

1. My name is Sara.
2. What is your name? (respectful)
3. This is Sara's book.
4. This is my room.

***Answers:***

1. `میرا نام سارا ہے۔` - `Merā nām Sārā hai.`
2. `آپ کا نام کیا ہے؟` - `Āp kā nām kyā hai?`
3. `یہ سارا کی کتاب ہے۔` - `Yeh Sārā kī kitāb hai.`
4. `یہ میرا کمرہ ہے۔` - `Yeh merā kamrā hai.`

### Why these answers work

Name and room select masculine possessives; book selects feminine kī. The owner can be expressed with a first-person possessive, a respectful pronoun plus link, or a name plus link. Each sentence describes one possessed item and therefore uses the singular copula.

### Retrieve and recombine

Introduce yourself, identify your book and identify Sara's room. Ask who owns another book. For each possessive, point to the noun that controls its ending and explain why the owner's gender does not determine the answer.

## Lesson 7: Locate something with a postposition

### Outcome and thinking point

Say where an object or person is. English places in before its noun. Urdu places the corresponding relationship word after the noun phrase. Where should “my room” change if it is prepared to stand before such a word?

`کتاب کمرے میں ہے۔` - `Kitāb kamre mẽ hai.` means “The book is in the room.” The noun `کمرہ` becomes `کمرے` before `میں`. This is its oblique singular form, a grammatical form used before postpositions. It does not necessarily mean several rooms.

### Place vocabulary

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| میں | mẽ | in |
| پر | par | on, at |
| یہاں | yahā̃ | here |
| وہاں | vahā̃ | there |
| کہاں | kahā̃ | where |
| میز | mez | table |
| گھر | ghar | home, house |
| دفتر | daftar | office |
| اسکول | iskūl | school |

### Build the location phrase first

`کتاب میز پر ہے۔` - `Kitāb mez par hai.` means “The book is on the table.” `سارا میرے کمرے میں ہے۔` - `Sārā mere kamre mẽ hai.` means “Sara is in my room.” The possessive becomes `میرے` to agree with the oblique masculine noun. You can build the whole place phrase before placing it in the larger sentence.

`آپ کہاں ہیں؟` - `Āp kahā̃ haĩ?` asks “Where are you?” `میں یہاں ہوں۔` - `Maĩ yahā̃ hū̃.` answers “I am here.” Here and there are already location words; do not mechanically add in after them. `میں گھر پر ہوں۔` - `Maĩ ghar par hū̃.` says “I am at home.” Everyday speech also uses a shorter home expression without `پر`.

The written form `میں` has two familiar uses now: `maĩ`, I, and `mẽ`, in. Ordinary Urdu does not always write enough vowel information to distinguish these without context. A reader interprets the word's grammatical position and surrounding vocabulary. A sentence can contain both: `میں کمرے میں ہوں۔` - `Maĩ kamre mẽ hū̃.`

### Common trap

Do not read every `کمرے` as a plural or every `میں` as I. Script recognition and grammar work together. The same visible form can have more than one grammatical reading, and the postposition is strong evidence for an oblique noun phrase.

### Practice

Locate the person or item.

1. The book is on the table.
2. Sara is in my room.
3. Where are you? (respectful)
4. I am in the room.

***Answers:***

1. `کتاب میز پر ہے۔` - `Kitāb mez par hai.`
2. `سارا میرے کمرے میں ہے۔` - `Sārā mere kamre mẽ hai.`
3. `آپ کہاں ہیں؟` - `Āp kahā̃ haĩ?`
4. `میں کمرے میں ہوں۔` - `Maĩ kamre mẽ hū̃.`

### Why these answers work

Relationship words follow their noun phrases. Room and its possessive visibly change in the oblique form, while table does not show that same masculine ending change. The final sentence contains both pronunciations of `میں`, determined by their different roles.

### Retrieve and recombine

Locate a book first on a table and then in your room. Ask a person where they are, and answer from inside the room yourself. Read the two instances of `میں` aloud with their correct vowels.

## Lesson 8: Describe a daily habit

### Outcome and thinking point

Say what somebody regularly does. In a present habit, the subject controls the habitual form. If a woman and a man both drink water, what changes and what remains identical? Compare the verb endings without changing the object.

`میں پانی پیتا ہوں۔` - `Maĩ pānī pītā hū̃.` means “I drink water”, for a male speaker. `میں پانی پیتی ہوں۔` - `Maĩ pānī pītī hū̃.` is the female-speaker form. The object precedes the action, and the auxiliary closes the sentence.

### Everyday actions

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| پینا | pīnā | to drink |
| کھانا | khānā | to eat |
| پڑھنا | paṛhnā | to read, study |
| لکھنا | likhnā | to write |
| کرنا | karnā | to do |
| جانا | jānā | to go |
| روز | roz | every day |
| روٹی | roṭī | flatbread |

### Build from a verb stem

Remove the infinitive `نا` to recognise the stem, then use the habitual ending: masculine singular `تا`, feminine `تی`, and masculine plural `تے`. `وہ روز پڑھتی ہے۔` - `Voh roz paṛhtī hai.` means “She studies every day.” The pronoun alone did not mark her gender, but the habitual form does.

`ہم کام کرتے ہیں۔` - `Ham kām karte haĩ.` means “We work”, for a masculine or mixed group. The expression literally combines work with do. For an all-female group, `ہم کام کرتی ہیں۔` - `Ham kām kartī haĩ.` is the relevant feminine habitual pattern. A feminine plural subject does not require changing the ordinary habitual ī ending into an invented long plural suffix.

`آپ اردو پڑھتے ہیں۔` - `Āp urdū paṛhte haĩ.` respectfully addresses a man or a mixed group. A respectful female listener takes `آپ اردو پڑھتی ہیں۔` - `Āp urdū paṛhtī haĩ.` Respect governs plural-style auxiliary agreement, while gender still matters in the participle.

### Common trap

Do not make the present habitual verb agree with the drink or book simply because that noun sits close to it. The speaker's grammatical gender determines pītā or pītī in the first examples. The object will become important in a different completed-action construction later.

### Practice

Express a regular action.

1. I drink water. (male speaker)
2. I drink tea. (female speaker)
3. She studies every day.
4. We work. (mixed group)

***Answers:***

1. `میں پانی پیتا ہوں۔` - `Maĩ pānī pītā hū̃.`
2. `میں چائے پیتی ہوں۔` - `Maĩ chāe pītī hū̃.`
3. `وہ روز پڑھتی ہے۔` - `Voh roz paṛhtī hai.`
4. `ہم کام کرتے ہیں۔` - `Ham kām karte haĩ.`

### Why these answers work

The habitual participle agrees with the subject, while the auxiliary marks person and number or respect. Every example keeps the object or frequency expression before the final verb phrase. The dictionary infinitive is not used as an uninflected present verb.

### Retrieve and recombine

Give a daily drinking habit and a reading habit for one person. Change that person from a man to a woman, then to a mixed group. Keep the objects unchanged and explain every change inside the verb phrase.

## Lesson 9: Ask about habits and deny them

### Outcome and thinking point

Ask what somebody studies and say what they do not drink. English questions often add do or does. Before inserting a new verb, ask whether Urdu can leave its habitual construction in place and replace only the unknown information.

`آپ کیا پڑھتے ہیں؟` - `Āp kyā paṛhte haĩ?` asks “What do you study?”, respectfully to a male listener. `وہ چائے نہیں پیتا۔` - `Voh chāe nahī̃ pītā.` means “He does not drink tea.” A present auxiliary is commonly omitted in negative habitual statements, though fuller forms are possible.

### Routine questions

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| کیا | kyā | what |
| کب | kab | when |
| کہاں | kahā̃ | where |
| کیوں | kyõ | why |
| صبح | subah | morning |
| شام کو | shām ko | in the evening |
| کام | kām | work |
| اردو | urdū | Urdu |

### Find the missing slot

`سارا دفتر میں کام کرتی ہے۔` - `Sārā daftar mẽ kām kartī hai.` means “Sara works in an office.” `سارا کہاں کام کرتی ہے؟` - `Sārā kahā̃ kām kartī hai?` asks where. The place phrase has been replaced by a question word, but the feminine habitual form remains.

`میں صبح اردو پڑھتی ہوں۔` - `Maĩ subah urdū paṛhtī hū̃.` means “I study Urdu in the morning”, for a female speaker. To ask when, use `آپ کب اردو پڑھتی ہیں؟` - `Āp kab urdū paṛhtī haĩ?`, respectfully to a female listener. Moving to the listener changes the auxiliary while retaining appropriate feminine agreement.

For yes-no questions, put `کیا` before the whole statement: `کیا آپ چائے پیتے ہیں؟` - `Kyā āp chāe pīte haĩ?`, “Do you drink tea?”, respectfully to a male listener. Contrast the initial yes-no marker with an internal what question. A short answer can be followed by a complete positive or negative statement when a preference needs clarification.

### Common trap

`کرنا` is a real verb meaning do or make, not a universal equivalent of English grammatical do. It belongs in `کام کرنا` because that expression means to work. It is not added to every Urdu question about drinking or studying.

### Practice

Ask about a habit or negate one.

1. What do you study? (respectful male listener)
2. He does not drink tea.
3. Where does Sara work?
4. I study Urdu in the morning. (female speaker)

***Answers:***

1. `آپ کیا پڑھتے ہیں؟` - `Āp kyā paṛhte haĩ?`
2. `وہ چائے نہیں پیتا۔` - `Voh chāe nahī̃ pītā.`
3. `سارا کہاں کام کرتی ہے؟` - `Sārā kahā̃ kām kartī hai?`
4. `میں صبح اردو پڑھتی ہوں۔` - `Maĩ subah urdū paṛhtī hū̃.`

### Why these answers work

Information questions preserve the habitual verb and ask only for the missing content. The negative places nahī̃ before the habitual form and omits the conversationally optional auxiliary. Sara and the female speaker select feminine participles without changing the study or work nouns.

### Retrieve and recombine

Ask a learner what they study, where they work and when they drink tea. Answer one positively and one negatively. Check that each response supplies the type of information requested rather than merely reusing any grammatically complete sentence.

## Lesson 10: Speak about an action in progress

### Outcome and thinking point

Describe what is happening now. A regular habit and an activity under way can be true of the same person, but they use different viewpoints. Predict how an extra progressive element might replace the habitual ending rather than being piled on top of it.

`میں پڑھ رہا ہوں۔` - `Maĩ paṛh rahā hū̃.` means “I am reading”, for a male speaker. `میں پڑھ رہی ہوں۔` - `Maĩ paṛh rahī hū̃.` is the female-speaker form. The main action is a bare stem followed by rahā, rahī or rahe and the auxiliary.

### Progressive toolkit

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| اب | ab | now |
| ابھی | abhī | right now, just now in context |
| رہا | rahā | progressive, masculine singular |
| رہی | rahī | progressive, feminine |
| رہے | rahe | progressive, masculine plural |
| بولنا | bolnā | to speak |
| سننا | sunnā | to hear, listen |
| گانا | gānā | song; to sing in other contexts |

### Compare viewpoints

`سارا اردو پڑھتی ہے۔` - `Sārā urdū paṛhtī hai.` describes Sara's habit. `سارا اردو پڑھ رہی ہے۔` - `Sārā urdū paṛh rahī hai.` describes her current study activity. The progressive does not contain the habitual `پڑھتی`. Choose the viewpoint before constructing the verb phrase.

`وہ پانی پی رہا ہے۔` - `Voh pānī pī rahā hai.` means “He is drinking water.” `ہم گانا سن رہے ہیں۔` - `Ham gānā sun rahe haĩ.` means “We are listening to a song”, for a masculine or mixed group. In both, agreement belongs to the participants doing the action rather than to water or song.

`آپ کیا کر رہی ہیں؟` - `Āp kyā kar rahī haĩ?` respectfully asks a woman “What are you doing?” The feminine progressive combines with the respectful auxiliary. An answer can replace doing with a specific activity rather than retaining kar unnecessarily.

### Common trap

Do not add rahā to every English sentence containing am, is or are. “I am a student” identifies a person; “I am reading” presents an ongoing action. Also avoid using the progressive merely because an event happens today; a daily habit remains habitual even when today's instance is relevant.

### Practice

Describe an ongoing action.

1. I am reading. (male speaker)
2. Sara is studying Urdu.
3. He is drinking water.
4. What are you doing? (respectful female listener)

***Answers:***

1. `میں پڑھ رہا ہوں۔` - `Maĩ paṛh rahā hū̃.`
2. `سارا اردو پڑھ رہی ہے۔` - `Sārā urdū paṛh rahī hai.`
3. `وہ پانی پی رہا ہے۔` - `Voh pānī pī rahā hai.`
4. `آپ کیا کر رہی ہیں؟` - `Āp kyā kar rahī haĩ?`

### Why these answers work

The action stems remain separate from the progressive element. Masculine singular subjects select rahā, feminine subjects rahī. The respectful female question uses rahī plus haĩ, showing that gender and respect contribute different parts of the verb phrase.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe a man reading, a woman speaking and a group listening. Ask the woman what she is doing. Then change one description into a regular habit and explain which progressive material disappears.

## Lesson 11: Give something to someone

### Outcome and thinking point

Distinguish a giver, a recipient and a thing being given. English often relies on position to distinguish “Sara gives me a book” from “I give Sara a book”. Urdu can mark the recipient explicitly while keeping the action at the end.

`سارا مجھے کتاب دیتی ہے۔` - `Sārā mujhe kitāb detī hai.` means “Sara gives me a book.” `مجھے` means me or to me in the appropriate construction. The unmarked book is what changes hands, while the marked person is the recipient.

### Participant forms

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| کو | ko | to; marker for some objects |
| مجھے | mujhe | me, to me |
| آپ کو | āp ko | you, to you, respectful |
| ہمیں | hamẽ | us, to us |
| اسے | use | him, her, to that person |
| دینا | denā | to give |
| لینا | lenā | to take |
| دیکھنا | dekhnā | to see |

### More than one use of کو

`میں سارا کو کتاب دیتا ہوں۔` - `Maĩ Sārā ko kitāb detā hū̃.` means “I give Sara a book”, for a male speaker. The habitual verb agrees with the giver, not the recipient. The recipient's gender does not turn detā into detī in this present construction.

`میں اسے دیکھ رہا ہوں۔` - `Maĩ use dekh rahā hū̃.` means “I am looking at him or her”, for a male speaker. Here the marked person is an object of seeing, not a recipient. Urdu commonly marks definite human objects, while ordinary nonspecific objects such as a book in a reading sentence may remain unmarked.

`مجھے` and `مجھ کو` - `mujh ko` are related alternatives. The course uses the compact mujhe in its keys, but the fuller form is real Urdu rather than an error. Likewise, use and us ko can refer to the same third person in suitable contexts. Learn the oblique base instead of attaching ko blindly to every direct pronoun.

### Common trap

Do not put ko after every English object. Animacy, specificity, verb meaning and construction all matter. Conversely, a human recipient such as Sara needs its relationship made clear; leaving all participants unmarked can change the interpretation or sound unnatural.

### Practice

Keep the three roles distinct.

1. Sara gives me a book.
2. I give Sara a book. (male speaker)
3. I am looking at that person. (male speaker)
4. Give the Urdu form “to us”.

***Answers:***

1. `سارا مجھے کتاب دیتی ہے۔` - `Sārā mujhe kitāb detī hai.`
2. `میں سارا کو کتاب دیتا ہوں۔` - `Maĩ Sārā ko kitāb detā hū̃.`
3. `میں اسے دیکھ رہا ہوں۔` - `Maĩ use dekh rahā hū̃.`
4. `ہمیں` - `hamẽ`

### Why these answers work

The first two preserve the transfer pattern but change the giver and its agreement. The third demonstrates a marked person as the object of seeing. The final answer is the requested participant phrase; it is not supposed to be a full sentence.

### Retrieve and recombine

Imagine a teacher giving you a book and you giving it to Sara. Say both transfers, naming giver, recipient and object before each attempt. Then describe seeing Sara and explain why recipient-like marking can occur without any transfer.

## Lesson 12: Say that you have something

### Outcome and thinking point

Express possession without translating English have as an ordinary action verb. You know a pattern for locating something near a person. Could an existence statement with a possessor phrase express that the person has it?

`میرے پاس کتاب ہے۔` - `Mere pās kitāb hai.` means “I have a book.” The phrase `میرے پاس` establishes the possessor; the book is what exists in that possession. The final copula is singular because the book is singular, not because the possessor is I.

### Possession and resources

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| پاس | pās | near, with, in possession of |
| میرے پاس | mere pās | I have, with me |
| آپ کے پاس | āp ke pās | you have, respectful |
| اس کے پاس | us ke pās | he or she has |
| ہمارے پاس | hamāre pās | we have |
| وقت | vaqt | time |
| پیسے | paise | money |
| فون | fon | phone |

### Build from what is possessed

`میرے پاس دو کتابیں ہیں۔` - `Mere pās do kitābẽ haĩ.` means “I have two books.” The plural item changes the final copula. `میرے پاس وقت نہیں ہے۔` - `Mere pās vaqt nahī̃ hai.` means “I do not have time.” Possession extends naturally to an available resource rather than only a physical object in someone's hand.

`کیا آپ کے پاس فون ہے؟` - `Kyā āp ke pās fon hai?` respectfully asks “Do you have a phone?” The respectful possessor phrase does not force a plural copula, because one phone is the item being discussed. Contrast `یہ آپ کا فون ہے` - `Yeh āp kā fon hai`, “This is your phone”, which identifies ownership rather than merely possession or availability.

Family relations often use a possessive existence construction without pās: `میری ایک بہن ہے۔` - `Merī ek bahan hai.` means “I have one sister.” This prevents an overbroad English-have rule. The relationship is expressed as the existence of one's sister, not normally as a portable item held in one's possession.

### Common trap

Do not use first-person hū̃ at the end simply because English begins with I. Identify the head of the existence statement. Books take plural haĩ, while time and a phone take singular hai in these examples.

### Practice

Use the appropriate possession pattern.

1. I have a phone.
2. I have two books.
3. I do not have time.
4. Do you have a phone? (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `میرے پاس فون ہے۔` - `Mere pās fon hai.`
2. `میرے پاس دو کتابیں ہیں۔` - `Mere pās do kitābẽ haĩ.`
3. `میرے پاس وقت نہیں ہے۔` - `Mere pās vaqt nahī̃ hai.`
4. `کیا آپ کے پاس فون ہے؟` - `Kyā āp ke pās fon hai?`

### Why these answers work

The possessor remains inside the pās phrase. The possessed item determines singular or plural existence. Negation and yes-no marking operate on the whole claim without turning the possessor into an ordinary first- or second-person subject.

### Retrieve and recombine

At a class, ask whether someone has a phone and say how many books you have. Distinguish having Sara's book with you from identifying the book as your property. Explain the different information supplied by the two constructions.

## Lesson 13: Express a need or a preference

### Outcome and thinking point

Say what you need and what you like. English I need begins with an ordinary subject. Urdu often marks the experiencer with the form already used for me or to me. Predict whether changing a male speaker to a female speaker should alter the form of need in these sentences.

`مجھے پانی چاہیے۔` - `Mujhe pānī chāhie.` means “I need water” or “I would like water” in an appropriate service context. `مجھے کتاب چاہیے۔` - `Mujhe kitāb chāhie.` means “I need a book.” The common singular need expression chāhie does not change for the speaker's gender or the difference between water and book.

### Needs and likes

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| چاہیے | chāhie | is needed, should in other constructions |
| پسند | pasand | liking, pleasing |
| ضرورت | zarūrat | need, necessity |
| مدد | madad | help |
| کھانا | khānā | food |
| ٹکٹ | ṭikaṭ | ticket |
| اور | aur | more, and |
| کافی | kāfī | enough; coffee in a different reading/context |

### Compare two experiencer constructions

`مجھے چائے پسند ہے۔` - `Mujhe chāe pasand hai.` means “I like tea.” `آپ کو کیا چاہیے؟` - `Āp ko kyā chāhie?` respectfully asks “What do you need?” The person is marked as the experiencer; the thing needed or liked remains a separate part of the sentence.

`ہمیں مدد چاہیے۔` - `Hamẽ madad chāhie.` means “We need help.” Several people can share a need without changing chāhie according to their number. When the needed items themselves are plural, standard usage may use `چاہییں` - `chāhiẽ`; conversational usage also varies. The practice here stays with singular or mass items so the main experiencer pattern remains clear.

`مجھے پانی کی ضرورت ہے۔` - `Mujhe pānī kī zarūrat hai.` is a fuller “I need water”, literally framing a need for water. The linking kī agrees with feminine zarūrat rather than masculine water. This is another reason to locate the head noun of a phrase before selecting a possessive-looking link.

### Common trap

Do not transfer Punjabi chāhīdā/chāhīdī agreement into Urdu chāhie. The languages share many structures, but their need expressions are not identical. Also distinguish liking tea generally from requesting a cup now; context and the choice of construction communicate that difference.

### Practice

Express the need or preference.

1. I need water.
2. We need help.
3. I like tea.
4. What do you need? (respectful)

***Answers:***

1. `مجھے پانی چاہیے۔` - `Mujhe pānī chāhie.`
2. `ہمیں مدد چاہیے۔` - `Hamẽ madad chāhie.`
3. `مجھے چائے پسند ہے۔` - `Mujhe chāe pasand hai.`
4. `آپ کو کیا چاہیے؟` - `Āp ko kyā chāhie?`

### Why these answers work

The experiencer is expressed with mujhe, hamẽ or āp ko. Chāhie states the need without agreeing with the speaker's sex. The preference expression uses pasand plus a copula instead, so it does not automatically mean an immediate request.

### Retrieve and recombine

At a café, state a need for water and a preference for tea. Move to a station and state a need for a ticket. Explain which words identify the person needing something and which identify the requested item.

## Lesson 14: Ask someone to act politely

### Outcome and thinking point

Make a respectful request without turning a habitual statement into a command. If you address an unfamiliar adult with āp, which verb form should maintain that respectful relationship? Learn a small set of practical polite imperatives as whole forms.

`بیٹھیے۔` - `Baiṭhie.` means “Please sit.” `پانی دیجیے۔` - `Pānī dījie.` means “Please give water.” The imperative already addresses the listener, so an explicit āp is optional. Politeness comes from the verb form and context, not from appending an English word to an unmodified command.

### Polite forms you can use

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| آئیے | āie | come, polite |
| جائیے | jāie | go, polite |
| بیٹھیے | baiṭhie | sit, polite |
| دیجیے | dījie | give, polite |
| لیجیے | lījie | take, polite |
| سنیے | sunie | listen, polite |
| بولیے | bolie | speak, polite |
| آہستہ | āhista | slowly, softly |

### Build a request with useful detail

`یہاں بیٹھیے۔` - `Yahā̃ baiṭhie.` means “Please sit here.” `آہستہ بولیے۔` - `Āhista bolie.` asks “Please speak slowly.” The place or manner expression precedes the action. `یہ کتاب لیجیے۔` - `Yeh kitāb lījie.` politely offers “Please take this book.” Imperatives can be welcoming offers as well as instructions.

For a negative request, use `نہ` or `مت` in appropriate contexts. `یہاں مت بیٹھیے۔` - `Yahā̃ mat baiṭhie.` means “Please do not sit here.” The polite ending remains, though a direct prohibition can still feel firm. If you are warning someone about a wet seat, the context makes the intention helpful rather than hostile.

Urdu has spelling variants in some polite forms, particularly the representation of vowel sequences and hamza. The keys use one conventional set consistently. Seeing a legitimate alternate spelling in a book does not mean the speaker is using a different grammatical politeness level.

### Common trap

Do not add haĩ after a polite imperative by analogy with āp statements. The imperative is already a complete verb form. Also avoid assuming that a respectful ending guarantees a considerate message regardless of what you demand or how you deliver it.

### Practice

Use a respectful imperative.

1. Please sit here.
2. Please speak slowly.
3. Please give water.
4. Please do not sit here.

***Answers:***

1. `یہاں بیٹھیے۔` - `Yahā̃ baiṭhie.`
2. `آہستہ بولیے۔` - `Āhista bolie.`
3. `پانی دیجیے۔` - `Pānī dījie.`
4. `یہاں مت بیٹھیے۔` - `Yahā̃ mat baiṭhie.`

### Why these answers work

Each request uses the appropriate polite imperative without a present copula. Place and manner words specify the action. The negative request puts mat before the verb and retains the same respectful form of sit used in the positive request.

### Retrieve and recombine

Welcome a learner, offer a book and request slower speech. Then imagine asking for water at a café. Change the action and object while preserving the respectful relationship encoded in the imperative.

## Lesson 15: Count things and check a price

### Outcome and thinking point

Ask for a small number of objects and ask their cost. A numeral indicates quantity, but nouns still follow their own plural patterns. Predict how one room and two rooms differ, then compare that with one book and two books.

`ایک کمرہ` - `ek kamrā` is “one room”; `دو کمرے` - `do kamre` is “two rooms”. `ایک کتاب` - `ek kitāb` is “one book”; `دو کتابیں` - `do kitābẽ` is “two books”. Urdu does not apply one universal plural suffix to every noun.

### First quantities

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| ایک | ek | one |
| دو | do | two |
| تین | tīn | three |
| چار | chār | four |
| پانچ | pā̃ch | five |
| دس | das | ten |
| کتنے | kitne | how many, masculine plural |
| کتنی | kitnī | how much or many, feminine |
| روپے | rupaē | rupees |

### Quantity and price phrases

`آپ کے پاس کتنی کتابیں ہیں؟` - `Āp ke pās kitnī kitābẽ haĩ?` asks “How many books do you have?” Feminine kitnī accompanies the plural feminine noun without acquiring a further plural ending. `میرے پاس تین کتابیں ہیں۔` - `Mere pās tīn kitābẽ haĩ.` answers “I have three books.”

`یہ کتنے کا ہے؟` - `Yeh kitne kā hai?` asks “How much is this?”, for a masculine singular item in a price context. `یہ کتاب کتنے کی ہے؟` - `Yeh kitāb kitne kī hai?` asks the price of a feminine book. The final possessive-like link follows the item's gender. A more explicit currency phrase can name rupees, but context often supplies the unit.

`دو کتابیں دیجیے۔` - `Do kitābẽ dījie.` means “Please give two books.” The polite imperative does not change according to the number of books. It addresses the person doing the giving, while the object phrase supplies the quantity.

### Common trap

Do not treat price and count questions as identical. “How many books?” asks for a quantity of objects; “How much is the book?” asks for a price. The noun and the link in the price expression tell the listener which relationship you mean.

### Practice

Ask or state the quantity clearly.

1. I have three books.
2. Please give two books.
3. How many books do you have? (respectful)
4. How much is this book?

***Answers:***

1. `میرے پاس تین کتابیں ہیں۔` - `Mere pās tīn kitābẽ haĩ.`
2. `دو کتابیں دیجیے۔` - `Do kitābẽ dījie.`
3. `آپ کے پاس کتنی کتابیں ہیں؟` - `Āp ke pās kitnī kitābẽ haĩ?`
4. `یہ کتاب کتنے کی ہے؟` - `Yeh kitāb kitne kī hai?`

### Why these answers work

Book uses its feminine plural in quantity phrases, with the corresponding feminine question adjective. The imperative remains listener-directed. In the price question, kī links the price to the feminine book, while the singular copula describes one item.

### Retrieve and recombine

At a bookshop, ask for two books, state that you already have three and ask the price of one new book. Identify which question seeks a count and which seeks a monetary amount.

## Lesson 16: Say when and where you travel

### Outcome and thinking point

Describe a journey and place it in time. You know location inside a place. Is coming from a place the same relationship? Add an origin marker and notice that a familiar destination can often appear without a separate to word.

`میں اسکول جاتا ہوں۔` - `Maĩ iskūl jātā hū̃.` means “I go to school”, for a male speaker. `میں اسکول سے آ رہا ہوں۔` - `Maĩ iskūl se ā rahā hū̃.` means “I am coming from school.” The source marker se follows the place noun.

### Journey and schedule words

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| آنا | ānā | to come |
| جانا | jānā | to go |
| سے | se | from; with, by or than in other uses |
| تک | tak | as far as, until |
| بازار | bāzār | market |
| آج | āj | today |
| کل | kal | yesterday or tomorrow |
| بجے | baje | o'clock |
| شام کو | shām ko | in the evening |

### Keep direction and time separate

`سارا بازار جا رہی ہے۔` - `Sārā bāzār jā rahī hai.` means “Sara is going to the market.” `آپ کہاں سے آ رہے ہیں؟` - `Āp kahā̃ se ā rahe haĩ?` respectfully asks a male listener “Where are you coming from?” Where plus se asks an origin; ordinary kahā̃ with go asks a destination.

`میں پانچ بجے آتا ہوں۔` - `Maĩ pā̃ch baje ātā hū̃.` means “I come at five o'clock”, for a male speaker in a routine or scheduled context. The numeral plus baje gives the clock time without adding a literal English at. `وہ شام کو گھر آتی ہے۔` - `Voh shām ko ghar ātī hai.` describes her evening arrival routine.

`کل` can mean yesterday or tomorrow. The verb tense and context establish which side of today is intended. Do not memorise kal as only tomorrow and then misread a past narrative. Upcoming lessons make the distinction explicit with past and future forms.

### Common trap

Do not interpret every se as from regardless of context. It also marks other relationships, including comparison later in the course. Likewise, a destination noun before go is not automatically a direct object that behaves like a book being read.

### Practice

Describe a journey or schedule.

1. Sara is going to the market.
2. I go to school. (male speaker)
3. Where are you coming from? (respectful male listener)
4. I come at five o'clock. (male speaker, routine)

***Answers:***

1. `سارا بازار جا رہی ہے۔` - `Sārā bāzār jā rahī hai.`
2. `میں اسکول جاتا ہوں۔` - `Maĩ iskūl jātā hū̃.`
3. `آپ کہاں سے آ رہے ہیں؟` - `Āp kahā̃ se ā rahe haĩ?`
4. `میں پانچ بجے آتا ہوں۔` - `Maĩ pā̃ch baje ātā hū̃.`

### Why these answers work

The destination precedes go without an English-style preposition. The origin question uses se after where. Habitual and progressive forms preserve their different viewpoints, and the clock phrase appears before the arrival verb without changing its agreement.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe leaving school, going to the market and coming home at a regular time. Ask another traveller where they are coming from. Keep location, source, destination and time as separate pieces of information.

## Lesson 17: Describe a past state

### Outcome and thinking point

Say where somebody was and how they felt. Before attempting a completed action, change a simple present description into a past description. Which final linking word can carry the time change while the location or adjective remains intact?

`میں گھر پر تھا۔` - `Maĩ ghar par thā.` means “I was at home”, for a male speaker. `میں گھر پر تھی۔` - `Maĩ ghar par thī.` is the female-speaker form. Unlike present hū̃, the past copula itself distinguishes grammatical gender.

### Past copulas and settings

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| تھا | thā | was, masculine singular |
| تھی | thī | was, feminine singular |
| تھے | the | were, masculine plural or respectful |
| تھیں | thī̃ | were, feminine plural or respectful |
| کل | kal | yesterday in a past context |
| تب | tab | then |
| بند | band | closed |
| کھلا | khulā | open, masculine singular |

### Match the past state to its subject

`سارا کل بیمار تھی۔` - `Sārā kal bīmār thī.` means “Sara was ill yesterday.” The adjective bīmār is unchanged; the past copula carries feminine agreement. `کمرے کھلے تھے۔` - `Kamre khule the.` means “The rooms were open.” Both the changing adjective and past copula are masculine plural.

`آپ کہاں تھیں؟` - `Āp kahā̃ thī̃?` respectfully asks a woman “Where were you?” A respectful male listener receives `آپ کہاں تھے؟` - `Āp kahā̃ the?`. Nasalisation distinguishes feminine plural or respectful thī̃ from feminine singular thī in the written standard.

A past state does not itself report the event that produced it. `اسکول بند تھا۔` - `Iskūl band thā.` means “The school was closed.” It does not identify someone who closed the school. A person being at home likewise does not necessarily imply a narrated journey home.

### Common trap

Do not use thā for every past sentence. Gender, number and respect still matter. Also do not confuse a past copula with the completed form of an action: being at a place and travelling to it answer different questions.

### Practice

Describe the past state with the specified subject.

1. I was at home. (male speaker)
2. I was at home. (female speaker)
3. Sara was ill yesterday.
4. Where were you? (respectful female listener)

***Answers:***

1. `میں گھر پر تھا۔` - `Maĩ ghar par thā.`
2. `میں گھر پر تھی۔` - `Maĩ ghar par thī.`
3. `سارا کل بیمار تھی۔` - `Sārā kal bīmār thī.`
4. `آپ کہاں تھیں؟` - `Āp kahā̃ thī̃?`

### Why these answers work

The past copula changes with the subject's gender and respectful number. Kal is interpreted as yesterday because the clause describes a past state. The respectful feminine question uses the nasalised plural-style form, rather than ordinary singular thī.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe where a man and a woman were yesterday. Ask the woman the same question respectfully, then describe several open rooms. Explain which endings carry gender, which carry number or respect, and which indicate past time.

## Lesson 18: Report a completed journey

### Outcome and thinking point

Say that someone went or came. The previous lesson located people in the past. A completed journey adds an event. Predict whether the moving person remains the agreement controller when no thing is being acted on as an object.

`سارا گھر گئی۔` - `Sārā ghar gaī.` means “Sara went home.” `علی گھر گیا۔` - `Alī ghar gayā.` means “Ali went home.” Go has an irregular completed stem, so the forms are not produced by mechanically adding a suffix to jā.

### Completed movement forms

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| گیا | gayā | went, masculine singular |
| گئی | gaī | went, feminine singular |
| گئے | gae | went, masculine plural |
| گئیں | gaī̃ | went, feminine plural or respectful |
| آیا | āyā | came, masculine singular |
| آئی | āī | came, feminine singular |
| آئے | āe | came, masculine plural |
| واپس | vāpas | back |

### Report an event without inventing an object

`میں کل بازار گیا۔` - `Maĩ kal bāzār gayā.` means “I went to the market yesterday”, for a male speaker. `ہم گھر آئے۔` - `Ham ghar āe.` means “We came home”, for a masculine or mixed group. The destination is not an acted-on object; the verb agrees with the people moving.

`آپ کب آئیں؟` - `Āp kab āī̃?` respectfully asks a woman “When did you come?” A respectful male listener receives `آپ کب آئے؟` - `Āp kab āe?`. The respect pattern continues into completed movement forms, rather than disappearing whenever the sentence is past.

Completed forms can combine with auxiliaries to relate an event to present or past reference points. `وہ آیا ہے` can present his arrival as currently relevant, while a past auxiliary can place it relative to another past context. The bare completed forms here suffice for a short event report with an understood time.

### Common trap

Do not add `نے` after every past subject. The ordinary completed movement clauses here retain direct subject agreement. The actor marker in the next lesson belongs to a particular completed-transitive pattern, not to the meaning yesterday by itself.

### Practice

Report the completed movement.

1. Sara went home.
2. Ali came home.
3. I went to the market yesterday. (male speaker)
4. We came home. (mixed group)

***Answers:***

1. `سارا گھر گئی۔` - `Sārā ghar gaī.`
2. `علی گھر آیا۔` - `Alī ghar āyā.`

The completed form agrees with the moving person or group. Go has its irregular completed family, while come has the demonstrated vowel forms. Yesterday is established jointly by kal and the completed past interpretation. No actor marker is needed in these movement clauses.

## Lesson 19: Complete an action on an object

### Outcome and thinking point

Report eating, drinking, reading and writing as completed actions. In a present habit, the actor controls agreement. What changes if Urdu marks that actor with ne and leaves a book unmarked? Compare the gender of the book with the completed verb before deciding that the sentence has a mistake.

`علی نے کتاب پڑھی۔` - `Alī ne kitāb paṛhī.` means “Ali read the book.” Ali is male, but the completed reading form is feminine because it agrees with the unmarked feminine book. This is a different construction from the present habitual.

### Completed transitive forms

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| نے | ne | actor marker in this completed construction |
| پڑھا | paṛhā | read, completed masculine singular |
| پڑھی | paṛhī | read, completed feminine singular |
| لکھا | likhā | wrote, masculine singular |
| لکھی | likhī | wrote, feminine singular |
| کھایا | khāyā | ate, masculine singular |
| کھائی | khāī | ate, feminine singular |
| خط | khat | letter, masculine |

### Follow the unmarked object

`سارا نے خط لکھا۔` - `Sārā ne khat likhā.` means “Sara wrote the letter.” Sara is female, but the letter is masculine, so likhā is correct. `میں نے روٹی کھائی۔` - `Maĩ ne roṭī khāī.` means “I ate the flatbread.” Either a male or a female speaker can use this same sentence because the feminine bread controls agreement.

`علی نے پانی پیا۔` - `Alī ne pānī piyā.` means “Ali drank the water.” `سارا نے چائے پی۔` - `Sārā ne chāe pī.` means “Sara drank the tea.” The completed drink forms have their own vowel pattern. Do not confuse the feminine completed pī with the bare stem merely because they look alike in isolation.

The useful decision sequence is: identify the completed transitive construction, mark the actor appropriately, then inspect the object for agreement. This is not a command to put ne into every past sentence. The preceding completed journeys did not use this pattern.

### Common trap

Do not choose the completed participle from the actor's sex when ne has marked the actor and an unmarked object controls agreement. Equally, do not erase the object's gender merely because English uses the same past verb for every object.

### Practice

Use completed transitive agreement.

1. Ali read the book.
2. Sara wrote the letter.
3. I ate the flatbread.
4. Sara drank the tea.

***Answers:***

1. `علی نے کتاب پڑھی۔` - `Alī ne kitāb paṛhī.`
2. `سارا نے خط لکھا۔` - `Sārā ne khat likhā.`
3. `میں نے روٹی کھائی۔` - `Maĩ ne roṭī khāī.`
4. `سارا نے چائے پی۔` - `Sārā ne chāe pī.`

### Why these answers work

Ne marks each actor, including the first-person pronoun. The unmarked book, flatbread and tea are feminine; the letter is masculine. Their grammatical gender determines the completed form independently of the actor's sex.

### Retrieve and recombine

Describe Ali reading a book and Sara writing a letter, then swap the actors while preserving each object and action. Explain why the completed endings stay tied to the objects. Add one journey sentence and explain why it follows another agreement pattern.

## Lesson 20: Marked objects and present relevance

### Outcome and thinking point

Recognise what happens when a completed clause's object is marked, and connect a completed event to the present. If ne blocks ordinary subject agreement and ko marks the object, is there an unmarked noun available to control the verb? Look for a default form rather than forcing agreement with a marked person.

`علی نے سارا کو دیکھا۔` - `Alī ne Sārā ko dekhā.` means “Ali saw Sara.” The object is marked with ko. The completed verb appears in default masculine singular form rather than feminine agreement with Sara. This is a productive contrast with an unmarked feminine book being read.

### Contrasts to inspect

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| دیکھا | dekhā | saw, completed masculine singular |
| سنا | sunā | heard, masculine singular |
| بلایا | bulāyā | called, summoned, masculine singular |
| اسے | use | him, her, marked object |
| مجھے | mujhe | me, marked object |
| پڑھا ہے | paṛhā hai | has read, masculine agreement |
| پڑھی ہے | paṛhī hai | has read, feminine agreement |
| ابھی | abhī | just now, now |

### Compare two completed reports

`سارا نے مجھے دیکھا۔` - `Sārā ne mujhe dekhā.` means “Sara saw me.” The form dekhā remains default masculine singular regardless of the sex of the person represented by mujhe. Do not try to restore feminine agreement merely because both people in the situation happen to be women.

`علی نے کتاب پڑھی ہے۔` - `Alī ne kitāb paṛhī hai.` means “Ali has read the book.” The present auxiliary gives the completed event a present reference or relevance in this simple contrast. The feminine agreement continues to follow the unmarked book. `سارا نے خط لکھا ہے۔` - `Sārā ne khat likhā hai.` means “Sara has written the letter.”

The distinction between a bare completed form and one with hai is not a perfect mechanical copy of English simple past versus present perfect in every discourse. Use these examples to recognise viewpoint and current relevance, then listen to how actual speakers frame completed events.

### Common trap

Do not let a following present auxiliary trick you into applying present-habitual subject agreement. The core construction is still completed and transitive. Ne and object marking remain relevant even when the event has a present reference point.

### Practice

Choose the correct completed agreement.

1. Ali saw Sara.
2. Sara saw me.
3. Ali has read the book.
4. Sara has written the letter.

***Answers:***

1. `علی نے سارا کو دیکھا۔` - `Alī ne Sārā ko dekhā.`
2. `سارا نے مجھے دیکھا۔` - `Sārā ne mujhe dekhā.`
3. `علی نے کتاب پڑھی ہے۔` - `Alī ne kitāb paṛhī hai.`
4. `سارا نے خط لکھا ہے۔` - `Sārā ne khat likhā hai.`

### Why these answers work

The first two have marked objects and use default masculine singular completed forms. The last two have unmarked objects that control agreement. Adding hai supplies a present reference without changing the grammatical relationship between the completed participle and its unmarked object.

### Retrieve and recombine

Report seeing a person and reading a book. Explain why the person-marked sentence does not use the person's gender for agreement, while the book sentence does. Then make the reading result relevant to a conversation happening now.

## Lesson 21: Announce a future plan

### Outcome and thinking point

Say what you or another person will do. Kal can point to yesterday or tomorrow, but an explicitly future verb makes tomorrow the natural interpretation. Predict which part of a first-person future distinguishes a male from a female speaker.

`میں جاؤں گا۔` - `Maĩ jāū̃ gā.` means “I will go”, for a male speaker. `میں جاؤں گی۔` - `Maĩ jāū̃ gī.` is the female-speaker form. Urdu normally writes the future gender element as a separate word. Preserve that spacing rather than importing another language's spelling convention.

### Common future forms

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| جاؤں گا | jāū̃ gā | I will go, masculine |
| جاؤں گی | jāū̃ gī | I will go, feminine |
| جائے گا | jāe gā | he will go |
| جائے گی | jāe gī | she will go |
| آئیں گے | āẽ ge | will come, respectful masculine/plural |
| آؤں گا | āū̃ gā | I will come, masculine |
| کروں گی | karū̃ gī | I will do, feminine |
| کل | kal | tomorrow in a future context |

### Combine a plan and a time

`میں کل اسکول جاؤں گی۔` - `Maĩ kal iskūl jāū̃ gī.` means “I will go to school tomorrow”, for a female speaker. `وہ شام کو آئے گا۔` - `Voh shām ko āe gā.` means “He will come in the evening.” Person-related vowel endings and the gender element work together; do not look only at gā or gī.

`میں پانچ بجے آؤں گا۔` - `Maĩ pā̃ch baje āū̃ gā.` announces “I will come at five o'clock”, for a male speaker. `میں آج کام نہیں کروں گی۔` - `Maĩ āj kām nahī̃ karū̃ gī.` says “I will not work today”, for a female speaker. The negative retains its familiar position before the finite verb.

Future forms can also express inference or probability in other contexts, especially with forms of be. This lesson uses straightforward plans and predictions so the time relationship stays clear. A future form is not a guarantee that the event will occur, just as an English future statement can be a plan rather than a certainty.

### Common trap

Do not add hū̃ or hai after an already finite future merely because present habitual forms needed an auxiliary. Also do not use the first-person jāū̃ with a third-person subject; gender is only one component of the future form.

2. He will come in the evening.

## Lesson 22: Ability, desire and obligation

### Outcome and thinking point

Say what you can do, want to do and should do. These three meanings are related but not interchangeable. Before reading the examples, decide whether ability is best built with an action stem or the infinitive form used to name an action.

`میں اردو پڑھ سکتا ہوں۔` - `Maĩ urdū paṛh saktā hū̃.` means “I can read Urdu”, for a male speaker. The action appears as the bare stem paṛh before saktā. A female speaker uses saktī. This differs from the infinitive used in a wanting construction.

### Modal building blocks

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| سکتا | saktā | can, masculine singular |
| سکتی | saktī | can, feminine |
| سکتے | sakte | can, masculine plural |
| چاہتا | chāhtā | wants, masculine singular |
| چاہتی | chāhtī | wants, feminine |
| سیکھنا | sīkhnā | to learn |
| سمجھنا | samajhnā | to understand |
| چاہیے | chāhie | should, in an obligation construction |

### Compare the three meanings

`میں اردو سیکھنا چاہتی ہوں۔` - `Maĩ urdū sīkhnā chāhtī hū̃.` means “I want to learn Urdu”, for a female speaker. The learning action is an infinitive before the wanting verb. `میں اردو لکھ سکتی ہوں۔` - `Maĩ urdū likh saktī hū̃.` means “I can write Urdu”; the main action is a stem before ability.

`مجھے گھر جانا چاہیے۔` - `Mujhe ghar jānā chāhie.` means “I should go home.” The person under the obligation is marked with mujhe rather than ordinary maĩ. In this demonstrated infinitive construction, chāhie expresses advisability or obligation, not simply wanting an object. Compare the earlier `مجھے پانی چاہیے`, which names a needed thing.

`میں ابھی نہیں آ سکتا۔` - `Maĩ abhī nahī̃ ā saktā.` means “I cannot come right now”, for a male speaker. It gives a present limitation, not necessarily a permanent inability. The auxiliary can be omitted in this negative conversational form.

### Common trap

Do not use one main-verb shape after all modal meanings. Ability takes a bare stem here, wanting an infinitive, and obligation an infinitive with an experiencer-like subject. The English words can, want and should do not determine Urdu structure by themselves.

### Practice

Choose the construction that matches the meaning.

1. I can read Urdu. (male speaker)
2. I can write Urdu. (female speaker)
3. I want to learn Urdu. (female speaker)
4. I should go home.

***Answers:***

1. `میں اردو پڑھ سکتا ہوں۔` - `Maĩ urdū paṛh saktā hū̃.`
2. `میں اردو لکھ سکتی ہوں۔` - `Maĩ urdū likh saktī hū̃.`
3. `میں اردو سیکھنا چاہتی ہوں۔` - `Maĩ urdū sīkhnā chāhtī hū̃.`
4. `مجھے گھر جانا چاہیے۔` - `Mujhe ghar jānā chāhie.`

### Why these answers work

The ability phrases use bare stems and subject-agreeing saktā or saktī. Wanting uses an infinitive followed by the female speaker's chāhtī. Obligation changes the person to mujhe and uses the infinitive plus chāhie rather than an ordinary habitual wanting verb.

### Retrieve and recombine

State something you can do, something you want to learn and somewhere you should go. Explain the difference in meaning before naming the different main-verb forms. Then turn your ability statement into a current inability without implying a permanent limitation.

## Lesson 23: Connect reasons, contrasts and conditions

### Outcome and thinking point

Join short clauses while preserving the grammar inside each one. A connector tells the listener how two ideas relate; it does not replace tense or agreement. Build the two independent claims first, then decide whether the relationship is addition, contrast, reason or condition.

`میں گھر پر ہوں کیونکہ میں بیمار ہوں۔` - `Maĩ ghar par hū̃ kyõki maĩ bīmār hū̃.` means “I am at home because I am ill.” The second clause gives a reason for the first. The repeated subject can be omitted in natural contexts, but keeping it explicit reveals the two-clause structure.

### Connections and scope

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| اور | aur | and |
| لیکن | lekin | but |
| کیونکہ | kyõki | because |
| اس لیے | is lie | therefore, for this reason |
| اگر | agar | if |
| تو | to | then, in a conditional pattern |
| بھی | bhī | also, too |
| صرف | sirf | only |

### Connect complete thoughts

`میں آؤں گا لیکن وہ نہیں آئے گا۔` - `Maĩ āū̃ gā lekin voh nahī̃ āe gā.` means “I will come, but he will not come”, for a male speaker. The future forms continue to distinguish their subjects. The contrast word does not make one shared ending sufficient for both clauses.

`اگر وقت ہے تو آئیے۔` - `Agar vaqt hai to āie.` means “If there is time, please come.” The condition appears with agar and the resulting invitation with to. This is a useful small conditional using already familiar existence and imperative forms; it is not a complete account of hypothetical tense distinctions.

`میں بھی اردو پڑھتی ہوں۔` - `Maĩ bhī urdū paṛhtī hū̃.` means “I also study Urdu”, with the natural reading that the female speaker is another learner. Putting bhī next to the intended added element helps manage focus. English also can float more freely than a literal translation might suggest.

### Common trap

Do not confuse because with therefore. One introduces a reason and the other a result. Also distinguish conditional to from intimate tū: Urdu spelling and romanised vowel length help separate grammatical functions that may look similar to a beginner.

### Practice

Express the indicated relationship.

1. I am home because I am ill.
2. I will come, but he will not come. (male speaker)
3. If there is time, please come.
4. I also study Urdu. (female speaker)

***Answers:***

1. `میں گھر پر ہوں کیونکہ میں بیمار ہوں۔` - `Maĩ ghar par hū̃ kyõki maĩ bīmār hū̃.`
2. `میں آؤں گا لیکن وہ نہیں آئے گا۔` - `Maĩ āū̃ gā lekin voh nahī̃ āe gā.`
3. `اگر وقت ہے تو آئیے۔` - `Agar vaqt hai to āie.`
4. `میں بھی اردو پڑھتی ہوں۔` - `Maĩ bhī urdū paṛhtī hū̃.`

### Why these answers work

The reason clause explains the location statement. The contrasting futures retain separate agreement. The conditional links an existence condition to an invitation, while the addition particle places the speaker alongside other learners without changing the habitual construction.

### Retrieve and recombine

Give a reason for staying home, contrast two future plans and invite someone conditionally. Then add yourself to an existing group of learners. Name the intended logical relationship before choosing the connector each time.

## Lesson 24: Compare choices and manage a service exchange

### Outcome and thinking point

Compare two objects, choose one and repair a misunderstanding. You know se as from. A comparison can use the same marker to identify the reference point. Predict which room is being described in a sentence meaning “This room is larger than that room”.

`یہ کمرہ اس کمرے سے بڑا ہے۔` - `Yeh kamrā us kamre se baṛā hai.` means “This room is larger than that room.” The reference room stands in an oblique phrase before se. The adjective agrees with the room being evaluated, not with an English comparative suffix.

### Choosing and clarifying

| Urdu | Romanisation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| مہنگا | mahãgā | expensive, masculine |
| سستا | sastā | inexpensive, masculine |
| سب سے | sab se | most, of all |
| کون سی | kaun sī | which, feminine |
| دوبارہ | dobārā | again |
| شکریہ | shukriyā | thank you |
| دکان | dukān | shop |
| اسٹیشن | isṭeshan | station |

### Build a practical exchange

`یہ کتاب اس کتاب سے سستی ہے۔` - `Yeh kitāb us kitāb se sastī hai.` means “This book is cheaper than that book.” `آپ کو کون سی کتاب چاہیے؟` - `Āp ko kaun sī kitāb chāhie?` asks “Which book do you need?” The feminine selecting expression follows the known noun's gender.

Customer: `مجھے یہ کتاب چاہیے۔` - `Mujhe yeh kitāb chāhie.` says “I need this book.” Seller: `یہ کتاب لیجیے۔` - `Yeh kitāb lījie.` offers “Please take this book.” Customer: `شکریہ۔` - `Shukriyā.` says “Thank you.” These short turns combine earlier grammar with a new decision.

If the price is unclear, `دوبارہ بولیے۔` - `Dobārā bolie.` asks “Please say it again.” `آہستہ بولیے۔` asks for slower speech. At a station the same repair tools work with a location question: `اسٹیشن کہاں ہے؟` - `Isṭeshan kahā̃ hai?`, “Where is the station?” Transfer the communicative purpose rather than memorising one unchangeable dialogue.

### Common trap

Do not add English -er to a romanised adjective or assume every comparison needs a separate word for more. The reference phrase plus adjective establishes the comparison here. Also avoid pretending to understand a price or direction when a short repair request can clarify it.

### Practice

Compare, select or repair the exchange.

1. This book is cheaper than that book.
2. Which book do you need? (respectful)
3. Please say it again.
4. Where is the station?

***Answers:***

1. `یہ کتاب اس کتاب سے سستی ہے۔` - `Yeh kitāb us kitāb se sastī hai.`
2. `آپ کو کون سی کتاب چاہیے؟` - `Āp ko kaun sī kitāb chāhie?`
3. `دوبارہ بولیے۔` - `Dobārā bolie.`
4. `اسٹیشن کہاں ہے؟` - `Isṭeshan kahā̃ hai?`

### Why these answers work

The comparison marks its reference point with se and uses a feminine adjective for book. The selecting question uses feminine sī. The repair request is a polite imperative, while the station question requests a place with the singular copula.

### Retrieve and recombine

Create a short bookshop exchange: compare two books, choose one, ask the price and request a repeat. Move the same repair request to a station conversation. Keep the reason for each turn clear even when the vocabulary changes.

## Lesson 25: Tell a small story and inspect its patterns

### Outcome and thinking point

Introduce a learner, describe a routine, report a completed event and announce a plan. Before building each sentence, identify its job. Is it an identity, state, habit, current activity, completed action or future? The right construction matters more than keeping one ending throughout a story.

Use Sara as a female learner and Ali as a male friend. Their ordinary activities provide enough content for a connected account without introducing a large new word list. You are practising flexible foundations, not claiming that finishing these lessons establishes fluency or replaces conversation with speakers.

### A review map

| Urdu | Romanisation | Function |
|---|---|---|
| خوش ہے | khush hai | is happy |
| پڑھتی ہے | paṛhtī hai | studies habitually, feminine |
| پڑھ رہی ہے | paṛh rahī hai | is studying, feminine |
| گھر پر تھی | ghar par thī | was home, feminine |
| گھر گئی | ghar gaī | went home, feminine |
| کتاب پڑھی | kitāb paṛhī | read a book, completed object agreement |
| جائے گی | jāe gī | will go, feminine |
| مجھے چاہیے | mujhe chāhie | I need |

### Follow one learner through several viewpoints

`سارا خوش ہے۔` - `Sārā khush hai.` says “Sara is happy.” `وہ روز اردو پڑھتی ہے۔` - `Voh roz urdū paṛhtī hai.` describes her daily study. `وہ ابھی چائے پی رہی ہے۔` - `Voh abhī chāe pī rahī hai.` says “She is drinking tea right now.” All three can be true, but they describe different kinds of information.

`سارا کل گھر پر تھی۔` - `Sārā kal ghar par thī.` locates her at home yesterday. `اس نے کتاب پڑھی۔` - `Us ne kitāb paṛhī.` means “She read the book.” The third-person pronoun becomes us before ne. The feminine completed form follows the unmarked book, even though Sara also happens to be female.

`وہ کل اسکول جائے گی۔` - `Voh kal iskūl jāe gī.` means “She will go to school tomorrow.” Kal has changed its calendar interpretation because the verb now frames the event as future. A listener can ask when she will come or what she needs, giving you a reason to reuse time and experiencer patterns.

### Common trap

Agreement that happens to look right can hide incorrect reasoning. Sara and book are both feminine, so a feminine completed form alone does not prove you identified its controller. Replace Sara with Ali: the book still requires paṛhī in the completed-transitive pattern. This substitution exposes the underlying rule.

### Practice

Recombine the foundation patterns.

1. Sara is studying Urdu now.
2. Ali read the book.
3. She will go to school tomorrow.
4. I want to learn Urdu. (female speaker)

***Answers:***

1. `سارا اب اردو پڑھ رہی ہے۔` - `Sārā ab urdū paṛh rahī hai.`
2. `علی نے کتاب پڑھی۔` - `Alī ne kitāb paṛhī.`
3. `وہ کل اسکول جائے گی۔` - `Voh kal iskūl jāe gī.`
4. `میں اردو سیکھنا چاہتی ہوں۔` - `Maĩ urdū sīkhnā chāhtī hū̃.`

### Why these answers work

The progressive agrees with Sara, while the completed reading form agrees with the unmarked book despite Ali's masculine reference. The future form identifies third-person feminine agreement. Wanting uses an infinitive followed by the female speaker's habitual wanting form and first-person auxiliary.

### Retrieve and recombine

Tell a six-sentence story covering identity, habit, current activity, past state, completed event and future plan. Ask two questions about it and include a respectful repair request. Review any construction whose agreement you cannot explain, then seek fluent-speaker feedback on pronunciation, natural phrasing and regional conventions.
