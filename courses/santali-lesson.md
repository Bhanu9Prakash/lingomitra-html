# Santali: A Roman-script pattern-first foundation

Editorial status: original AI-assisted draft; fluent-speaker review pending.

This is a limited **romanised Santali grammar foundation**, grounded in the readable Roman transcription of Lars Olsen Skrefsrud’s 1873 *A grammar of the Santhal language*. It is not presented as a reviewed modern standard or as a current conversational phrasebook. **Ol Chiki and other native-script literacy lessons are pending.** Roman transcription here is a learning representation, not a claim that it replaces those writing traditions.

The course begins with usable presence and description statements, then develops participant distinctions, questions, places, possession, and selected action patterns. Examples are original controlled combinations of documented forms; constructions outside that evidence are not guessed. Direct instructions are labelled as such, without claiming a modern politeness level. Native-speaker audio and current regional usage remain review tasks.

### How to read the Roman forms

The source distinguishes palatal **ń** from velar **ṅ** (approximately the final nasal in English sing), and distinguishes retroflex **ṛ** and **ṭ** from ordinary r and t. The checked-consonant mark is consistently represented as **’**, as in oṛak’, chet’, and menak’. Hyphens expose grammatical pieces and are not spoken pauses. We retain the source’s main-example spellings; vowel-quality underlines are retained in forms such as ho̱po̱n and kho̱n. Where a citation form supplies a vowel macron, it is retained too, as in mō̱ṛe and tahēkan. The source’s typography is not a modern standardised orthography or a complete phonetic transcription. No automated conversion to Ol Chiki is attempted.

### References and review notes

The primary reference was inspected as page images, with OCR used only to locate passages: [Skrefsrud’s public-domain grammar, Wikimedia Commons PDF](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/da/A_grammar_of_the_Santhal_language_%28IA_aau0434.0001.001.umich.edu%29.pdf). Supporting source sections are printed pages 2–7 (Roman sound distinctions), 13–24 and 29–38 (nouns, number, pronouns, possession and questions), 146–153 (presence, copula, past states, absence and possession), 156–159 and 173 (regular active patterns and instructions), and 333–340 (syntax and worked examples). Historical cultural judgments and obsolete language classifications are not adopted. Current dialect choices, register, naturalness of recombined examples, and native-script presentation require fluent-speaker review.

## Lesson 1: Say who is present

**Outcome:** Recognise a person marker and report that you or another person is present.

### Think first

Compare mena-ń-a and mena-m-a. Both contain mena and finish with a. Which small part could carry the difference between I and you? Before reading on, try to predict what remains constant when the person changes.

### Build the pattern

Santali can put information that English expresses with separate pronouns inside a verb. In this lesson, mena expresses existence or presence. The small element between mena and final a identifies the person: ń for I, m for one person addressed, and e for a third person. The resulting words can be complete statements in an appropriate situation. Imagine a roll call: the relevant meaning is “I am here/present,” even though the word itself does not separately name “here.”

The full pronouns are iń, am, and oni. Learn them alongside the smaller markers, but do not mechanically insert the full pronoun into every position occupied by a suffix. Full iń names the speaker; the shorter ń fits the present pattern. Oni can refer to a male or female person. English forces a choice between he and she that this form does not make.

The hyphens show grammatical pieces for learning; do not pronounce them as pauses. The letter ń represents a palatal nasal, approximately the consonant in a carefully joined “ny,” rather than ordinary English n. Preserve its accent when copying. This first pattern is about being present, not about being a teacher or being good. A separate construction for descriptions will follow.

### Examples and vocabulary

| Santali in this Roman convention | Meaning |
|---|---|
| mena | exist; be present |
| ń | I, short person marker |
| iń | I, full pronoun |
| am | you, one person |
| oni | he or she |
| mena-ń-a | I am present |
| mena-m-a | you are present, singular |
| mena-e-a | he or she is present |

### Common trap

Do not translate every English am, is, or are with mena. An attendance statement and an identity statement use different constructions. Also keep the final a after the person marker: the order in this pattern is mena, person, a.

### Practice

Produce the Santali form for each meaning using this lesson’s pattern and spelling. Preserve the specified person and number.

1. I, full pronoun
2. You, one person
3. He or she
4. I am present
5. You are present, singular
6. He or she is present

***Answers:***

1. `iń`
2. `am`
3. `oni`
4. `mena-ń-a`
5. `mena-m-a`
6. `mena-e-a`

### Why these answers work

The first three answers retrieve full pronouns; the last three assemble presence statements. Iń corresponds to the speaker, am to the person addressed, and oni to someone being discussed. Mena-ń-a therefore reports the speaker's presence, while mena-e-a reports a third person's presence. The e does not mean female or male. Compare the two answers containing I: one is a pronoun naming a participant, and the other is an entire statement about that participant. They serve different jobs.

### Retrieve and recombine

Picture yourself at a meeting with one friend. Give your own presence statement, then describe your friend’s presence. Point to the exact letter that changes. Tomorrow, recover the three full pronouns before looking at the table.

## Lesson 2: Two people and three or more

**Outcome:** Choose dual and plural pronouns when the number of people matters.

### Think first

English you and they can refer to two people or to a crowd. Santali offers aben beside ape, and onkin beside onko. Which recurring ending looks associated with a pair? Form a hypothesis before checking the explanation.

### Build the pattern

A dual form refers to two participants. A plural form in these basic contrasts refers to more than two. This gives Santali a three-way distinction: singular, dual, and plural. Aben addresses two people, while ape addresses a larger group. Onkin refers to two other people, while onko refers to a larger group of other people. These words are useful for keeping track of the participants in a story or a group task.

The contrast also appears inside presence statements. The short markers ben and kin occupy the same position that m and e occupied in Lesson 1. Mena-ben-a means that the two people addressed are present. Mena-kin-a reports the presence of two other people. For larger groups, substitute pe or ko. The construction still finishes with a, so the number difference belongs to the person marker, not to a changed English-style auxiliary.

Our exercises use literal numbers of people. Actual forms of address also interact with social relationships, and this historical-source foundation does not establish a current etiquette rule. In particular, do not infer that a form must be rude or respectful merely because its classroom translation says two or many. We are learning the grammatical contrast before broader register choices.

### Examples and vocabulary

| Santali in this Roman convention | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ben | you two, short marker |
| ko | they, plural short marker |
| aben | you two |
| ape | you, more than two |
| onkin | they two |
| onko | they, more than two |
| mena-kin-a | they two are present |
| mena-ko-a | they are present, plural |

### Common trap

Do not treat kin as a general plural ending and then use it for a crowd. In the contrasts here, kin is specifically dual. Do not use English they as a sufficient exercise cue without also asking how many people the prompt specifies.

### Why these answers work

The answers pair full pronouns with the matching presence forms. Aben and ape both address the listener side, but distinguish two from a larger group. Onkin and onko refer to people being discussed. In mena-kin-a, the ending kin is enclosed by the same mena and a used for singular statements. Mena-ko-a differs only in the number and person marker. That small change carries a real meaning difference, even though both might be translated “they are present” in ordinary English.

### Retrieve and recombine

Imagine two visitors arrive, followed by three more visitors as a separate group. Refer to the pair and then the larger group using the correct pronouns. Finally retrieve the singular oni and its presence statement from Lesson 1.

## Lesson 3: Who is included in we?

**Outcome:** Distinguish a group that includes the listener from one that excludes the listener.

### Think first

Imagine telling a colleague “We are present.” Does we mean you and the colleague, or you and someone else? Compare aliń with alaṅ. Identify the distinction that English leaves unstated before learning the forms.

### Build the pattern

Santali makes a distinction called inclusive versus exclusive. Inclusive we includes the person addressed. Exclusive we includes the speaker and another person or people, while excluding the person addressed. Exclusive does not mean unfriendly: it simply identifies the group. If you tell a visitor that you and your colleague are ready, the visitor may be outside that particular we.

For a pair, aliń is exclusive: I and one other person, not you. Alaṅ is inclusive: you and I. For a larger group, ale is exclusive and abon is inclusive. The source also records abo as an inclusive plural alternative; exercises consistently use abon so that there is one clear target. The two final nasal letters in aliń and alaṅ are different. Here ń is palatal, while ṅ represents the nasal heard at the end of English sing. Do not collapse either into an unmarked n.

Short person markers preserve the distinction. The exclusive dual marker liń and exclusive plural marker le can fit the presence construction already learned. This lesson practices those two complete forms alongside the four full pronouns. Start every we translation by drawing the participants: mark the speaker, mark the listener, then count the group. Only after that should you select a form.

### Examples and vocabulary

| Santali in this Roman convention | Meaning |
|---|---|
| liń | we two, excluding you, short marker |
| le | we, excluding you, plural short marker |
| aliń | we two, excluding you |
| alaṅ | we two, including you |
| ale | we, excluding you, plural |
| abon | we, including you, plural |
| mena-liń-a | we two are present, excluding you |
| mena-le-a | we are present, excluding you, plural |

### Common trap

English we does not tell you enough to select a Santali form. A prompt must establish both group size and whether the listener belongs to it. Also avoid switching aliń and alaṅ on the assumption that the accents are decorative spelling variants.

### Practice

2. We two, including you
3. We, excluding you, plural
4. We, including you, plural
5. We two are present, excluding you
6. We are present, excluding you, plural

***Answers:***

1. `aliń`
2. `alaṅ`
3. `ale`
4. `abon`
5. `mena-liń-a`
6. `mena-le-a`

### Why these answers work

Aliń and ale exclude the listener but differ in size: two versus more than two. Alaṅ and abon include the listener, again with the same size contrast. Mena-liń-a reports that the exclusive pair is present; mena-le-a reports that the exclusive larger group is present. The final a is unchanged. These forms add a participant distinction to the number system from Lesson 2. Keep that semantic decision separate from the mechanical task of attaching the short marker.

### Retrieve and recombine

Draw three people: yourself, the person you address, and a friend. Name the pair consisting of you and your friend, then the pair consisting of you and your listener. Add a fourth person and recover an exclusive plural form.

## Lesson 4: Describe someone with kan

**Outcome:** Build a description using a quality and the copula kan.

### Think first

Compare mena-e-a, “he or she is present,” with bugi kan-a-e, “he or she is good.” Does the person marker stand in the same position relative to final a? Inspect the two sequences before reading.

### Build the pattern

A copula connects a subject with a description or identity. English uses forms of be for this job. In the source pattern practiced here, Santali uses kan. Place the description before kan, then attach a and the person marker. Bugi means good; bugi kan-a-e therefore describes a third person as good. The source also gives maraṅ, great or big, in this construction.

Notice the difference in order: presence has mena-e-a, while description has kan-a-e. Both contain a third-person e, but in the copula construction it follows a. The marker is not a movable decoration. Learning the two complete shapes now prevents a common error later when presence, identity, and possession appear close together.

Use ń, m, or e after kan-a to describe yourself, one listener, or another person. The adjective stays the same in these examples. Bugi kan-a-ń means “I am good”; bugi kan-a-m addresses one person. A full pronoun is not required in these compact practice statements because the short marker already identifies the participant. When the context has not made the person clear, a noun or full pronoun can help establish the topic. This lesson keeps the subject information inside the practiced ending so that the order remains easy to see.

### Examples and vocabulary

| Santali in this Roman convention | Meaning |
|---|---|
| bugi | good |
| maraṅ | great; big |
| bugi kan-a-ń | I am good |
| bugi kan-a-m | you are good, singular |
| bugi kan-a-e | he or she is good |
| maraṅ kan-a-ń | I am great |
| maraṅ kan-a-m | you are great, singular |
| maraṅ kan-a-e | he or she is great |

### Common trap

Do not build bugi mena-e-a by translating the English word is in isolation. That replaces description with an existence pattern. Also do not use kan-e-a: the taught copula order is kan-a-e. Compare whole constructions, not just roots.

2. You are good, singular
3. He or she is good
4. I am great
5. You are great, singular
6. He or she is great

***Answers:***

1. `bugi kan-a-ń`
2. `bugi kan-a-m`
3. `bugi kan-a-e`
4. `maraṅ kan-a-ń`
5. `maraṅ kan-a-m`
6. `maraṅ kan-a-e`

### Why these answers work

Bugi and maraṅ provide the quality; the rest of each answer identifies the person being described. The ń answers describe the speaker, the m answers describe one listener, and the e answers describe another person. The adjective does not tell you which person is involved. Read bugi kan-a-e in three parts: good, copula plus a, third-person marker. Then compare mena-e-a from Lesson 1, where e occurs before a. The contrast is grammatical rather than an English translation preference.

### Retrieve and recombine

Choose an imaginary character and describe that character as good. Then change only the participant to yourself. Finally give a presence statement about the same character and explain why its ending has a different order.

## Lesson 5: Descriptions of pairs and groups

**Outcome:** Carry dual and plural participant distinctions into descriptive statements.

### Think first

You already know onkin versus onko and kan-a-e for a third-person description. Try predicting the two descriptions “they two are good” and “they are good, more than two.” Keep the quality constant while changing the participant marker.

### Build the pattern

Descriptions can identify a pair or a larger group through the same person-and-number distinctions seen in presence statements. Put kin after kan-a for two people being discussed, and ko for a larger group. Thus bugi kan-a-kin and bugi kan-a-ko differ in how many people are described. The adjective bugi does not itself supply that count in these examples.

Addressing people uses ben for a pair and pe for a larger group. Describing a group that includes yourself but excludes the listener uses liń for a pair and le for more than two. We deliberately retain the exclusive forms from Lesson 3 in the production exercises, so the English cues specify “excluding you.” This is a meaning requirement, not a footnote to ignore.

Build the forms in two stages. First decide the participants without any adjective: two listeners, two other people, an exclusive pair, or a larger group. Then add the quality and the copula frame. This prevents confusion between ben and kin, which both involve two people but place them on different sides of the conversation. A pair of listeners is not the same grammatical person as a pair being discussed.

These are structural practice descriptions. The course does not assign a special modern compliment register to them or claim that every context would prefer this copula phrasing.

### Examples and vocabulary

| Santali in this Roman convention | Meaning |
|---|---|
| kan-a | copula frame before person markers |
| bugi | good |
| bugi kan-a-ben | you two are good |
| bugi kan-a-pe | you are good, plural |
| bugi kan-a-kin | they two are good |
| bugi kan-a-ko | they are good, plural |
| bugi kan-a-liń | we two are good, excluding you |
| bugi kan-a-le | we are good, excluding you, plural |

### Common trap

Do not choose an ending by number alone. Ben and kin both involve two, but ben addresses the pair and kin talks about the pair. Likewise, pe and ko are both plural but have different conversational roles.

## Lesson 6: Identify people with noun descriptions

**Outcome:** Use a noun in the descriptive position and distinguish lexical sex from person marking.

### Think first

Bugi kan-a-e contains an adjective before kan. What would happen if the first word named a person category instead of a quality? Compare koṛa, boy, and koṛi, girl, before deciding whether the final person marker should change.

### Build the pattern

The slot before kan can hold a noun as well as an adjective. Koṛa names a boy and koṛi names a girl. Koṛa kan-a-e and koṛi kan-a-e identify a third person with the corresponding category. The e stays the same. The difference between boy and girl is expressed in the noun, not in a different third-person subject marker.

This is a useful distinction between vocabulary and grammar. English he and she distinguish sex or gender in the pronoun itself. In the Santali forms practiced here, oni and the short e can refer to either. Some nouns have paired lexical forms, such as koṛa and koṛi. Do not turn that observation into a general rule that every noun ending in a becomes feminine by changing to i. Learn the actual vocabulary item and the pattern documented for it.

The letter ṛ represents the retroflex consonant distinguished from ordinary r in the source. The dot matters: it is part of the Roman representation of the word. Buru, mountain or hill, will later help us build place expressions, but this lesson keeps the identity statements about people.

You can describe the speaker with kan-a-ń, a single listener with kan-a-m, or a third person with kan-a-e. Keep the noun before the copula, and choose the final marker from the conversational role rather than from the noun’s meaning.

### Examples and vocabulary

|---|---|

| koṛa | boy |
| koṛi | girl |

| koṛi kan-a-e | she is a girl |
| koṛa kan-a-ń | I am a boy |
| koṛi kan-a-ń | I am a girl |

### Common trap

Do not invent separate male and female versions of e. Also do not generalise the koṛa/koṛi pair into a rule for every noun or adjective. Vocabulary can encode a distinction that a pronoun or verb ending leaves unspecified.

## Lesson 7: Ask who someone is

**Outcome:** Build identity questions without importing English word order.

### Think first

Compare koṛa kan-a-e with okoe kan-a-e? The second keeps the same copula and ending. Which part has changed, and what kind of information is the question asking the listener to supply?

### Build the pattern

Okoe asks who. It occupies the descriptive or identifying position in the model okoe kan-a-e? The question means “Who is he or she?” English places is before the pronoun, but the Santali pattern does not need that English inversion. The interrogative word already indicates that the identity is unknown.

Changing the person marker changes whose identity you ask about. Okoe kan-a-m? asks one listener “Who are you?” Okoe kan-a-ben? addresses a pair, and okoe kan-a-pe? addresses a larger group. For people being discussed, use kin for two and ko for a larger group. The same distinctions that mattered in attendance statements still matter in questions.

A second interrogative, chele, asks what a person is, rather than which person. The historical source contrasts okoe kanae with chele kanae. For now, use okoe for identity and learn chele as a contrast in meaning, without assuming it is the general word for every English what question. Questions about inanimate things use a different item introduced next.

A short answer may simply supply the missing noun or name. A full practice answer can repeat the copula frame with the identifying noun. We do not need to invent a universal greeting or an honorific formula to learn this useful question structure.

### Examples and vocabulary

|---|---|
| okoe | who |
| chele | what a person is; person-category question |
| okoe kan-a-m? | Who are you, singular? |
| okoe kan-a-e? | Who is he or she? |
| okoe kan-a-ben? | Who are you two? |
| okoe kan-a-pe? | Who are you, plural? |
| okoe kan-a-kin? | Who are they two? |
| okoe kan-a-ko? | Who are they, plural? |

### Common trap

Do not translate English “what” by chele when asking about an object such as a house. In this source distinction, chele concerns a person’s category. Also avoid moving kan before okoe in imitation of English question formation.

## Lesson 8: Talk about things that are present

**Outcome:** Distinguish inanimate existence from human presence and from identification.

### Think first

Mena-e-a reports a person’s presence, but menak’-a reports the existence of an inanimate thing. Look for the extra consonant. Then compare ona and oni: which change might distinguish a thing from a person?

### Build the pattern

The inanimate existence form is menak’-a. It means that something exists or is there. The marked k’ is part of the form; it is not a third-person e replaced by an English it pronoun. The source writes checked final consonants with a raised mark, represented here by the apostrophe-like character ’. Preserve that mark rather than adding an extra vowel after the consonant.

Ona can refer to an inanimate that or it, whereas oni refers to a person or other animate referent. Noa is inanimate this. With a noun such as oṛak’, house, you can identify something through the inanimate copula kan-a: oṛak’ kan-a means “It is a house.” The human e is absent. This gives you two separate contrasts: presence versus identity, and animate versus inanimate reference.

A question about the identity of an inanimate thing can use chet’, what, with the same kan-a frame. Chet’ kan-a? asks what it is. The answer oṛak’ kan-a supplies the category. Oṛak’ menak’-a, by contrast, reports that a house exists or is there; it does not answer which kind of thing an already identified object is.

Read the apostrophe mark in chet’ and oṛak’ as part of the consonant spelling convention. It does not indicate possession as an English apostrophe might.

### Examples and vocabulary

| Santali in this Roman convention | Meaning |
|---|---|
| oṛak’ | house |
| chet’ | what, inanimate question |
| ona | that; it, inanimate |
| noa | this, inanimate |
| menak’-a | it exists; it is there |
| oṛak’ menak’-a | a house is there |
| oṛak’ kan-a | it is a house |
| chet’ kan-a? | What is it? |

### Common trap

Do not use mena-e-a for a house merely because English translates both a person and a house with is there. Also distinguish oṛak’ kan-a, identifying it as a house, from oṛak’ menak’-a, reporting that a house exists.

### Why these answers work

Ona and noa are the inanimate pointing forms. Menak’-a is the bare existence statement, and oṛak’ menak’-a specifies what exists. Oṛak’ kan-a identifies a thing as a house; chet’ kan-a? asks for that identity. The absence of e in the copula examples is deliberate because the subject is inanimate. The marked k’ belongs to the existence form and also occurs independently in the noun oṛak’. Similar spelling pieces can belong to different words without having the same grammatical job.

## Lesson 9: Locate people and things

**Outcome:** Attach a place marker and report where someone or something is.

### Think first

Compare oṛak’, house, with oṛak’-re mena-e-a, “he or she is in the house.” Which piece turns the house into a location? Decide whether that piece names the person or relates the place to the statement.

### Build the pattern

The marker re expresses a location, often translated in or on according to the noun and situation. Attach it after the place noun. Oṛak’-re means in the house; buru-re means on or in the mountain or hill area. The source’s location construction combines this phrase with existence: oṛak’-re mena-e-a reports a person in the house.

The place phrase does not change just because the person changes. Use mena-ń-a for yourself, mena-m-a for a listener, or mena-e-a for a third person. A pair of other people takes mena-kin-a. The place and the participant each have their own job, so a listener can recover both where and who from the complete statement.

For an inanimate thing in the house, use oṛak’-re menak’-a. English might translate this as “It is in the house.” That English it does not justify inserting e. The animate versus inanimate difference from Lesson 8 remains active when you add a location.

Location also differs from destination. Re in this lesson describes being at a place, not going towards it. Later, movement expressions will use a destination construction. Keeping that difference visible makes the course more useful than memorising re as a universal translation of every English preposition associated with places.

## Lesson 10: Report absence instead of presence

**Outcome:** Use the negative existence family without confusing it with a negative description.

### Think first

You know menak’-a for an inanimate thing’s presence. Compare banuk’-a for its absence. Predict whether replacing mena with banu will automatically work in every human form; then inspect the singular and plural examples carefully.

### Build the pattern

Banuk’-a is the inanimate negative existence form. It can mean that a thing is not there or does not exist. Oṛak’ banuk’-a therefore reports that there is no house. It does not mean that a particular object is not a house. That second meaning would deny an identity, which belongs to a different construction.

Human forms in the source are not a perfectly uniform substitution table. For the first person singular the form is banu-gi-ń-a. The source’s third-person singular is banu-gi-ch’-a. We retain these forms as complete patterns instead of pretending that every negative can be built by putting a word equivalent to English not beside the positive form. For two or more participants, the source records banuk’ with the familiar markers, such as banuk’-kin-a and banuk’-ko-a.

A location phrase can be retained when presence changes to absence. Oṛak’-re banuk’-a says that an inanimate referent is not in the house. The place is still oṛak’-re; the new information concerns existence at that place. This is useful in a search: a person may ask where an item is, and an answer can rule out one location.

Keep descriptive negatives outside this small pattern. The positive copula kan and the existence verb mena were already separate; their negatives need not collapse into one shape.

## Lesson 11: Point out this and that

### Think first

### Build the pattern

A demonstrative points to a referent in the situation: this one, that one, these, or those. The source distinguishes noi, an animate this, from oni, an animate that. For inanimate things the corresponding forms are noa and ona. These are grammatical pointing choices, not substitutes for personal names. A listener still needs context to know which person or object you mean.

Inanimate demonstratives also have recorded dual and plural forms, but this lesson keeps active practice to the most useful singular pair and the animate number contrasts. Mastering a small, consistent set is more valuable than treating a long historical table as a collection of interchangeable spellings.

## Lesson 14: Recognise possession after the noun

**Outcome:** Build short possessive noun phrases using attached owner markers.

### Think first

You know iń-ak’ oṛak’ for my house. The source also gives oṛak’-tiń. Which piece now identifies the owner, and on which side of the noun does it stand? Compare the two routes before choosing a rule.

### Build the pattern

Santali can also express the possessor through a marker following the noun. The source’s oṛak’-tiń means my house, and oṛak’-tam means your house when addressing one person. The third-person owner marker is tae. These forms identify the owner without requiring a separate English-style possessive word before the noun.

The markers have their own shapes. Tiń is not simply the subject marker ń pasted directly after every noun; tam is not merely m. Recognising the t element helps distinguish possessor marking from the person markers already seen in descriptions and presence statements. The owner is a grammatical participant, but ownership and being the subject of a description are different relationships.

For an exclusive plural owner group, tale corresponds to our. The source also documents possessive suffixes with animate nouns, such as ho̱po̱n-tae, his or her son. This is why you should not force the ren versus ak’ linker rule from Lesson 13 onto every possible possessive construction. That rule described a particular phrase structure; the suffix construction is another documented route.

These compact phrases are useful for labelling and reference. In a conversation, the referent must still be identifiable from context. The suffix tells whose house or son is meant, but it does not by itself say where that referent is, whether it exists, or whether the speaker is currently looking at it.

## Lesson 17: Separate location from source

**Outcome:** Use kho̱n to express from and recognise noun-number marking before a relation marker.

### Think first

Buru-re means on the hill, while buru-kho̱n means from the hill. Which piece changed? Now inspect buru-kin-kho̱n: where is the information about two hills placed relative to the source marker?

### Build the pattern

Kho̱n expresses a source or separation, commonly translated from. It follows the noun, just as re followed the noun to mark a location. Buru-kho̱n is from the hill. The noun still identifies the place; kho̱n gives its relationship to the rest of the message. A place phrase can therefore answer different questions depending on its final marker.

Nouns in the source can also take kin for a pair and ko for a larger group. The relation marker follows those number markers. Buru-kin-kho̱n means from the two hills; buru-ko-kho̱n means from the hills. Similarly, koṛa-kin-kho̱n means from the two boys. These are phrases, not complete motion statements. The course does not silently add a verb meaning come or go where the Santali answer has none.

Notice how the same visible kin and ko now occur on nouns rather than inside a finite verb. Their number meaning is related, but their location in the expression matters. In mena-kin-a, kin identifies the dual animate participant of a presence statement. In buru-kin-kho̱n, it belongs to the noun phrase before the source relation.

| oṛak’ benao-ket’-a-ko | they built a house, completed event, plural |

### Common trap

Do not select a construction by spotting one English word. A house is there, it is a house, and someone is building a house express different relationships. Also do not lose the pair-versus-group distinction when the exercise returns to a familiar noun.

### Practice

Produce the Santali form for each meaning using this lesson’s pattern and spelling. Preserve the specified person and number.

1. Who are the two visitors?
2. The two visitors are in the house
3. It is a house
4. I have a house
5. They are building a house now, plural
6. They built a house, completed event, plural

***Answers:***

1. `okoe kan-a-kin?`
2. `oṛak’-re mena-kin-a`
3. `oṛak’ kan-a`
4. `oṛak’ menak’-tiń-a`
5. `oṛak’ benao-et’-kan-a-ko`
6. `oṛak’ benao-ket’-a-ko`

### Why these answers work

The first answer asks for the identity of two people and therefore uses the copula with kin. The second locates that same pair using mena. The third identifies an inanimate thing with kan-a, while the fourth asserts the speaker’s possession through menak’-tiń-a. The final two keep the plural builders and house object constant but contrast ongoing et’-kan with completed ket’. These answers demonstrate independent control of meaning, participants, animacy, and aspect within the course’s verified pattern set.

### Retrieve and recombine

Retell the scene with one visitor instead of two, then with an exclusive pair of builders instead of a third-person group. Check each changed participant marker against earlier lessons. Finally choose one statement and make either a documented condition or a perhaps version, explaining the changed meaning.
