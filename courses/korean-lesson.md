# Korean: A pattern-first foundation

This course teaches contemporary standard South Korean, written in Hangul. Everyday production defaults to the polite 해요 style, suitable in many ordinary interactions with unfamiliar adults. Formal 합니다 style is introduced separately, and casual speech is identified rather than silently mixed into polite examples. Korean endings mark social stance, tense and clause relationships, not the speaker's gender.

Readings use Revised Romanization conventions, including eo for ㅓ and eu for ㅡ. These are single vowel sounds, not sequences of English letters to pronounce separately. The readings are an aid to Hangul, not a substitute orthography. Common sound changes are reflected where relevant, such as 같이 gachi and 합니다 hamnida. Word spacing generally follows the Korean examples; particles attach to their nouns. Proper names retain a consistent simple spelling.

### References and review notes

The [National Institute of Korean Language romanization guide](https://www.korean.go.kr/front_eng/roman/roman_01.do) was consulted for vowel notation and common sound-change conventions. [How to Study Korean's introductory lesson index](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/unit1/unit-1-lessons-1-8/) was consulted for the scope of basic sentence structure and particles. The lessons and examples below are original. Topic versus subject nuance, honorific choices and natural omission of pronouns remain priorities for fluent-speaker review.

## Lesson 1: Introducing yourself politely

**Outcome:** give a simple identity in polite everyday Korean.

### Think first

If 저 means “I” and 학생 means “student,” where should the identity ending go? Would Korean need a separate word before student corresponding to English am?

### A topic and an identity ending

저는 학생이에요 means “I am a student.” 저 is a humble first-person pronoun, and 는 marks the topic, roughly “as for me.” The identity ending 이에요 attaches after a noun ending in a consonant. After a vowel-final noun, use 예요: 저는 의사예요, “I am a doctor.” These are forms of the copula 이다, which links an identity or classification.

The ending is polite, but it does not conjugate for I, you, he or she. A student's gender does not change 학생이에요. Korean nouns do not have a grammatical masculine/feminine agreement system. Context usually supplies the person being described when a pronoun is omitted.

Avoid translating English you mechanically as 당신. Korean often uses a name, title or no explicit pronoun where English uses you. 선생님 is teacher with an honorific title element and can also be used respectfully to address someone in appropriate contexts. Today we introduce ourselves rather than constructing a full second-person pronoun table.

The particles and endings are attached to the preceding word in writing: 저는, 학생이에요. Hangul syllable blocks group letters, but spaces still separate words and phrases. Learning that boundary now will make later object and location particles easier to recognise.

### Working vocabulary

| Native form | Reading | Meaning and use |
|---|---|---|
| 저 | jeo | I, humble |
| 저는 | jeoneun | as for me; I as topic |
| 학생 | haksaeng | student |
| 의사 | uisa | doctor |
| 선생님 | seonsaengnim | teacher; respectful title |
| 이에요 | ieyo | polite identity ending after a consonant |
| 예요 | yeyo | polite identity ending after a vowel |

### Worked examples

- `저는 학생이에요.` - `Jeoneun haksaengieyo.`: I am a student; the noun ends in a consonant.
- `저는 의사예요.` - `Jeoneun uisayeyo.`: I am a doctor; the vowel-final noun takes 예요.
- `민수 씨는 학생이에요.` - `Minsu ssineun haksaengieyo.`: Minsu is a student; 씨 follows the name as a polite title.

### Common trap

Do not use the same identity ending after every noun. Also, polite Korean does not require repeating 저 in every sentence once the speaker is clear, and 당신 is not a universal neutral substitute for English you.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: I am a student.
2. Say: I am a doctor.
3. Say: Minsu is a doctor. Use 민수 씨는.
4. Say politely: It is a student, with the subject understood.

***Answers:***

1. `저는 학생이에요.` - `Jeoneun haksaengieyo.`
2. `저는 의사예요.` - `Jeoneun uisayeyo.`
3. `민수 씨는 의사예요.` - `Minsu ssineun uisayeyo.`
4. `학생이에요.` - `Haksaengieyo.`

### Why these answers work

학생 takes 이에요 after its final consonant, while 의사 takes 예요 after a vowel. 씨는 marks Minsu politely as the topic. The last answer omits an understood subject but keeps the polite ending.

### Retrieve and recombine

Introduce yourself and one imaginary classmate. Change the occupation once, deciding whether the final sound of the noun requires 이에요 or 예요 before you say the sentence.


## Lesson 2: Hangul blocks and useful identifications

**Outcome:** recognise how letters combine into syllable blocks and ask what an object is.

### Think first

The word 물 has three letters arranged in one block. Does one block always mean one individual alphabet letter, or can it represent a whole syllable?

### Letters inside blocks

Hangul is an alphabet organised into syllable blocks. 물 contains ㅁ, ㅜ and ㄹ: an initial consonant, a vowel and a final consonant. 차 contains ㅊ and ㅏ without a final consonant. A syllable beginning with a vowel uses ㅇ as a silent initial placeholder, as in 우유. At the end of a syllable, ㅇ represents ng, so its position matters.

The vowels ㅓ and ㅡ are written eo and eu in our readings. Each represents one vowel, not two English vowels. Consonants also distinguish plain, aspirated and tense series, such as ㄱ, ㅋ and ㄲ. English spelling approximations do not fully capture that contrast; use the script and listening together.

이거 means this thing in everyday speech. 이거 뭐예요? asks “What is this?” 뭐 means what and combines here with the polite identity ending. A response such as 책이에요, “it is a book,” uses the noun-final consonant rule from Lesson 1. 우유예요 uses the vowel-final rule.

The words in the table are short enough to inspect block by block. First recognise the blocks as syllables, then identify their component letters. This is more useful than assuming each square-shaped block is an indivisible character that must be memorised separately.

### Working vocabulary

| Native form | Reading | Meaning and use |
|---|---|---|
| 물 | mul | water |
| 차 | cha | tea; also car in another context |
| 책 | chaek | book |
| 우유 | uyu | milk |
| 커피 | keopi | coffee |
| 이거 | igeo | this thing |
| 뭐 | mwo | what |

### Worked examples

- `이거 뭐예요?` - `Igeo mwoyeyo?`: What is this? The object is presented as the topic of the question.
- `책이에요.` - `Chaegieyo.`: It is a book; the final consonant links into the following vowel in speech.
- `우유예요.` - `Uyuyeyo.`: It is milk; the noun is vowel-final.

### Common trap

Do not pronounce eo as an English e followed by o. Also, the initial ㅇ in a vowel-starting block is silent, while final ㅇ is pronounced ng. A symbol's position changes its role.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Ask: What is this?
2. Identify politely: It is coffee.
3. Identify politely: It is water.
4. Identify politely: It is tea.

***Answers:***

1. `이거 뭐예요?` - `Igeo mwoyeyo?`
2. `커피예요.` - `Keopiyeyo.`
3. `물이에요.` - `Murieyo.`
4. `차예요.` - `Chayeyo.`

### Why these answers work

커피 and 차 take 예요 after vowels. 물 takes 이에요 after a final consonant, with ㄹ linking as r in the reading murieyo. The question retains 뭐예요 as the polite request for identity.

### Retrieve and recombine

Read 물, 차 and 책 without looking at the romanisation. Then identify a drink and a book, explaining how the final consonant of the noun affects the identity ending.


## Lesson 3: Identity questions and corrections

**Outcome:** ask whether an identification is correct and deny it politely.

### Think first

Could 학생이에요 become a question without moving the noun? What different expression would be needed to say someone is not a student?

### Intonation and 아니다

In everyday polite speech, a yes-or-no question can use the same form as a statement with question intonation: 학생이에요? means “Are you a student?” or “Is the person a student?” according to context. Korean does not invert an English-style auxiliary. The written question mark makes the function clear, while speech uses intonation and context.

아니에요 is the polite form of 아니다, not to be. In a full negative identity pattern, the noun commonly takes 이 after a consonant or 가 after a vowel: 저는 학생이 아니에요, “I am not a student”; 저는 의사가 아니에요, “I am not a doctor.” These particles will receive a broader subject explanation later. Here, learn their required place in this useful construction.

네 means yes, and 아니요 is a conventional no response. 아니요 and 아니에요 are related-looking but serve different functions in the examples: one answers no, the other forms the negative predicate. A short 아니요 can stand alone; a correction adds the actual identity.

Questions in a conversation need not repeat a pronoun. 학생이에요? directed to a new acquaintance is often more natural than forcing an explicit you. The polite ending does the social work while the situation identifies the addressee.

### Working vocabulary

| Native form | Reading | Meaning and use |
|---|---|---|
| 네 | ne | yes |
| 아니요 | aniyo | no |
| 아니에요 | anieyo | is not, polite |
| 학생이 | haksaengi | student with 이 |
| 의사가 | uisaga | doctor with 가 |
| 맞아요 | majayo | that is right |
| 학생이에요? | haksaengieyo? | is someone a student? |

### Worked examples

- `학생이에요?` - `Haksaengieyo?`: Are you a student? The context identifies the listener.
- `저는 학생이 아니에요.` - `Jeoneun haksaengi anieyo.`: I am not a student; 이 follows a consonant-final noun.
- `저는 의사가 아니에요.` - `Jeoneun uisaga anieyo.`: I am not a doctor; 가 follows a vowel-final noun.

### Common trap

Do not use 아니요 as the predicate in 저는 학생이 아니요. The sentence requires 아니에요. Also, 아니에요 is the standard spelling here, not the tempting form 아니예요.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Ask politely: Are you a doctor? Omit the pronoun.
2. Say: I am not a student.
3. Say: I am not a doctor.
4. Answer simply: No.

***Answers:***

1. `의사예요?` - `Uisayeyo?`
2. `저는 학생이 아니에요.` - `Jeoneun haksaengi anieyo.`
3. `저는 의사가 아니에요.` - `Jeoneun uisaga anieyo.`
4. `아니요.` - `Aniyo.`

### Why these answers work

의사예요 becomes a question through intonation and punctuation. The negative noun takes 이 after 학생 and 가 after 의사, followed by 아니에요. 아니요 is a complete independent response rather than a sentence-ending identity predicate.

### Retrieve and recombine

Create a mistaken guess about your occupation, then deny it and provide the correct identity. Keep the question polite without inserting an unnecessary Korean word for you.


## Lesson 4: Topics, subjects and what is new

**Outcome:** distinguish the topic particles 은/는 from the subject particles 이/가 in simple contexts.

### Think first

If a conversation is already about you, 저는 can establish that topic. But if someone asks who is a student, which expression would highlight the newly identified person?

### Different jobs for particles

은 follows a consonant and 는 follows a vowel to mark a topic or contrast. 이 follows a consonant and 가 follows a vowel to mark a subject in the patterns here. These are not interchangeable labels meaning “the person doing the verb” in every sentence. A topic may frame what the sentence is about, while a subject participates in the predicate's grammatical relation.

저는 학생이에요 introduces information about me. 제가 학생이에요 can identify me as the student, for example in answer to a question about who the student is. 저 plus 가 becomes 제가, not 저가 in the standard form. 누가 학생이에요? asks “Who is a student?” 누구 plus 가 contracts to 누가.

For an object, 책은 여기 있어요 can mean “as for the book, it is here,” while 책이 있어요 announces that a book exists or is present. We will teach 있어요 fully in a later lesson; this contrast is an advance glimpse of information packaging, not a demand to master all nuance immediately.

Topic and subject choices depend heavily on discourse. The exercises specify the intended context rather than claiming that every isolated English I must always become 저는 or 제가. A different context can make a different particle natural without making the basic sentence ungrammatical.

### Working vocabulary

| Native form | Reading | Meaning and use |
|---|---|---|
| 은 | eun | topic after a consonant |
| 는 | neun | topic after a vowel |
| 이 | i | subject after a consonant |
| 가 | ga | subject after a vowel |
| 제가 | jega | I with subject particle |
| 누가 | nuga | who as subject |
| 여기 | yeogi | here |

### Worked examples

- `저는 학생이에요.` - `Jeoneun haksaengieyo.`: As for me, I am a student; this introduces information about the speaker.
- `제가 학생이에요.` - `Jega haksaengieyo.`: I am the student; the speaker is identified as the relevant person.
- `누가 의사예요?` - `Nuga uisayeyo?`: Who is a doctor? The unknown person is the subject.

### Common trap

Do not treat 은/는 as a universal subject marker. Also, the standard subject form of 저 is 제가. Particle choice cannot always be decided from an isolated English sentence without its conversational context.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Ask: Who is a student?
2. Identify yourself as the doctor: I am the doctor. Use 제가.
3. Introduce your identity as a topic: I am a student. Use 저는.
4. Ask: Who is a doctor?

***Answers:***

1. `누가 학생이에요?` - `Nuga haksaengieyo?`
2. `제가 의사예요.` - `Jega uisayeyo.`
3. `저는 학생이에요.` - `Jeoneun haksaengieyo.`
4. `누가 의사예요?` - `Nuga uisayeyo?`

### Why these answers work

누가 asks for a subject. 제가 identifies the speaker as the requested person. 저는 establishes a topic for an introduction. The identity ending still depends on the final sound of 학생 or 의사, independently of the earlier particle.

### Retrieve and recombine

Imagine two settings: introducing yourself to a group and answering “who is the doctor?” Choose a first-person particle for each and explain the conversational difference without inventing a rigid English translation rule.


## Lesson 5: Actions and objects

**Outcome:** build a polite action sentence with an object before the verb.

### Think first

If 책 is book and 읽어요 is read, where should the reading action go in a neutral Korean sentence? How can the noun signal that it is the object?

### Object particles and final predicates

The object particle is 을 after a consonant and 를 after a vowel. 책을 읽어요 means “I read a book” or “I am reading a book,” with the subject understood. 물을 마셔요 means “I drink water.” 커피를 마셔요 uses 를 after the vowel-final noun 커피.

Korean typically places the main predicate at the end of a neutral clause. A useful starting order is topic or subject, then object, then verb. 저는 책을 읽어요 therefore makes each relation visible. Word order can vary for emphasis, and particles are often omitted in casual conversation, but the explicit neutral pattern is a reliable learning foundation.

The polite form does not change for first, second or third person. 읽어요 can refer to me, you or another person, depending on context. It can describe a habit or a current action; English chooses between read and am reading according to the setting.

The dictionary forms are 읽다, to read, and 마시다, to drink. Remove 다 to identify a stem, but do not assume the polite ending always attaches without sound or vowel changes. Today the complete forms 읽어요 and 마셔요 give usable anchors; the next lesson explains the common vowel patterns.

### Working vocabulary

| Native form | Reading | Meaning and use |
|---|---|---|
| 책을 | chaegeul | book as object |
| 물을 | mureul | water as object |
| 커피를 | keopireul | coffee as object |
| 읽다 | ikda | to read, dictionary form |
| 읽어요 | ilgeoyo | read, polite |
| 마시다 | masida | to drink |
| 마셔요 | masyeoyo | drink, polite |

### Worked examples

- `저는 책을 읽어요.` - `Jeoneun chaegeul ilgeoyo.`: I read a book; the object comes before the verb.
- `물을 마셔요.` - `Mureul masyeoyo.`: I drink water; the subject is understood.
- `민수 씨는 커피를 마셔요.` - `Minsu ssineun keopireul masyeoyo.`: Minsu drinks coffee; the verb does not acquire a third-person ending.

### Common trap

Do not add an English-style s to a Korean verb for he or she. Also, 을/를 attaches to the object noun, not to the verb, and its choice follows the noun's final sound.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: I drink coffee. Include 저는.
2. Say politely: I read a book. Omit the subject.
3. Say: Minsu drinks water.
4. Ask politely: Do you read books? Omit the subject.

***Answers:***

1. `저는 커피를 마셔요.` - `Jeoneun keopireul masyeoyo.`
2. `책을 읽어요.` - `Chaegeul ilgeoyo.`
3. `민수 씨는 물을 마셔요.` - `Minsu ssineun mureul masyeoyo.`
4. `책을 읽어요?` - `Chaegeul ilgeoyo?`

### Why these answers work

커피 takes 를 after a vowel, while 책 and 물 take 을 after consonants. The same polite verb form serves different subjects and becomes a question through intonation. The subject can be omitted when the conversation makes it clear.

### Retrieve and recombine

Replace water with coffee in a drinking sentence, then change the action to reading. Retrieve the appropriate object particle from the noun's final sound rather than from its English meaning.


## Lesson 6: Building everyday polite verb forms

**Outcome:** recognise the common 아요/어요 pattern and the frequent 해요 contraction.

### Think first

가다 becomes 가요, 먹다 becomes 먹어요, and 하다 becomes 해요. Can one broad vowel pattern explain much of this, while still leaving special forms to learn?

### Stem and ending

For many regular verbs, a stem whose last vowel is ㅏ or ㅗ takes 아요; other vowels usually take 어요. 가 plus 아요 contracts to 가요, “go.” 오 plus 아요 becomes 와요, “come.” 먹 plus 어요 becomes 먹어요, “eat.” A vowel-final stem may contract with the ending, so the written result is not always the stem plus three visibly separate letters.

하다, to do, has the common polite form 해요. Many activity words combine with 하다: 공부하다, to study, becomes 공부해요. 일하다, to work, becomes 일해요. These are frequent productive combinations, but not every noun can be turned into a natural verb merely by adding 하다.

지금 means now. 지금 공부해요 can mean “I am studying now.” The same verb form can describe an ordinary habit in a different time context. This course begins with the polite everyday style and does not add a formal 합니다 ending to a form already ending in 요.

Irregular stems require additional patterns, which will be introduced selectively. Learn one dictionary form and one polite anchor together. That gives you both a way to look the word up and a form you can actually use in a conversation.

### Working vocabulary

| Native form | Reading | Meaning and use |
|---|---|---|
| 가다 | gada | to go |
| 가요 | gayo | go, polite |
| 오다 | oda | to come |
| 와요 | wayo | come, polite |
| 먹어요 | meogeoyo | eat, polite |
| 해요 | haeyo | do, polite |
| 공부해요 | gongbuhaeyo | study, polite |
| 지금 | jigeum | now |

### Worked examples

- `지금 공부해요.` - `Jigeum gongbuhaeyo.`: I am studying now; 하다 becomes 해요.
- `밥을 먹어요.` - `Babeul meogeoyo.`: I eat a meal or rice; 먹 takes 어요.
- `민수 씨가 와요.` - `Minsu ssiga wayo.`: Minsu is coming; 오 plus 아요 contracts to 와요.

### Common trap

Do not attach 아요 to every stem ending in the letter 다; 다 is the dictionary ending, not the vowel used to select the polite form. Remove it and inspect the actual stem.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say politely: I go.
2. Say politely: I come.
3. Say: I am studying now.
4. Say: I eat a meal. Use 밥을.

***Answers:***

1. `가요.` - `Gayo.`
2. `와요.` - `Wayo.`
3. `지금 공부해요.` - `Jigeum gongbuhaeyo.`
4. `밥을 먹어요.` - `Babeul meogeoyo.`

### Why these answers work

가요 and 와요 show vowel contraction. 공부해요 uses the common 하다 pattern. 먹어요 takes 어요 after a stem with ㅓ, and 밥을 supplies a consonant-final object noun with 을.

### Retrieve and recombine

Take 가다, 오다 and 공부하다 from dictionary form to polite speech without looking. Explain which forms contract and which use the special 하다 to 해요 relationship.


## Lesson 7: Not doing and not being able

**Outcome:** distinguish an ordinary negative action from inability.

### Think first

If someone does not drink coffee, is that necessarily because they cannot drink it? What distinction would two different negative words make available?

### 안 and 못

안 before an ordinary verb expresses simple negation: 안 가요, “I do not go” or “I am not going.” 커피를 안 마셔요 means “I do not drink coffee.” The negative word precedes the predicate, while the object retains its particle and position.

못 expresses inability or practical failure: 못 가요, “I cannot go.” The limitation may be situational rather than a permanent lack of skill. A scheduling conflict can make 못 가요 appropriate even when walking itself is physically possible. Context distinguishes inability from unwillingness.

For many noun-plus-하다 activity verbs, 안 commonly goes before 해요 after the activity noun: 공부 안 해요, “I do not study.” 공부를 안 해요 is also possible with an object particle. This lesson uses 공부 안 해요 as the compact taught form. Do not assume that every compound or lexical verb can be split in exactly the same way.

아니에요 remains the negative identity predicate from earlier lessons; it is not the ordinary negative action pattern. The three constructions answer different questions: is someone a student, do they study, and are they able to study? Keeping those meanings distinct prevents an English not from flattening all Korean negatives into one form.

### Working vocabulary

| Native form | Reading | Meaning and use |
|---|---|---|
| 안 | an | not, ordinary negation |
| 못 | mot | cannot; unable to |
| 안 가요 | an gayo | do not go |
| 못 가요 | mot gayo | cannot go |
| 안 마셔요 | an masyeoyo | do not drink |
| 공부 안 해요 | gongbu an haeyo | do not study |
| 오늘 | oneul | today |

### Worked examples

- `오늘 안 가요.` - `Oneul an gayo.`: I am not going today; this is ordinary negation.
- `오늘 못 가요.` - `Oneul mot gayo.`: I cannot go today; a limitation prevents it.
- `커피를 안 마셔요.` - `Keopireul an masyeoyo.`: I do not drink coffee; the object stays before the negative verb.

### Common trap

Do not use 못 when you merely want to deny a habit or choice without implying inability. Also, 아니에요 negates an identity, while 안 or 못 works with the action patterns taught here.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: I do not drink water.
2. Say: I cannot go today.
3. Say: I am not studying. Use 공부 안 해요.
4. Say: I am not coming today.

***Answers:***

1. `물을 안 마셔요.` - `Mureul an masyeoyo.`
2. `오늘 못 가요.` - `Oneul mot gayo.`
3. `공부 안 해요.` - `Gongbu an haeyo.`
4. `오늘 안 와요.` - `Oneul an wayo.`

### Why these answers work

안 negates drinking, studying and coming without necessarily claiming inability. 못 in the second answer signals a practical limitation. 오늘 sets the time, and the polite endings stay unchanged because negation does not create a new person paradigm.

### Retrieve and recombine

Give one action you choose not to do and another you cannot do today. Explain why you selected 안 or 못, then change only the time expression to test whether the meaning still fits.


## Lesson 8: Being at a place and having something

**Outcome:** locate a person or object and state existence or absence.

### Think first

책이에요 identifies something as a book. Would that same identity ending be enough to say that a book exists here, or does Korean use another predicate?

### 있어요 and 없어요

있어요 expresses existence, presence or possession in suitable constructions. 책이 있어요 means “there is a book” or “I have a book,” depending on context. 없어요 expresses absence: 책이 없어요, “there is no book” or “I do not have a book.” The item takes subject particle 이 or 가, not the object particle used with reading.

For location, attach 에 to a place and use 있어요: 집에 있어요, “I am at home.” 책이 학교에 있어요 means the book is at school. 학교, school, takes the same 에 as 집; this particle does not alternate after consonants and vowels.

어디 means where. 어디에 있어요? asks where someone or something is. In natural speech 에 is sometimes omitted from the question, but the full form makes the location relation visible. 여기, 거기 and 저기 mean here, there near the listener or previously mentioned, and over there, with distinctions depending on context.

Possession can be introduced with a topic: 저는 책이 있어요, “I have a book.” Literally, the topic is me and the book exists in relation to me. Korean does not require an English-style have verb taking a direct object in this construction.

### Working vocabulary

| Native form | Reading | Meaning and use |
|---|---|---|
| 있어요 | isseoyo | exists; is present; have |
| 없어요 | eopseoyo | does not exist; is absent |
| 집 | jip | home; house |
| 학교 | hakgyo | school |

## Lesson 9: Where an action happens and where it goes

**Outcome:** distinguish action location 에서 from destination 에.

### Think first

집에 있어요 means being at home. If you study at home, does Korean use the same particle for the place where the action occurs?

### Place roles differ

에서 commonly marks the location of an action: 집에서 공부해요, “I study at home.” 도서관에서 책을 읽어요 means “I read a book at the library.” Contrast 집에 있어요, where 에 marks the static location of presence. The English phrase “at home” does not tell you which Korean particle to choose; the predicate and relation do.

에 also marks a destination with 가요 or 와요: 학교에 가요, “I go to school.” The same particle serves static location with 있어요 and destination with a motion verb. There is no contradiction because the verb makes the relation clear.

에서 can also mark a source, as in 한국에서 왔어요, “I came from Korea,” once the past form is learned. Today's production focuses on action locations so that the contrast remains manageable. Do not assume that one particle has exactly one English preposition as its permanent equivalent.

A sentence can contain both a place and an object before its final verb: 도서관에서 책을 읽어요. In our neutral order the setting comes before the object, although context can motivate other orders. Each particle tells the listener the noun's job, allowing you to add useful detail without losing the main action.

### Working vocabulary

| Native form | Reading | Meaning and use |
|---|---|---|
| 에서 | eseo | at, action location; from in other contexts |
| 에 | e | at or to, depending on predicate |
| 도서관 | doseogwan | library |
| 카페 | kape | café |
| 학교에 | hakgyoe | to school; at school with existence |
| 집에서 | jibeseo | at home, action location |
| 공원 | gongwon | park |

### Worked examples

- `집에서 공부해요.` - `Jibeseo gongbuhaeyo.`: I study at home; the place hosts the action.
- `학교에 가요.` - `Hakgyoe gayo.`: I go to school; 에 marks the destination.
- `카페에서 커피를 마셔요.` - `Kapeeseo keopireul masyeoyo.`: I drink coffee at a café; location and object both precede the verb.

### Common trap

Do not choose a particle by translating English at in isolation. Use 에 with the presence pattern and 에서 for the location of an action in these examples. A destination with 가요 also uses 에.

### Practice

2. Say: I go home.
3. Say: I drink water at school.
4. Say: I am at the library.

***Answers:***

### Why these answers work

Reading and drinking use 에서 for the action setting. Going uses 에 for its destination. The final answer uses 에 with 있어요 because it describes presence rather than an action performed there. Object particles remain 을/를.

## Lesson 10: Time expressions and routines

**Outcome:** place familiar actions at a time of day or on a particular day.

### Think first

If 에 can mark a point in time, should every word meaning today, tomorrow or now automatically receive it?

### Time words have their own patterns

아침에 means in the morning, and 저녁에 means in the evening. The particle 에 marks many time expressions, just as it marks certain location relations. 아침에 커피를 마셔요 means “I drink coffee in the morning.” 밤에 means at night.

오늘, 내일 and 어제 mean today, tomorrow and yesterday. These common day words normally appear without 에 in the basic adverbial use taught here: 오늘 공부해요, “I study today.” 지금, now, likewise does not require 에. Learn the difference through the actual expression rather than adding the particle after every English time phrase.

매일 means every day. 매일 책을 읽어요 describes a reading habit. The ordinary polite present form can express this habit; Korean does not need a separate third-person s or a mandatory progressive marker just because English uses a different tense form in translation.

A time phrase often appears early to establish the frame, followed by a location, an object and the final predicate. 오늘 도서관에서 책을 읽어요 is therefore a useful expandable pattern. It remains one event, with each phrase supplying a distinct piece of information. Keep the sentence short enough that you can track the particles before adding more detail.

| 내일 | naeil | tomorrow |

## Lesson 11: My things and shared ownership

**Outcome:** identify whose object something is and connect possession to an existence statement.

### Think first

저는 책이 있어요 means I have a book. If you want to say “my book” inside another sentence, what different construction identifies the owner directly?

### Possessor before possession

의 connects an owner to something owned: 민수 씨의 책, Minsu's book. In many everyday noun phrases this particle can be omitted when the relation is clear, but the explicit form makes the structure visible. The possessor comes before the possessed noun. This order resembles English Minsu's book, although Korean uses its own particle rather than an English apostrophe.

저의 commonly contracts to 제, meaning my in humble self-reference. 제 책 is my book, and 제 커피 is my coffee. The noun itself does not acquire a gender or person ending merely because it is possessed. 제 책이에요 means “it is my book,” with the identity ending selected by 책 as usual.

누구의 means whose. 누구의 책이에요? asks whose book it is. In ordinary speech 누구 책이에요? is also common, but the answer key uses the explicit particle form when requested. 의 is written ui in Revised Romanization, although its spoken realisation varies by position and function; as a possessive particle it is often pronounced like 에.

우리 means our, but Korean also uses it conventionally for some close relationships and shared affiliations where English might say my. 우리 집 can refer to one's home. Do not mechanically infer that every 우리 phrase asserts a legal group ownership claim. Today we use transparent object ownership and introduce that cultural usage as a listening note.

### Working vocabulary

| Native form | Reading | Meaning and use |
|---|---|---|
| 의 | ui | possessive particle |
| 저의 | jeoui | my, uncontracted humble form |
| 제 | je | my, contracted humble form |
| 우리 | uri | our; shared affiliation in context |
| 누구의 | nuguui | whose |
| 제 책 | je chaek | my book |
| 민수 씨의 | Minsu ssiui | Minsu's |

### Worked examples

- `제 책이에요.` - `Je chaegieyo.`: It is my book; 제 identifies the owner.
- `민수 씨의 커피예요.` - `Minsu ssiui keopiyeyo.`: It is Minsu's coffee; the owner precedes the possession.
- `누구의 책이에요?` - `Nuguui chaegieyo?`: Whose book is it? The explicit possessive particle marks the relation.

### Common trap

Do not treat 제 as a gendered form of my. It reflects humble first-person possession. Also, written 의 remains part of standard spelling even when its pronunciation in a possessive phrase resembles 에.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: It is my coffee.
2. Say: My book is here. Use 제 책이.
3. Ask: Whose coffee is it? Use 누구의.
4. Say: It is Minsu's book.

***Answers:***

1. `제 커피예요.` - `Je keopiyeyo.`
2. `제 책이 여기에 있어요.` - `Je chaegi yeogie isseoyo.`
3. `누구의 커피예요?` - `Nuguui keopiyeyo?`
4. `민수 씨의 책이에요.` - `Minsu ssiui chaegieyo.`

### Why these answers work

제 and 민수 씨의 identify owners before the nouns. 책이 is the subject of presence in the second answer, while 여기에 marks location. The other answers use identity endings determined by the final sound of 커피 or 책.

### Retrieve and recombine

Identify an object as yours, then locate it with 있어요. Change the owner to a named classmate and explain which part changes for ownership and which particle still marks the object's sentence role.


## Lesson 12: Descriptive verbs and how things are

**Outcome:** describe a place or object using Korean descriptive predicates.

### Think first

English says “the house is big.” If Korean 크다 itself means “to be big,” would adding 이에요 after its polite form create an unnecessary extra predicate?

### Descriptions conjugate

Korean words often called adjectives behave as descriptive verbs in predicates. 크다 becomes 커요, “is big”; 작다 becomes 작아요, “is small”; 좋다 becomes 좋아요, “is good.” These words take their own polite endings. 집이 커요 means “the house is big,” with no added identity copula.

커요 illustrates a vowel change involving a stem ending in ㅡ. We learn it as a common anchor rather than trying to cover every ㅡ pattern at once. 좋아요 is pronounced joayo because the ㅎ is not pronounced in this vowel environment. 맛있어요 means delicious and has the common reading masisseoyo.

아주 and 정말 can intensify a description: 아주 커요, very big; 정말 좋아요, really good. They do not agree with a noun's gender or number. Korean does not have the masculine/feminine adjective endings found in some other languages.

Predicate descriptions differ from adjectives placed before nouns. “A big house” will require a noun-modifying form, introduced later. Today, produce complete statements: the house is big, the coffee is good, the food is delicious. This limits the pattern precisely and avoids placing a dictionary form before a noun as if Korean copied English adjective order without morphological change.

|---|---|---|
| 크다 | keuda | to be big |
| 커요 | keoyo | is big, polite |
| 작아요 | jagayo | is small, polite |
| 좋아요 | joayo | is good, polite |
| 맛있어요 | masisseoyo | is delicious, polite |
| 아주 | aju | very |
| 정말 | jeongmal | really |
| 음식 | eumsik | food |

### Worked examples

- `집이 커요.` - `Jibi keoyo.`: The house is big; the descriptive verb is the predicate.
- `커피가 좋아요.` - `Keopiga joayo.`: The coffee is good; 가 follows a vowel-final noun.

### Common trap

Do not attach 이에요 to 커요 or 좋아요. They already form polite predicates. Also, dictionary 크다 is not simply placed before 집 to mean a big house; noun modification has its own form.

## Lesson 13: Likes and wants

**Outcome:** distinguish liking an object from wanting to perform an action.

### Think first

좋아요 describes something as good. If you want to state that you like coffee, why might a different verb take coffee as an object?

### Liking takes an object

좋아해요 is the polite form of 좋아하다, to like. 저는 커피를 좋아해요 means “I like coffee.” Compare 커피가 좋아요, which presents coffee as good or preferable in context. Both can support an English liking translation in some situations, but their grammar differs: 좋아하다 takes an object, while 좋다 is a descriptive predicate with a subject.

To express wanting to act, attach 고 싶어요 to an action stem: 가고 싶어요, “I want to go”; 읽고 싶어요, “I want to read.” The stem loses dictionary 다 before 고. 집에 가고 싶어요 means “I want to go home,” with the destination attached to the desired action.

In ordinary statements, 고 싶어요 most directly expresses the speaker's own desire. A question can ask the listener's desire: 커피를 마시고 싶어요? Third-person desire is often expressed with 싶어해요 or another context-sensitive construction, so do not automatically use the speaker pattern to claim access to another person's internal wishes.

The action remains before 싶어요, and the object remains before the action phrase. This lets you reuse existing noun particles while changing the predicate from performing an activity to wanting it. Desire is not the same as an intention, ability or polite request, even when English wording can overlap.

### Working vocabulary

| Native form | Reading | Meaning and use |
|---|---|---|
| 좋아하다 | joahada | to like |
| 좋아해요 | joahaeyo | like, polite |
| 가고 싶어요 | gago sipeoyo | want to go |
| 읽고 싶어요 | ilkgo sipeoyo | want to read |
| 마시고 싶어요 | masigo sipeoyo | want to drink |
| 한국어 | hangugeo | Korean language |
| 배우다 | baeuda | to learn |

### Worked examples

- `저는 차를 좋아해요.` - `Jeoneun chareul joahaeyo.`: I like tea; the liked item is an object.
- `집에 가고 싶어요.` - `Jibe gago sipeoyo.`: I want to go home; the destination belongs to go.
- `한국어를 배우고 싶어요.` - `Hangugeoreul baeugo sipeoyo.`: I want to learn Korean; 배우다 loses 다 before 고.

### Common trap

Do not use the same particle automatically with 좋아요 and 좋아해요. Also, a third person's internal desire needs more care than simply replacing 저는 with a name before 고 싶어요.

### Practice

## Lesson 14: Past events with 었어요 and 았어요

**Outcome:** recount a simple event from yesterday in polite speech.

### Think first

먹어요 is eat. What extra material appears in 먹었어요, and does the ending change if the speaker is a woman rather than a man?

### Past marking before politeness

The common polite past uses 았어요 or 었어요 according to a pattern related to the present 아요/어요 choice. 먹다 becomes 먹었어요, read 읽다 becomes 읽었어요, and 가다 becomes 갔어요 through contraction. 하다 becomes 했어요, so 공부하다 becomes 공부했어요. The past marker comes before the polite ending.

These forms do not change for the subject's person or gender. 저는 갔어요 and 민수 씨가 갔어요 use the same past verb. A clear context may allow the subject to be omitted, just as in present sentences.

어제 supplies yesterday: 어제 집에서 공부했어요 means “I studied at home yesterday.” The location particle still marks the action setting, and a direct object still takes 을/를. Changing tense does not change every noun's role.

The Korean past can correspond to several English past or perfect translations according to context. This lesson uses straightforward yesterday events rather than claiming that 었어요 always maps to exactly one English tense. To ask whether an event happened, use the same form with question intonation: 책을 읽었어요? To deny it with the simple pattern already learned, place 안 before the past verb: 안 갔어요.

### Working vocabulary

| Native form | Reading | Meaning and use |
|---|---|---|
| 갔어요 | gasseoyo | went, polite |
| 왔어요 | wasseoyo | came, polite |
| 먹었어요 | meogeosseoyo | ate, polite |
| 읽었어요 | ilgeosseoyo | read, past polite |
| 마셨어요 | masyeosseoyo | drank, polite |
| 했어요 | haesseoyo | did, polite |
| 공부했어요 | gongbuhaesseoyo | studied, polite |

### Worked examples

- `어제 집에서 공부했어요.` - `Eoje jibeseo gongbuhaesseoyo.`: I studied at home yesterday; the place remains an action location.
- `책을 읽었어요.` - `Chaegeul ilgeosseoyo.`: I read a book; the past form does not mark gender.
- `학교에 갔어요?` - `Hakgyoe gasseoyo?`: Did you go to school? Intonation turns the past statement into a question.

### Common trap

Do not add an English-style auxiliary did before a Korean past form. Also, 하다 becomes 했어요 in this common pattern, not a mechanically concatenated 하었어요.

### Practice

2. Say: I went home.
3. Ask politely: Did you read this book?
4. Say: I did not go yesterday.

***Answers:***

4. `어제 안 갔어요.` - `Eoje an gasseoyo.`

### Why these answers work

마셨어요 and 갔어요 show common contractions in past forms. 읽었어요 retains the reading stem's spelling while linking in pronunciation. 안 negates the past action, and 어제 sets the time without taking 에.

## Lesson 15: Past identities and earlier presence

**Outcome:** say what you were and where something was or was not.

### Think first

If 학생이에요 is a present identity, should its past be formed in exactly the same way as the action verb 가요? What happens to the copula itself?

### Different predicates keep their own forms

After a consonant-final noun, the common polite past identity ending is 이었어요: 학생이었어요, “was a student.” After a vowel-final noun, it contracts to 였어요: 의사였어요, “was a doctor.” These are past forms of the identity copula, not an added English was placed before the noun.

Presence uses 있었어요, “was there” or “had,” while absence uses 없었어요, “was not there” or “did not have.” 어제 집에 있었어요 means “I was at home yesterday.” 책이 없었어요 means there was no book or I did not have a book in the relevant context. The existing subject and location patterns remain intact.

아니었어요 is the past negative identity predicate: 학생이 아니었어요, “was not a student.” Compare 책이 없었어요 with 책이 아니었어요. The first denies a book's presence; the second denies that something was a book. English not can hide that important distinction.

A past description such as 좋았어요 means “was good.” It follows the descriptive verb's past pattern rather than the noun identity pattern. This lesson therefore reviews three predicate types side by side: classification, existence and description. Identifying the type first makes the correct past form easier to retrieve.

### Working vocabulary

| Native form | Reading | Meaning and use |
|---|---|---|
| 학생이었어요 | haksaengieosseoyo | was a student |
| 의사였어요 | uisayeosseoyo | was a doctor |
| 있었어요 | isseosseoyo | was present; had |
| 없었어요 | eopseosseoyo | was absent; did not have |
| 아니었어요 | anieosseoyo | was not, identity |
| 좋았어요 | joasseoyo | was good |
| 작았어요 | jagasseoyo | was small |

### Worked examples

- `저는 학생이었어요.` - `Jeoneun haksaengieosseoyo.`: I was a student; the noun takes the past copula.
- `어제 학교에 있었어요.` - `Eoje hakgyoe isseosseoyo.`: I was at school yesterday; presence has its own past form.
- `커피가 좋았어요.` - `Keopiga joasseoyo.`: The coffee was good; the descriptive verb carries past marking.

### Common trap

Do not use 없었어요 to deny an identity. Absence and classification are different meanings. Also, 의사였어요 uses the contracted vowel-final past copula rather than the consonant-final 이었어요 pattern.

2. Say: I was at home yesterday.
3. Say: There was no water.
4. Say: I was not a student.

1. `저는 의사였어요.` - `Jeoneun uisayeosseoyo.`
2. `어제 집에 있었어요.` - `Eoje jibe isseosseoyo.`

### Why these answers work

의사 takes 였어요 after a vowel. 집에 marks location before 있었어요. 물이 is the subject of absence. 학생이 combines with 아니었어요 to deny an earlier identity, preserving the negative identity construction.

### Retrieve and recombine

Compare a room's contents yesterday and today, then contrast your former and current role in an imaginary biography. Explain why those two tasks need existence and identity predicates respectively.


## Lesson 16: Plans and likely futures

**Outcome:** express a simple intention or expected future event with -(으)ㄹ 거예요.

### Think first

If 가요 can mean go in context, what does 갈 거예요 add when you explicitly discuss tomorrow's plan?

### A future-oriented construction

Attach ㄹ 거예요 after a vowel-final stem and 을 거예요 after most consonant-final stems: 가다 becomes 갈 거예요, 먹다 becomes 먹을 거예요. 읽다 becomes 읽을 거예요. The written space before 거예요 matters because this is a multi-part construction, not a single unbroken word.

내일 공부할 거예요 means “I will study tomorrow” or “I am going to study tomorrow.” 하다 gives 할 in this pattern. With the speaker as subject, the construction often expresses an intention; with other subjects or contexts, it can express a prediction. Do not assume every future sentence is a binding promise.

A question asks about the listener's plan: 내일 학교에 갈 거예요? The destination still takes 에. For a current wish, use 가고 싶어요 from the earlier desire lesson; for a future plan, 갈 거예요 is a useful choice. Wanting and planning are related but not identical.

This construction is only one way Korean expresses future reference. Present forms, intention endings and other modal patterns also occur. We learn a broadly useful explicit form first, then keep the exercises within familiar activities so the new challenge is the stem ending and spacing rather than an entirely new vocabulary set.

## Lesson 17: Counting things with native numbers

**Outcome:** count a few everyday objects with the general counter 개.

### Think first

English can say three books directly. Why might Korean put a counting word after the number, and why does 하나 become 한 before it?

### Noun, number and counter

Korean uses counters with many counted nouns. A common general object counter is 개. 책 한 개 means one book in a simple general-object count, though 권 is the more specific book counter introduced in the table. 커피 한 잔 means one cup of coffee, using 잔 for cups or glasses.

Native Korean numbers include 하나, 둘, 셋 and 넷. Before a counter, their common forms are 한, 두, 세 and 네. Thus 한 개, 두 개, 세 개, 네 개. 다섯 stays 다섯 before a counter. The shortened forms are not a careless pronunciation mistake; they belong to the counting construction.

몇 asks how many. 몇 개예요? asks “How many items are there?” or “How many is it?” in context. The counter is a noun-like element, so 개 takes 예요 after its vowel. A quantity can also combine with 주세요, please give, which will be developed in the request lesson.

A plural marker 들 is not normally needed simply because a number already indicates several items. Korean number systems and counters are substantial topics. This lesson limits active production to a few native numbers and familiar counters rather than asking you to guess every noun's counting category.

| 하나 | hana | one, independent native form |
| 둘 | dul | two, independent native form |
| 한 개 | han gae | one item |
| 두 개 | du gae | two items |
| 세 개 | se gae | three items |
| 네 개 | ne gae | four items |
| 권 | gwon | counter for books |
| 잔 | jan | counter for cups or glasses |

### Worked examples

- `책 두 권이 있어요.` - `Chaek du gwoni isseoyo.`: There are two books; 권 counts books and takes subject 이.
- `커피 한 잔이에요.` - `Keopi han janieyo.`: It is one cup of coffee; 잔 takes 이에요.
- `몇 개예요?` - `Myeot gaeyeyo?`: How many items is it? The quantity is being asked.

### Common trap

Do not use 하나 directly before 개 in the basic counter phrase. Use 한 개. Also, the general 개 does not make every more specific counter unnecessary; books conventionally use 권 and drinks often use 잔.

## Lesson 18: Clock time and Sino-Korean numbers

**Outcome:** tell simple clock times and distinguish the two number systems in that task.

### Think first

If 한 시 means one o'clock but 십 분 means ten minutes, why might a single clock expression use two different number systems?

### Hours and minutes

Clock hours commonly use native Korean numbers with 시: 한 시, 두 시, 세 시, 네 시. Minutes use Sino-Korean numbers with 분: 일 분, two minutes 이 분, ten minutes 십 분. 두 시 십 분 means 2:10. The two systems are chosen by the counting task, not by a general rule that one is informal and the other formal.

Sino-Korean numbers begin 일, 이, 삼, 사, 오, 육, 칠, 팔, 구, 십. This lesson actively uses a small subset for round minutes. 삼십 means thirty, literally three tens. 반 means half, so 두 시 반 means half past two.

몇 시예요? asks “What time is it?” To say when an action occurs, attach 에 to the time phrase: 세 시에 가요, “I go at three.” If the full time includes minutes, 에 follows the final part: 두 시 십 분에 가요.

오전 and 오후 distinguish morning or a.m. from afternoon/evening or p.m. They are useful where context is insufficient. Numbers also serve dates, money and phone numbers, but those domains have their own conventions. Mastering one mixed clock pattern does not mean every Korean number expression can be built by copying it.

|---|---|---|
| 일 | il | one, Sino-Korean |
| 이 | i | two, Sino-Korean |
| 삼 | sam | three, Sino-Korean |
| 십 | sip | ten |
| 삼십 | samsip | thirty |
| 시 | si | hour, clock counter |
| 분 | bun | minute |
| 반 | ban | half |
| 오후 | ohu | p.m.; afternoon |

### Worked examples

- `두 시 십 분이에요.` - `Du si sip bunieyo.`: It is 2:10; native hours combine with Sino-Korean minutes.

- `오후 네 시 반이에요.` - `Ohu ne si banieyo.`: It is 4:30 p.m.; 반 means half past.

### Common trap

Do not use the same number system automatically for both hours and minutes. Also, 시 in a time question takes 예요 after its vowel, while a final consonant in 분 or 반 takes 이에요.

### Practice

4. `네 시에 가요.` - `Ne sie gayo.`

### Why these answers work

몇 시 asks for clock time. 한, 두 and 네 are native counter forms for hours. 반 has a final consonant and takes 이에요. 에 marks the time of the going action rather than identifying the current clock reading.

## Lesson 19: Polite requests and asking for repetition

**Outcome:** ask for an object or a helpful action using 주세요.

### Think first

물 주세요 requests water. How could the same giving idea be attached to a verb so that someone performs an action for your benefit?

### An object or an action before 주세요

주세요 is a polite request form of 주다, to give. 물 주세요 means “Water, please” or “Please give me water.” 물을 주세요 includes the object particle explicitly; both occur naturally. The course's answer keys specify the requested form where needed rather than treating omitted particles as universally wrong.

For an action request, use the 아/어 connecting form followed by 주세요: 읽어 주세요, “please read”; 도와주세요, “please help.” 돕다 is an irregular verb whose request form is learned here as 도와주세요. The conventional joined spelling is common for this helpful-action expression; other auxiliary combinations may show spacing variation within standard conventions.

다시 means again, and 천천히 means slowly. 다시 말해 주세요 asks someone to say it again. 천천히 말해 주세요 asks them to speak slowly. 말하다 becomes 말해 before 주세요 through the familiar 하다 pattern.

A polite request is not the same as a command in every social setting. 주세요 is useful in service and ordinary assistance contexts, but tone, relationship and the size of the request still matter. Use names or titles appropriately rather than inserting 당신 whenever English has you. The action and context often identify the addressee without any pronoun.

## Lesson 20: Ability with 수 있어요

**Outcome:** say that an action is possible or that you know how to do it in context.

### Think first

갈 거예요 expresses a future plan. If the same ㄹ-shaped verb form is followed by 수 있어요, what different meaning does the whole construction create?

### A possibility construction

The pattern -(으)ㄹ 수 있어요 means can or be able to. 가다 becomes 갈 수 있어요, “can go.” 먹다 becomes 먹을 수 있어요, “can eat.” 읽다 becomes 읽을 수 있어요, “can read.” The space before 수 and between 수 and 있어요 is part of the standard written construction.

Absence of possibility uses 없어요: 갈 수 없어요, “cannot go.” This overlaps with 못 가요 from the earlier negative lesson. The longer construction explicitly presents possibility or ability, while 못 is a compact everyday negative pattern. Context determines whether the limitation is skill, permission-like possibility or circumstances; the construction alone does not identify the cause.

한국어를 읽을 수 있어요 means “I can read Korean.” The language remains an object of reading. 오늘 갈 수 있어요 asks or states practical availability today when used in the relevant context.

Do not confuse 수 있어요 with simple possession of a noun. 수 is a dependent noun within this grammatical construction, not an everyday object you possess. The whole pattern must be learned together. It shares a modifier-like verb form with several other constructions, but that resemblance does not make their meanings interchangeable.

## Lesson 21: Necessity and permission

**Outcome:** say that you need to act and ask whether an action is allowed.

### Think first

If 가야 해요 means “I have to go,” how does its meaning differ from 갈 수 있어요, “I can go”? Which construction should you choose for a rule rather than a capability?

### Required and allowed actions

Attach 아/어야 해요 to an action stem to express necessity: 가야 해요, have to go; 먹어야 해요, have to eat; 공부해야 해요, have to study. The connecting vowel follows familiar patterns, and 하다 produces 해야. The construction can express practical need or obligation, with the source supplied by context.

Permission uses 아/어도 돼요: 가도 돼요, may go or it is okay to go; 먹어도 돼요, may eat. A question such as 여기에서 먹어도 돼요? asks whether eating here is allowed. The place is an action location, so 에서 remains appropriate.

A common prohibition uses -(으)면 안 돼요, as in 가면 안 돼요, “you must not go.” It literally frames a condition that is not acceptable. We learn the complete familiar form without expanding the full conditional paradigm today.

Ability, permission and obligation can overlap in English can and must, but they ask different questions. Someone may be able to read a book, allowed to read it here, and required to return it later. Use the grammatical construction that states the relationship you actually intend.

## Lesson 22: Politeness to the listener and honorific subjects

**Outcome:** distinguish a polite speech ending from respect shown toward the person being described.

### Think first

먹어요 is polite to the listener. If you describe a respected teacher eating, is the final 요 the only way Korean can show respect?

### Two relationships, two choices

Speech level concerns the relationship to the listener. The 해요 style used throughout is polite in many everyday settings. The formal polite style uses endings such as 합니다 and 먹습니다, with questions such as 합니까? and 먹습니까? These are common in announcements, formal presentations and other settings requiring that register.

Subject honorification concerns the person whose action or state is described. The element -(으)시- appears in many verbs: 가세요 can mean “goes” with an honored subject or a polite directive in context. 선생님이 가세요 can describe the teacher going. Its function depends on the sentence, not merely on its English translation.

Some frequent honorific predicates use special vocabulary. 계세요 is an honorific presence form corresponding to 있어요 for a respected person. 선생님이 학교에 계세요 means “the teacher is at school.” 드세요 is a respectful eating or drinking form used in suitable contexts, including invitations.

Do not honorify yourself by attaching subject-honorific forms to every first-person action. Humble 저 and polite endings already support respectful self-reference. This lesson recognises the distinction rather than promising that one suffix resolves every social relationship. Natural Korean choices depend on age, role, familiarity and the people being discussed.

| 먹습니다 | meokseumnida | eat, formal polite |

| 계세요 | gyeseyo | is present, honorific subject |
| 가세요 | gaseyo | go, honorific or request in context |

## Lesson 23: Describing a noun and comparing choices

**Outcome:** build simple noun-modifying descriptions and compare two familiar objects.

### Think first

집이 커요 means the house is big. What form is needed when “big” belongs before house inside the phrase “a big house”?

### Before a noun

Many descriptive verbs use -(으)ㄴ before a noun: 작다 becomes 작은, small; 좋다 becomes 좋은, good. 크다 becomes 큰, big, with its stem pattern. 작은 집 is a small house, and 좋은 책 is a good book. The polite ending 요 is not used inside this noun-modifying position.

This pattern differs from action-verb noun modification, which has its own tense-related forms. Do not apply 은 blindly to every action stem. Today's scope is the familiar descriptions big, small and good, so you can build useful noun phrases without assuming a complete relative-clause system.

더 means more in a comparison: 더 커요, is bigger; 더 좋아요, is better. 보다 follows the comparison standard: 이 책이 그 책보다 더 좋아요, “this book is better than that book.” The standard is marked after the noun, consistent with Korean's particle system.

제일 can mark a superlative in a suitable set: 제일 작은 집, the smallest house. The comparison group should be clear from context. Korean does not require an English-style definite article before the superlative. Keep comparisons meaningful by stating or implying the alternatives rather than describing something as best without a set.

### Working vocabulary

| Native form | Reading | Meaning and use |
|---|---|---|
| 큰 | keun | big, before a noun |
| 작은 | jageun | small, before a noun |
| 좋은 | joeun | good, before a noun |
| 더 | deo | more |
| 보다 | boda | than, after comparison standard |
| 제일 | jeil | most, superlative marker |
| 이 책 | i chaek | this book |
| 그 책 | geu chaek | that book |

### Worked examples

- `작은 집이에요.` - `Jageun jibieyo.`: It is a small house; 작은 modifies the noun before the identity ending.
- `이 책이 더 좋아요.` - `I chaegi deo joayo.`: This book is better; the other option is understood.
- `이 집이 그 집보다 더 커요.` - `I jibi geu jipboda deo keoyo.`: This house is bigger than that house; 보다 follows the standard.

### Common trap

Do not put 작아요 directly before 집 to form a small house. Use 작은 집. Also, 보다 follows the comparison standard; it is not placed before the noun like English than.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: It is a good book.
2. Say: This house is smaller.
3. Say: It is the biggest house.
4. Say: This book is better than that book.

***Answers:***

1. `좋은 책이에요.` - `Joeun chaegieyo.`
2. `이 집이 더 작아요.` - `I jibi deo jagayo.`
3. `제일 큰 집이에요.` - `Jeil keun jibieyo.`
4. `이 책이 그 책보다 더 좋아요.` - `I chaegi geu chaekboda deo joayo.`

### Why these answers work

좋은 and 큰 modify nouns, while 작아요 and 좋아요 remain final predicates. 제일 selects the extreme within a contextually relevant set. 그 책보다 marks the comparison standard before the final descriptive predicate.

### Retrieve and recombine

Compare two imaginary rooms, then identify one with a noun phrase such as a small room. Explain the different form of the descriptive verb when it modifies a noun instead of ending the sentence.


## Lesson 24: Joining actions and giving a reason

**Outcome:** connect two familiar clauses while preserving their order and meaning.

### Think first

If you know “I read” and “I study,” how can Korean connect them without placing two separate polite endings in the middle of one clause sequence?

### Connective endings

The ending 고 joins predicates in a simple and relationship: 책을 읽고 공부해요, “I read a book and study.” Attach 고 to the stem rather than to the full polite form. The final predicate carries the polite ending for the whole connected sentence. Depending on context, 고 can list actions or connect a sequence; it does not by itself encode every possible chronological relation.

하지만 is a separate connector meaning but. It can begin a new sentence after a complete statement. For a compact contrast within a sentence, 지만 attaches to a stem: 가고 싶지만 못 가요, “I want to go, but I cannot.” The desire expression supplies the stem 싶- before 지만.

The connective 아/어서 often gives a reason or a connected sequence. 바빠서 못 가요 means “I cannot go because I am busy.” 바쁘다, to be busy, has the connecting form 바빠서. This is learned as a useful anchor for the ㅡ-stem pattern. The cause comes before the result in this example.

The 아/어서 construction has usage restrictions, especially with certain commands and proposals, so do not treat it as a universal replacement for every English because. Today's declarative reason sentence is a safe, useful starting point. Other causal endings can be learned when those additional contexts arise.

### Working vocabulary

| Native form | Reading | Meaning and use |
|---|---|---|
| 고 | go | and, attached to a predicate stem |
| 하지만 | hajiman | but, separate connector |
| 지만 | jiman | but, connective ending |
| 바쁘다 | bappeuda | to be busy |
| 바빠요 | bappayo | is busy, polite |
| 바빠서 | bappaseo | because busy; being busy |
| 그래서 | geuraeseo | so; therefore |

### Worked examples

- `책을 읽고 공부해요.` - `Chaegeul ilkgo gongbuhaeyo.`: I read a book and study; the final verb carries politeness.
- `가고 싶지만 못 가요.` - `Gago sipjiman mot gayo.`: I want to go, but I cannot; the two meanings contrast.
- `바빠서 못 가요.` - `Bappaseo mot gayo.`: I cannot go because I am busy; the cause comes first.

### Common trap

Do not attach 고 to 읽어요 as if the polite ending must remain inside every connected clause. Also, the reason pattern taught here is declarative; wider command and proposal contexts require further choices.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Say: I drink water and study.
2. Say: I want to read, but I cannot go. Use 지만.
3. Say: I cannot go because I am busy.
4. Say: I eat a meal and go home.

***Answers:***

1. `물을 마시고 공부해요.` - `Mureul masigo gongbuhaeyo.`
2. `읽고 싶지만 못 가요.` - `Ilkgo sipjiman mot gayo.`
3. `바빠서 못 가요.` - `Bappaseo mot gayo.`
4. `밥을 먹고 집에 가요.` - `Babeul meokgo jibe gayo.`

### Why these answers work

마시고 and 먹고 connect action stems to a final polite predicate. 싶지만 contrasts desire with a limitation. 바빠서 gives the reason before 못 가요. The objects and destination keep their ordinary particles inside the connected sentences.

### Retrieve and recombine

Explain a missed visit with a reason, then describe two actions you will do instead using 고. Keep the cause-result relationship separate from a simple list of activities.


## Lesson 25: Café conversation and cumulative review

**Outcome:** use identity, quantity, requests, time and plans in a short new interaction.

### Think first

At a café, you need to order two drinks, ask permission to sit, and explain that you will study afterwards. Which of these are requests, which are permission questions, and which are plans?

### Choose the construction by purpose

커피 두 잔 주세요 requests two cups of coffee. The native number 두 modifies the counter 잔, and 주세요 makes the service request. A counted noun does not need an added plural marker just because there are two items.

여기에 앉아도 돼요? asks “May I sit here?” 앉다 means to sit, with the connecting form 앉아. This uses the permission construction rather than simply asking whether sitting is physically possible. The polite question leaves room for the listener to explain whether the seat is available.

얼마예요? means “How much is it?” in a price context. It is a practical new question built with the familiar identity ending. The actual amount is not supplied here because the lesson teaches a language exchange, not current prices. If the answer is unclear, ask 다시 말해 주세요 rather than pretending to understand.

A plan such as 여기에서 공부할 거예요 uses an action location plus the future-oriented construction. Contrast 여기에 있어요, presence, with 여기에서 공부해요, an action at the place. The café setting brings together particles that can look similar but answer different questions.

Use the review as a test of grammatical choices, not a claim of fluency. Broader honorifics, listening variation, irregular verbs and natural discourse still need continued practice and fluent-speaker feedback.

### Working vocabulary

| Native form | Reading | Meaning and use |
|---|---|---|
| 커피 두 잔 | keopi du jan | two cups of coffee |
| 얼마예요 | eolmayeyo | how much is it |
| 앉다 | antda | to sit |
| 앉아도 돼요 | anjado dwaeyo | may sit; okay to sit |
| 자리 | jari | seat; place |
| 여기에 | yeogie | here, location or destination |
| 여기에서 | yeogieseo | here, action location |

### Worked examples

- `커피 두 잔 주세요.` - `Keopi du jan juseyo.`: Two coffees, please; quantity and request combine.
- `여기에 앉아도 돼요?` - `Yeogie anjado dwaeyo?`: May I sit here? This asks permission.
- `여기에서 공부할 거예요.` - `Yeogieseo gongbuhal geoyeyo.`: I will study here; the place hosts an action.

### Common trap

Do not confuse an ability statement, permission question and future plan merely because all concern the same action. Also, a useful service fragment can be complete in context without an explicit subject pronoun.

### Practice

Build the requested sentence before opening the answer key.

1. Order: One cup of tea, please.
2. Ask: How much is it?
3. Say: I studied at home yesterday.
4. Say: I will read this book tomorrow.

***Answers:***

1. `차 한 잔 주세요.` - `Cha han jan juseyo.`
2. `얼마예요?` - `Eolmayeyo?`
3. `어제 집에서 공부했어요.` - `Eoje jibeseo gongbuhaesseoyo.`
4. `내일 이 책을 읽을 거예요.` - `Naeil i chaegeul ilgeul geoyeyo.`

### Why these answers work

한 is the shortened native number before 잔. 얼마예요 asks price through a familiar polite identity frame. 공부했어요 places the study event in the past, while 읽을 거예요 presents a future reading plan with its object marked 을.

### Retrieve and recombine

Create a five-turn conversation in a new library or café setting: identify yourself, ask a location, request an item, clarify a reply and state a plan. Explain one particle choice and one politeness choice from your own exchange.
